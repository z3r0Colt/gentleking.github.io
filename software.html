Sojourner, Bible Study Companion

Scripture beside the commentaries, confessions, and reference works of the Reformed church. It runs on your own Windows machine, it works with the network off, and it is free.

What it is

Scripture, and the Church Reading It With You

Sojourner is a Windows desktop application for reading the Bible beside the commentaries, confessions, and reference works the Reformed church has been handing down for four hundred years. The Hebrew and Greek lie underneath, and the tools for preparing a sermon sit on top. All of it is on your own machine. None of it asks you to sign in.

Most study software wants something from you first. An account. A subscription. A connection. A tier that hides the good commentaries until you pay. Sojourner wants none of it. You install it, and the Bibles, the commentaries, and the reference works are already there.

The word sojourner is what the saints called themselves while they were still walking. You are passing through, and you need the road marked. That is the whole idea behind it.

It is a servant, not a substitute. A program cannot preach to you, baptize you, or feed you at the Lord’s Table. Use it through the week, and be under the Word in your own church on the Lord’s Day.

Two Honest Warnings

Neither of these is a problem with the program, but you should know about both before you double-click anything.

Windows will warn you. The installer is not code-signed, because a certificate costs money this work does not have. So SmartScreen shows a blue box the first time you run it. Click More info, then Run anyway. The release page lists the installer’s SHA-256 checksum, a kind of fingerprint for the file, so anyone who knows how can confirm the file is the one that was published. If the warning makes you uneasy, that instinct is a good one, and you are welcome to pass on it.

It is a large download. About 608 MB, because the texts and the reading voice are all inside it rather than fetched from a server later. That is the trade. One long download once, and then the program never needs the network again.

Version 0.3.0 means what it says. This is early, real, and in use, but it is not finished. Things will change and some of them will break. If something goes wrong, tell me and I will fix it.

What is inside

The Library It Ships With

All of this installs with the program. Nothing is held back, nothing unlocks later, and none of it needs a connection to open.

Fifteen translations

Public domain or free to share

The nine older ones are the King James of 1769, the American Standard of 1901, Young’s Literal, Darby, Webster’s, the Geneva of 1599, Douay-Rheims, Tyndale, and Wycliffe. The six modern ones are free because their makers let them be shared. They are the World English Bible, the Berean Standard Bible, the Literal Standard Version, unfoldingWord’s Literal and Simplified Texts, and the Open English Bible, which so far has the New Testament and part of the Old.

The original texts

Hebrew, Greek, and Latin

The Hebrew Old Testament from the Leningrad Codex, the oldest complete copy of the Hebrew Bible, read right to left. Five editions of the Greek New Testament. The Textus Receptus is the Greek text behind the King James. The Byzantine text follows the great number of later copies, and the Westcott and Hort, Tregelles, and SBL editions weigh the oldest copies most heavily. Also the Septuagint, the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament, and the Latin Vulgate in its Clementine edition. All of them follow English chapter and verse numbering, so they line up with the English beside them.

The lexicons

For readers with no Greek or Hebrew, and for those with some

A lexicon is a dictionary of the Hebrew or Greek words. Strong’s and Thayer’s are the ones most readers start with. The full Brown, Driver, and Briggs is here for Hebrew, with Abbott-Smith and Liddell, Scott, and Jones for Greek, and STEPBible’s brief lexicons for both. Double-click any word in the text for its entry. There is more on this below.

Commentaries

Henry, Calvin, Jamieson, Barnes, Spurgeon

Matthew Henry’s Concise, the forty-five volumes of Calvin, Jamieson, Fausset and Brown, Barnes’ Notes on the New Testament, and Spurgeon’s Treasury of David on all one hundred and fifty Psalms.

The Westminster Standards

With proofs and a topic index

The Confession, the Larger Catechism, and the Shorter Catechism, each with its Scripture proofs and a doctrinal index. Hodge and Shaw on the Confession and Vincent on the Shorter Catechism sit right beside them.

The wider confessions

Creeds and Continental standards

The Apostles’, Nicene, Athanasian, and Chalcedonian creeds, the Belgic Confession, the Heidelberg Catechism, and the Canons of Dort.

The Psalter

1650, with tunes you can play

The 1650 Scottish Metrical Psalter in full, with thirty-nine tunes that play in the program, so you can hear the line before you sing it.

Read aloud

A voice that runs on your machine

A neural voice ships with the program and reads any chapter, commentary, or book aloud with no connection. Words light up as it reads, there is a sleep timer, and you can correct how it says a name.

The book library

Four Shelves, and Your Own

The books come as four shelves, each a separate download on the release page, so you take only the ones you want and leave the rest. To install one, open Settings, choose Book library, and pick Install from file. Each shelf can be removed there too.

The Sojourner Library
Two hundred and seventy Puritan and Reformed works.
The Church Fathers
The writers of the early church. The Ante-Nicene Fathers, from before the Council of Nicaea, and both series of the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, as Roberts, Donaldson, Schaff, and Wace edited them, with Lightfoot’s Apostolic Fathers. Thirty-eight volumes in all.
Ancient literature
The writers around the Bible. Josephus and Philo, 1 Enoch, and three Roman writers, Tacitus on the reign of Tiberius, the letters of Pliny the Younger, and Suetonius’s Lives of the Twelve Caesars.
The nineteenth century
Schaff’s History of the Christian Church and his Creeds of Christendom, and Edersheim’s Life and Times of Jesus the Messiah, with his books on the Temple and on Jewish social life.

Inside the program, Resources sorts every book by shelf and by subject. Books of sermons, theology, commentary, and the Christian life each sit together, and the Fathers are grouped by series.

Cited in your library

Beside each verse, a small count shows how many books on your shelves cite it. Click it for the list, and click a citation to open the book at that page. It is a quiet way to find out who else has stood over the verse you are reading.

Your own books and recordings

Bring in your own EPUB, PDF, and MOBI books and your own audio and video. All of it goes into the search box and is scanned for the verses it cites. You can group it by author, by date, or by topic. Recordings play at the speed you choose, and a passage can link straight to the moment in a recording where it is discussed.

How it works

Panes That Follow Each Other

This is the part worth understanding, because it is what the picture at the top of the page is showing. You can open up to eight panes and split the window however you like. Drag a pane to dock it, stack tabs inside it, pick a ready-made arrangement, or save the whole layout as a named workspace that survives a reinstall.

Then you link them. Click a verse in one pane and every linked pane turns to it. The commentary, the cross references, the confession proofs, the encyclopedia article, and the atlas map all land on the same verse at once. So do the people and places the verse names, its place on the timeline, and the books on your shelves that cite it. You stop hunting and start reading.

One click, and the whole shelf opens to the same page.

You can also set two translations side by side, or hold one verse up against every translation at once. Every Scripture reference anywhere in the program, in a commentary or a confession or your own notes, shows the verse on hover.

Greek and Hebrew

The Original Words, for People Who Have None

Every phrase of the King James text is tagged with its Strong’s number, through both testaments. Turn the interlinear on and the original sits under the English, word by word, Hebrew in the Old Testament and Greek in the New, each one parsed. A switch puts it back into the original word order when you want to see the sentence as it was built.

Double-click any word, anywhere in the text, and its entry opens in the lexicon you choose. You can search the Greek and Hebrew without typing accents or vowel points.

Word study

Pick any Hebrew or Greek word and Sojourner lays out the facts about it. How often it occurs, how the King James renders it, the forms it takes, the words related to it, and every place it stands.

Search by grammar

Every Hebrew and Greek word is parsed, which means its grammar is labeled, such as its tense or whether it is a command. Search by grammar finds every word of one kind, such as every command in Ephesians. You choose from lists rather than typing code letters, so you do not need to know how the labels are written.

Where the Greek editions differ

Set the five Greek editions side by side on a verse and see where they read differently. At 1 John 5:7, the words about the three that bear record in heaven, known as the Comma Johanneum, stand only in the Textus Receptus.

None of this asks you to have studied the languages. That is the point of it. A man with a concordance and some patience has been able to do real work in the text for two hundred years, and this only makes the same work quicker.

Who, where, and when

The People and the Ground They Walked

A page of text cannot easily show you who a man was, where something happened, or how it lines up with everything else happening at the time. The Factbook, the timeline, the atlas, and the harmonies are for that.

The Factbook

A page for each of four thousand two hundred and seventy five people, places, and things in the Bible. The Bible has several men named Zechariah, and the Factbook tells them apart by the verses that name them. Each one gets his family, every verse, and what the encyclopedia and the dictionary say about him. Double-click a name in the text and it opens the person that verse means.

The timeline

Four hundred and sixty four events, from the Creation to Paul in Rome, on a line you can zoom. The eras are marked, the kings of Judah and Israel run side by side, and a strip beside the text shows where the chapter you are reading falls. The dates are approximate, taken from a traditional chronology.

The atlas

One thousand three hundred and forty two places, each with its coordinates, its modern identification where there is one, and every verse that names it. Fourteen journeys are traced across the map, from Abraham leaving Ur to Paul reaching Rome, and each stop carries its own reference and a line saying what happened there. The old regions are shaded as a soft wash inside a dashed line, since no one ever surveyed their borders and a crisp line would claim more than anyone knows.

The harmonies

Two harmonies of the Gospels, both laid out in four columns. Robert M. Sutherland’s of 2020 runs to a hundred and thirty six events, and A. T. Robertson’s of 1922 to a hundred and eighty five. Each event is titled and paired with where it is told in Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John, with Acts 1 brought in for the ascension. Where an event appears in more than one Gospel, one button sets the accounts side by side.

One Search Box

One box searches Scripture, the commentaries, the lexicons, the Factbook, the confessions, the encyclopedia, your books, and your own notes, prayers, sermons, and illustrations, with the same rules everywhere.

Put an exact phrase in quotation marks. Ask for either of two words with OR, or leave a word out with a minus sign. Group words in parentheses, ask for words near each other, match the start of a word, or, for those who know them, use regular expressions. A plus sign asks for the exact form and its capitals, so +LORD finds the covenant name as the King James prints it, and skips the ordinary word Lord.

You can also narrow a search by typing a filter into the box itself. There are filters for a book, a translation, a Strong’s number, the words of Christ, and the verses you have notes on, and they are saved with the search. Older spellings match, so shew finds show. Results can be counted by book and by translation, or laid out like a printed concordance, one line for each place the word appears.

Reading

Book and chapter navigation, paragraph mode, verse numbers on or off, red letters, five named highlight colors and an underline, bookmarks, a find bar for the open chapter, a focus mode that clears everything else away, and printing.

Seven themes, including sepia, true black, and two built for high contrast. A font made for dyslexic readers, text you can size, and reduced motion for anyone who needs the screen to hold still.

Notes That Stay Yours

Write on a verse, on a chapter, or on a highlight. Templates are there when a blank page is the problem, for observation and interpretation and application, for question and answer, for a sermon outline, for a prayer in response. Type a reference inside any note and that verse is marked in the text from then on.

Highlighting works the same way. Five colors and an underline, each one named for what it marks rather than for its color. Promise in yellow, command in green, doctrine in blue, warning in orange. Then you can pull up every promise you have ever marked, across every book, in one list.

The Mine pane gathers everything you have written on the chapter that is open. Anything you delete waits thirty days in a trash before it is gone.

For preachers

Preparing and Preaching

The sermon editor treats your points as the outline, so the structure comes out of the manuscript instead of being kept beside it. Passage blocks render live in the translation the sermon is set to.

Every verse, commentary entry, confession section, lexicon entry, word study, encyclopedia article, and book selection carries a small microphone button. Press it and that text drops into the open sermon with its source line already attached. You never lose track of where a quotation came from.

Illustrations go into a file of their own, each with where it came from and where you have used it. When you reach for one you have already told in this series, it says so before you put it in.

Around that sit a six-stage preparation track, timed rehearsals that measure your speaking rate, and a preaching mode with pulpit-sized type and a clock. It will generate slides and export them to PowerPoint, build fill-in handouts, print, and export to Markdown. Sermons group into a series with a reading plan for the congregation. When it is over there is a place to write down how it went.

The Ordinary Days

The Today page gathers what is actually in front of you. Where your reading left off, today’s portion of your plan, this Sunday’s sermon, the memory cards that are due, and the people you meant to pray for.

Reading plans come with catch-up tools for the weeks you fall behind, and you can build your own. The prayer journal follows the ACTS pattern, keeps a list of people, nudges you toward the ones you have not prayed for in a while, and prints.

Memory covers Scripture and the catechism together, on a spaced repetition schedule, with three ways to practice. One leaves you only the first letter of every word, which is harder than it looks and works better than reading it over again.

The Psalter is there for singing too. Open a psalm, choose one of its tunes, and it plays, with the words of each stanza set under the melody.

What It Does Not Have

It runs on Windows only, and nothing syncs between computers. It does not update itself either.

It cannot include the ESV, the NIV, or the NASB, because they are under copyright. The same is true of BDAG and HALOT, the standard scholarly lexicons for Greek and Hebrew. If you own a modern translation as a Zefania XML file, you can add it yourself.

Convictions

Offline, and Yours

Sojourner uses the network for exactly one thing. You press a button on the About page to check whether a newer version exists. That is all. There is no account, no sync service, and no telemetry of any kind. Nothing you read, search, write, or pray is reported anywhere, because there is nowhere for it to go. Even the book shelves install from files you download yourself, so they can just as well arrive on a USB stick.

Everything you write lives in one file on your machine. Snapshots and automatic backups run in the background. You can export the whole database and import it again, run an integrity check, keep a copy in a OneDrive or Dropbox folder if you want one, and look at your own reading statistics on a heatmap. If the program ever crashes, the log stays on your computer.

This is not a marketing position. It is the only arrangement that makes sense for a book you are supposed to read for the rest of your life. Companies fold and services shut down. A file on your own disk does not.

Where the Texts Came From

Every text in Sojourner is in the public domain or used under an open license. The About page in Settings lists each source and its terms, with every Creative Commons credit the licenses ask for.

The Hebrew and Greek texts, the lexicons, and the Factbook’s people and places come from STEPBible and Tyndale House, and the parsing and lexicon data from OpenScriptures. The Berean Standard Bible, the Literal Standard Version, and unfoldingWord’s two texts are used under their makers’ open licenses. The timeline draws on the Theographic Bible Metadata, and the atlas on OpenBible.info, with coastlines from Natural Earth. The books on the shelves come from the Christian Classics Ethereal Library and Project Gutenberg. The OpenDyslexic font is under the SIL Open Font License.

The 2020 harmony of the Gospels is the work of Robert M. Sutherland, copyright 2020, reproduced here with acknowledgment of his authorship as his terms ask. The 1922 harmony is A. T. Robertson’s and is public domain.

No text or reference data in Sojourner was written by AI. Where a source came with summaries written by AI, they were left out.

None of this is my work. It is the labour of Calvin, Henry, Spurgeon, Barnes, Orr, Easton, Smith, Strong, Thayer, Brown, Driver, Briggs, the Westminster divines, and the many people who typed their books into machines and gave them away. Sojourner only puts them on one shelf.
