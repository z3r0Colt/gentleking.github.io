When Jesus therefore saw his mother, and the disciple standing by, whom he loved, he saith unto his mother, Woman, behold thy son! Then saith he to the disciple, Behold thy mother! And from that hour that disciple took her unto his own home.

John 19:26-27 (KJV)

Dying on the cross in agony, he still saw his mother standing there. He gave her a son to look after her, and a home. He hasn’t stopped seeing the one who is left behind.

A father of the fatherless, and a judge of the widows, is God in his holy habitation.

Psalm 68:5 (KJV)

A judge of the widows is a defender who takes up their cause. God cares for children who have lost a parent. So if you've children at home, he hasn’t overlooked them. Let them see you cry, and let them cry too.

The next verse says that God setteth the solitary in families (68:6). He did that for his mother at the cross, and he does it still through his church. That verse is for the widower as much as the widow.

Paul tells a widow’s own family, and then the church, to honor her and care for her (1 Timothy 5:3-5, 16). If the cards and meals have stopped, you may tell your elders so. That isn’t complaining. It lets the church obey.

Anna lost her husband after seven years of marriage and lived as a widow into great old age. Her years alone weren’t empty. She served God with prayer night and day, and her long road brought her to the child Jesus (Luke 2:36-38).

For many, the mornings are the hardest. For a moment you forget, and then you remember. God put a whole book of weeping in the Bible. In the middle of it, with his city in ruins, Jeremiah wrote this.

It is of the Lord’s mercies that we are not consumed, because his compassions fail not. They are new every morning: great is thy faithfulness.

Lamentations 3:22-23 (KJV)

Jeremiah wrote these words amid a city’s ruin, after describing his distress in the same chapter. Remembering the Lord’s mercies gave him a reason to hope (Lamentations 3:21). The believer may endure chastening and grief, but Christ has borne the condemning wrath due to his people’s sins. You can ask the Father for help with the day before you.

The one who saw to his mother from the cross died there. On the third day he rose, and now he dieth no more (Romans 6:9). He lives to see to his own. Paul says marriage pictures Christ and his church (Ephesians 5:31-32). Christ’s love for his church endures, and if you’re his, he still holds you.

If you don’t know where your husband or wife stood with God, that’s too heavy to carry alone tonight. You aren’t asked to settle where they are. You may leave them with the Judge of all the earth, who will do right (Genesis 18:25), and bring your own soul to Christ. Read When You Fear They Did Not Know Christ, and tell your pastor this week. If they took their own life, that sorrow has its own place here.

You may notice the absence most in the daily habits you shared. Tell a friend which part of the day is hardest. A regular call or visit may be more useful than another offer of help that never becomes definite.

When you're ready to ask about heaven

If your husband or wife belonged to Christ, their soul is with him now, made perfect in holiness (Shorter Catechism Q&A 37).

At the resurrection, believers being raised up in glory, shall be openly acknowledged and acquitted in the day of judgment, and made perfectly blessed in the full enjoying of God to all eternity.

Westminster Shorter Catechism Q&A 38

Paul says that when the Lord comes, the dead in Christ shall rise first. Then his people who are still alive will be caught up together with them, and so shall we ever be with the Lord (1 Thessalonians 4:16-17).

In the resurrection, Jesus said, they neither marry, nor are given in marriage, but are as the angels of God in heaven (Matthew 22:30). Yet Christ’s people are raised as themselves, with bodies made like his glorious body (Philippians 3:21). Marriage is God’s gift for this life (Romans 7:2).

Scripture says less than we might wish about how his people will know one another there. But the one who gave his mother a home from the cross is there now, and he is their joy. If you’re his, you’ll see him on the day he chooses, not the day you choose. Until then, he hasn’t finished with your life here.

Evenings and Holidays

Some couples shared every meal and appointment for decades. After a death, even ordinary tasks can remind you of the person who is gone. You may wish to keep a family tradition, change it, or decline an invitation for a time. Tell family members what would actually help rather than leaving them to guess.

If you have children, they may fear losing you as well. Hear their questions and let them know which adults can help the family. If loneliness grows into hopelessness or you can no longer manage daily life, seek help from your pastor and a doctor.

Bills, Documents, and the House

A surviving spouse may have to deal with accounts, insurance, repairs, and appointments that the other person usually handled. Ask a trustworthy relative or friend to review urgent deadlines with you. Don’t sign away property or make major financial commitments under pressure.

Tell the deacons if you need money, rides, repairs, or help caring for children. The church’s duty toward widows does not expire when the funeral is over (1 Timothy 5:3-5, 16).
