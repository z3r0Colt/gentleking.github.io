Parents are responsible to teach, protect, and correct their children. Begin with clear instructions, patient attention, and nonviolent consequences suited to the child’s age and ability. This page examines the rod passages in Proverbs, the duties of parents, and what to do when a child has been harmed.

If someone hurts, threatens, or frightens you at home, tell a safe adult. You don’t need to decide whether it meets a legal definition before asking for help. Read If a child is being hurt now, and tell a teacher or another adult you trust today. If it feels like more than you can bear, call 988 or text 988 in the United States. In immediate danger, contact your local emergency service. If you’re a parent and you fear you’ve gone too far, go straight to When Correction Becomes Abuse.

Why God Commands Parents to Discipline

God doesn’t leave children to raise themselves. He places them under a father and a mother, and he tells those parents to correct them. That isn’t because God is harsh. It’s because he is a Father himself, and he loves.

My son, despise not the chastening of the Lord; neither be weary of his correction: For whom the Lord loveth he correcteth; even as a father the son in whom he delighteth.

Proverbs 3:11-12 (KJV)

The Lord’s correction is a mark of his delight. A father who never corrects isn’t kind. He has stopped caring where his child is going. Proverbs says it bluntly. He that spareth his rod hateth his son, but he that loveth him chasteneth him betimes (Proverbs 13:24). Betimes means early, while the child is young and the habit is still soft.

Hebrews quotes Proverbs 3 to suffering Christians and reasons from the home up to heaven. We gave our earthly fathers reverence when they corrected us, so we should much rather be in subjection unto the Father of spirits, and live (Hebrews 12:9).

For they verily for a few days chastened us after their own pleasure; but he for our profit, that we might be partakers of his holiness. Now no chastening for the present seemeth to be joyous, but grievous: nevertheless afterward it yieldeth the peaceable fruit of righteousness unto them which are exercised thereby.

Hebrews 12:10-11 (KJV)

Earthly fathers corrected after their own pleasure, as seemed best to them. They didn’t always get it right. God never errs. Parents should exercise authority with that humility. You answer to God for the care and protection of the child entrusted to you.

The Larger Catechism sums up what God requires of everyone set over others, parents first of all.

to love, pray for, and bless their inferiors; to instruct, counsel, and admonish them; countenancing, commending, and rewarding such as do well; and discountenancing, reproving, and chastising such as do ill

Westminster Larger Catechism Q&A 129

This is an excerpt from the Catechism’s longer answer. It names teaching, encouragement, and correction within a duty of love. Parents must consider the whole answer together with its prohibition of undue correction in Q&A 130.

Correction itself is a duty. Parents must not leave children to govern themselves or treat persistent disobedience as a harmless preference. The fifth commandment requires children to honor and obey their parents in things lawful (Ephesians 6:1-3, WLC 127-128). Their parents have a corresponding duty to teach, admonish, restrain wrongdoing, and apply fitting correction (WLC 129). Eli was reproved because his sons made themselves vile, and he restrained them not (1 Samuel 3:13). A child needs boundaries that are actually kept. Neglecting discipline is not kindness.

That positive command still has a limit set by God. WLC 130 condemns those in authority who correct unduly, command what cannot rightly be performed, or leave dependents exposed to danger. Neither neglect nor harshness fulfills parental duty. What follows examines how the rod passages were understood and how the biblical requirement to correct should be carried out without injury or cruelty.

The Rod in Proverbs

Proverbs 13:24, 22:15, 23:13-14, and 29:15 connect correction with a parent’s love and responsibility. They must be read alongside the commands to protect children and not provoke them to anger.

Foolishness is bound in the heart of a child; but the rod of correction shall drive it far from him.

Proverbs 22:15 (KJV)

The rod in its historical setting

The rod passages belong to an ancient setting in which physical punishment was used. Many Reformed interpreters understand a physical rod here. Recognizing that setting does not establish that every child must receive the same treatment, or that a blow is harmless. The duty to correct must be applied with the child’s safety and needs in view.

Proverbs joins the rod with reproof, or spoken correction (Proverbs 29:15). Scripture gives parents responsibility to teach and restrain wrongdoing. It also condemns correcting unduly and exposing children to danger (Larger Catechism Q&A 130). No interpretation gives permission to injure, terrify, or humiliate a child.

A proverb, not a fixed method

Proverbs teaches wisdom rather than a universal schedule or technique for punishment. Its paired sayings about answering a fool show why context and judgment matter (Proverbs 26:4-5). A child’s age, understanding, health, and circumstances matter when deciding how to correct a particular action.

Neither outward obedience nor any form of discipline can regenerate a child. Only the Spirit gives new life. Parents should teach repentance and faith without treating compliance as proof of conversion.

He shall not die

Withhold not correction from the child: for if thou beatest him with the rod, he shall not die. Thou shalt beat him with the rod, and shalt deliver his soul from hell.

Proverbs 23:13-14 (KJV)

This passage must not be turned into a medical assurance that physical punishment cannot injure or kill. Nor is survival the measure of acceptable treatment. The Larger Catechism requires preserving life and forbids striking, wounding, and neglecting necessary care (Q&A 135-136). The proverb teaches the seriousness of rescuing a child from destructive folly. It does not authorize an adult to ignore harm.

A parent’s correction cannot save a soul. Christ alone saves, and his mercy is freely offered to children as well as adults who hear the gospel.

Applying the duty with care

Reformed parents differ over how to apply these passages. The Westminster answers cited here describe duties of instruction and correction alongside protection from undue treatment. They do not supply a technique for striking children.

For practical child-health guidance, the American Academy of Pediatrics recommends nonviolent discipline, including clear limits, redirection, and encouragement of appropriate behavior. Ask your child’s clinician for help when ordinary approaches are not working. A theological discussion must not substitute for assessment of an injury or a child’s developmental needs.

Discipline Is More Than Spanking

And, ye fathers, provoke not your children to wrath: but bring them up in the nurture and admonition of the Lord.

Ephesians 6:4 (KJV)

Nurture is training, and admonition is warning with words. Children need repeated teaching and help practicing what is right. Discipline includes much more than responding after something has gone wrong.

  1. Teaching. Talk of God’s ways when thou sittest in thine house, and when thou walkest by the way (Deuteronomy 6:7). Gather them for family worship and teach them the catechism.
  2. Warning. Tell the child plainly what is wrong and what will follow, before it happens. A rule he never knew is a trap, not a rule.
  3. Consequences. Choose an age-appropriate response connected to what happened, such as putting away a toy that was being used dangerously or helping repair a mess. Never withhold food, necessary sleep, medicine, or other basic care.
  4. Example. Children learn more from how you handle your own sin than from how you handle theirs. When you wrong them, ask their forgiveness.
  5. Praise. The Catechism counts commending such as do well as part of the duty. Catch your children doing right, and tell them.

The Guardrails Scripture Sets

The same catechism that requires parents to chastise also names the sins of those in authority.

correcting them unduly; careless exposing, or leaving them to wrong, temptation, and danger; provoking them to wrath

Westminster Larger Catechism Q&A 130

A parent can sin by never correcting, and a parent can sin by correcting wrongly.

Wherefore, my beloved brethren, let every man be swift to hear, slow to speak, slow to wrath: For the wrath of man worketh not the righteousness of God.

James 1:19-20 (KJV)
  1. Hear what happened. Ask the child to explain. Consider whether the instruction was understood and whether hunger, fatigue, illness, or a disability made it difficult.
  2. Set a clear limit. Explain the conduct you expect in words the child can understand. Help a young child practice it.
  3. Respond without violence or humiliation. Use calm correction and proportionate nonviolent consequences. A child does not lose the right to protection because the parent feels disobeyed.
  4. Pause when you may lose control. Make sure the child is with a safe, responsible adult or in an appropriate safe place. Get help before resuming the conversation. Do not leave a dependent child without care.
  5. Restore ordinary care. After correcting, explain how to make things right and reassure the child of your love. Do not force physical affection, a confession, or an immediate display of happiness.
  6. Get advice when a pattern persists. A pediatrician or qualified child-health professional can help assess development and behavior. Elders can address parental duties, anger, and repentance.

Fathers, provoke not your children to anger, lest they be discouraged (Colossians 3:21). A child corrected too often, too hard, or without warmth doesn’t grow wise. He loses heart. If your children flinch or fear you, stop the frightening behavior and seek qualified help. Take their fear seriously rather than demanding that they hide it.

When a Child Has Been Harmed

Hitting, shaking, choking, threats, and humiliation can harm a child. You do not need to wait for a visible mark before seeking help. The sixth commandment requires protection of life, and parental authority cannot excuse cruelty (Larger Catechism Q&A 135-136).

  1. Protect the child first. Stop the harmful conduct. If danger is immediate, contact emergency services. Make sure a safe, responsible adult is caring for the child.
  2. Seek medical care for injury or worrying symptoms. Describe honestly what happened. Do not delay care to ask a church leader whether it is necessary.
  3. Contact the appropriate protective authority. Reporting rules vary by jurisdiction. The child-safety guidance gives routes to reporting and support. Do not ask the child or family to keep harm secret or withdraw a report.
  4. Seek sustained help. Tell your pastor truthfully and get qualified help with anger or behavior that puts others at risk. An elder’s approval cannot establish that a particular physical punishment is medically safe or lawful.

Christ freely receives sinners who repent and trust him. His pardon does not cancel a child’s need for protection or the responsibilities of civil authorities. If you were harmed by someone who called it biblical discipline, you can seek help without accepting that description of what happened.

What the Law Requires

Know the law where you live. Definitions of abuse, restrictions on physical punishment, and reporting duties differ. Get current information from the appropriate child-protection authority or qualified local legal advice rather than treating this page as permission.

Foster care, adoption, school, and other care arrangements can carry additional rules and agreements. Follow lawful obligations. Scripture commends keeping promises (Psalm 15:4). If you are uncertain about a rule, ask the responsible authority before acting. Church counsel does not replace legal requirements or a clinician’s assessment of harm.

Discipline Under the Cross

But he was wounded for our transgressions, he was bruised for our iniquities: the chastisement of our peace was upon him; and with his stripes we are healed.

Isaiah 53:5 (KJV)

The chastisement of our peace is the punishment that made peace between sinners and God. It fell on Christ. He bore it in full for his people, and he rose on the third day because the debt was paid. Christ bare our sins in his own body on the tree, by whose stripes ye were healed (1 Peter 2:24). If you don’t yet know him, read The Gospel before anything else on this page.

So if you’re in Christ, God’s discipline of you is never payment for your sin. That was paid once, on the cross. The Confession describes God’s adopted children in this excerpt as

pitied, protected, provided for, and chastened by him as by a father; yet never cast off

Westminster Confession of Faith 12.1

A sinning believer may fall under God’s fatherly displeasure (Westminster Confession 11.5), but it’s a Father’s displeasure, not a Judge’s sentence (Romans 8:1).

And so you correct your child as one who has been forgiven. You aren’t making him pay for his sin. No child can pay that debt, and only Christ could. You correct to turn him from folly and to point him to the Savior who bore the stripes. When it’s over, tell him of Christ. A parent who remembers how much he has been forgiven won’t correct with contempt. Be ye kind one to another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, even as God for Christ’s sake hath forgiven you (Ephesians 4:32).

Before You Discipline Your Child

  1. Choose the instruction the child needs. Explain one clear rule rather than reciting every past fault.
  2. Check your expectations. Ask whether the child understands and can perform the task. Offer help before treating inability as refusal.
  3. Agree on a safe response. Parents and other caregivers should use consistent, age-appropriate, nonviolent consequences and keep essential care secure.
  4. Make time outside correction. Listen, play, and encourage the child. Notice specific efforts to do what is right.
  5. Take responsibility for your own wrong. If you frightened or hurt the child, stop, seek help, and confess without asking the child to conceal it or comfort you.

The King your children most need to know is the one who shall gather the lambs with his arm, and carry them in his bosom (Isaiah 40:11). Let your care and teaching reflect that gentleness.
