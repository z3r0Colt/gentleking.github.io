Where Christ Feeds His People

The Church

Christ didn’t save you and leave you on your own. He put you in a body, and he set a table in it.

Christ gathers the people he has redeemed into his church. They belong to him and to one another, and they live under his rule. He has appointed plain, ordinary means to give his grace, and they haven’t changed.

The outward and ordinary means whereby Christ communicateth to us the benefits of redemption are his ordinances, especially the word, sacraments, and prayer; all which are made effectual to the elect for salvation.

Westminster Shorter Catechism Q&A 88

The preached word comes first. Not a talk about the Bible, but the Bible opened, explained and pressed home, with Christ held out in it. God has been pleased to save and to strengthen through this plain thing week after week.

Baptism is a sign and seal of the covenant of grace. It marks the washing away of sin, union with Christ in his death and resurrection, and belonging to God’s people. The Westminster Standards hold that it belongs to believers and to their children, as the covenant sign always has. The water doesn’t save. It signifies and seals what God does.

At the Lord’s Supper, Christ nourishes his people through a sacrament that remembers his once-for-all sacrifice. Christ is really present, not bodily but spiritually, and he is received by faith as his people eat and drink. This meal strengthens repentant believers who trust him.

And they continued stedfastly in the apostles’ doctrine and fellowship, and in breaking of bread, and in prayers.

Acts 2:42 (KJV)

Membership makes that care personal. To join a church is to be known, taught, corrected, and cared for by particular people. Elders must give account for their care of the flock (Hebrews 13:17). Christ gives church officers the keys of the kingdom for teaching and discipline (WCF 30.1-4). Their office is distinct from the civil magistrate's, although the original 1647 Confession also assigns the magistrate duties toward true religion (WCF 23.3). Church censures aim at repentance, the recovery of offenders, and the purity of the church.

On worship, the original Confession teaches what is called the regulative principle. God determines how he is to be worshiped (WCF 21.1). Chapter 21.5 names the reading and preaching of Scripture, prayer, singing of psalms, and the sacraments as ordinary parts of public worship. This site follows the Reformed Presbyterian practice of unaccompanied psalm singing. The words of the 1647 Confession are the standard quoted here. Later revisions and differing Presbyterian interpretations must be identified rather than silently substituted. How God Wants to Be Worshiped explains the practice, and the civil magistrate guide explains the original Confession's teaching on public religion.
