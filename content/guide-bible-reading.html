Why Read It Yourself

The Bible is God speaking. It's enough to make you wise unto salvation and to equip you for every good work (2 Timothy 3:15-17). Hearing it preached on the Lord’s Day comes first, but God also commands his people to read it, to know it, and to teach it at home (Deuteronomy 6:6-7, Acts 17:11, WLC 156). See How to Lead Family Worship.

Begin with a book of Scripture and read it in its context. Ask your pastor when a passage troubles you. The things you must know to be saved are set out plainly. An ordinary reader, using ordinary means, can understand them (WCF 1.7).

How to Read It

The Larger Catechism says the Scriptures are to be read with a high and reverent esteem of them, with a firm persuasion that they're the very Word of God, with desire to know and obey, with diligence, and with prayer (Q&A 157). In plain steps, that comes to this.

  1. Pray before you read. Ask God to open your eyes (Psalm 119:18).
  2. Read whole books. Each book was written to be read through. Don’t hop between favorite verses.
  3. Read slowly enough to notice. Ask what it says, what it means, and what it asks of you.
  4. Let clear places explain hard ones. Scripture is its own interpreter (WCF 1.9). When a verse is hard, find other places that say the same thing more plainly.
  5. Look for Christ. The whole Bible points to him (Luke 24:27, John 5:39).
  6. Do what it says. Be ye doers of the word, and not hearers only (James 1:22). Write down one thing to obey or believe today.

Where to Start

  1. If you’re new, start with the Gospel of Mark or John, then Acts, then Romans, then Genesis. Read a Psalm each day alongside.
  2. If you want the whole Bible, use a plan. Robert Murray M’Cheyne’s takes you through the Old Testament once and the New Testament and Psalms twice in a year. A separate, simpler plan of two chapters a day takes about twenty months to read all 1,189 chapters once.
  3. If you fall behind, skip to today’s reading and keep going. A plan is a help, not a law.

Work Through One Passage

Try Mark 2:1-12. Read the whole account before stopping over individual words. Notice where Jesus is, who brings the sick man, what Jesus says first, and why the scribes object. Read the passages on either side too. Mark has been showing Christ preaching, healing, and exercising authority. This account belongs to that unfolding testimony.

Now describe what happens in your own words. Friends bring a man who cannot walk. Jesus declares his sins forgiven. The scribes recognize that forgiveness concerns God's authority. Jesus then heals the man as a visible demonstration of his authority to forgive sins. The passage directs attention to who Christ is and what he has authority to do.

Keep observation and application connected. The account gives a reason to trust Christ for forgiveness. It also shows the friends' determined care for the man. It doesn't promise that every sick person will be healed immediately, or that removing an obstacle obliges God to give the result you want. An application must follow what the passage actually teaches.

You could finish by confessing a known sin and asking Christ for mercy. You might also ask how you can help someone reach the church's care. Write down a question you can't answer and bring it to your pastor. You have read fruitfully even if you haven't discovered something no one has noticed before.

When Reading Is Difficult

A difficult chapter doesn't cancel the clarity of the gospel. The Confession distinguishes the saving truths Scripture states plainly from passages that require more work (WCF 1.7). Read what you can understand, note the difficulty, and use the ordinary helps God provides. You don't need to solve every question about Revelation before you can believe what John says about Christ.

In a long argument, follow the connecting words. A therefore points back to reasons already given. A command may follow several chapters explaining what Christ has done. In an Old Testament law, ask who received it and how the New Testament explains its continuing use. The moral law remains binding, while Israel's ceremonial laws have been fulfilled in Christ and its civil laws require careful attention to their general equity (WCF 19.3-6). How to Read the Bible Rightly explains those distinctions.

If concentration or reading ability limits you, use a smaller portion and listen to it as well. Repeating a chapter can be more useful than rushing through unfamiliar pages. A parent can read with a child, and a friend can read with someone who struggles to read alone. These are ways to receive the same Word.

Keep a question open when the text hasn't settled it. Don't treat the first search result as an explanation simply because it sounds certain. Compare its answer with the passage, a careful commentary, and your church's teaching. Bring the actual verse and the proposed explanation to someone who can help you examine both.

Helps Worth Having

  1. A faithful translation. This site quotes the King James Version. The ESV and the NASB are careful modern translations.
  2. A good commentary. Matthew Henry is warm and plain. Calvin is careful and deep. Read the text first and the commentary second.
  3. The Shorter Catechism. It shows you how the parts of the Bible fit together.
  4. Your pastor. Bring your questions to him. That’s part of what he is for.
