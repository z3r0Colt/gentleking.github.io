Gather your household at a workable time, read a short Bible passage, sing a psalm, and pray together. Begin with what the people present can understand and take part in. The length and time of day are practical choices, not a rule for every home.

Why a Household Worships

God commanded parents to teach his words to their children at home, when they sit, walk, lie down, and rise up (Deuteronomy 6:6-7). The Confession says God is to be worshiped in private families daily, as well as in secret and in public (WCF 21.6). The Church of Scotland approved the Directory for Family-Worship in 1647 to guide this duty.

Fathers, this is your charge first (Ephesians 6:4). A mother raising children alone and a couple without children can lead worship in their households too. If you live alone, keep private worship. Believing roommates can agree to pray together, but don’t presume authority over another adult or force an unbelieving housemate to take part.

Parents who want to understand their children's place in the covenant can read Baptism and Church Membership.

What to Do

Ten or fifteen minutes may be a useful starting point. A shorter gathering may fit young children, illness, or limited attention. Use a Bible and a psalter, a book of psalms arranged for singing.

  1. Read. Read a short passage, going straight through a Bible book a chapter or part of a chapter at a time. Explain unfamiliar words. An audio Bible or someone reading for a person with limited sight can help. Ask one or two simple questions about it. What does this teach us about God? What does it tell us to do?
  2. Sing. Sing a psalm, or part of one. Learn a few tunes and use them often. Ask your congregation for a psalter and help learning a tune. If someone cannot sing, help them follow the words as they are able.
  3. Pray. Thank God, confess sin, and ask for the needs of the family, the church, and the world. Let older children pray too.

Many households add a question or two from the Shorter Catechism, or the Catechism for Young Children with little ones.

How to Start and Keep Going

  1. Pick a time you already share. A meal may provide a shared time. With changing shifts, use the time when the household can gather rather than insisting on an evening schedule.
  2. Start small. Read a few verses, sing a short portion, and pray about the needs you have discussed. Let children ask questions without turning the gathering into a test.
  3. Do not wait until you feel ready. Read the passage, sing a psalm, and pray together. A short, faithful gathering is enough to begin.
  4. Resume after a missed day. If you miss a day, resume without trying to repay it with a long session. Meet immediate care needs rather than making a sick or distressed child wait for the gathering to finish.
  5. Ask your elders for help. Ask for help understanding a passage or leading prayer. If another adult in the home opposes worship, seek counsel about teaching your children without exposing anyone to threats or abuse.
