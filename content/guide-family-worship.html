Gather your household at a workable time, read a short Bible passage, sing a psalm, and pray together. Begin with what the people present can understand and take part in. The length and time of day are practical choices, not a rule for every home.

Why a Household Worships

God commanded parents to teach his words to their children at home, when they sit, walk, lie down, and rise up (Deuteronomy 6:6-7). The Confession says God is to be worshiped in private families daily, as well as in secret and in public (WCF 21.6). The Church of Scotland approved the Directory for Family-Worship in 1647 to guide this duty.

Fathers, this is your charge first (Ephesians 6:4). A mother raising children alone and a couple without children can lead worship in their households too. If you live alone, keep private worship. Believing roommates can agree to pray together, but don’t presume authority over another adult or force an unbelieving housemate to take part.

Parents who want to understand their children's place in the covenant can read Baptism and Church Membership.

What to Do

Ten or fifteen minutes may be a useful starting point. A shorter gathering may fit young children, illness, or limited attention. Use a Bible and a psalter, a book of psalms arranged for singing.

  1. Read. Read a short passage, going straight through a Bible book a chapter or part of a chapter at a time. Explain unfamiliar words. An audio Bible or someone reading for a person with limited sight can help. Ask one or two simple questions about it. What does this teach us about God? What does it tell us to do?
  2. Sing. Sing a psalm, or part of one. Learn a few tunes and use them often. Ask your congregation for a psalter and help learning a tune. If someone cannot sing, help them follow the words as they are able.
  3. Pray. Thank God, confess sin, and ask for the needs of the family, the church, and the world. Let older children pray too.

Many households add a question or two from the Shorter Catechism, or the Catechism for Young Children with little ones.

An Ordinary Evening Together

Suppose the household has finished supper and a child is restless. Open Mark 2:1-12 and read about the man brought to Jesus. Ask what Jesus said before healing him and why the scribes objected. Explain that the miracle showed Christ's authority to forgive sins. A younger child might answer one simple question, while an older child asks why forgiveness is the greater need.

Sing a familiar portion of a psalm from your congregation's psalter, without instrumental accompaniment. Use the same tune for a while if learning several tunes makes the gathering difficult. Read the words before singing when their meaning is unfamiliar. The object is thoughtful praise to God, not a musical performance.

Then pray about what you read and the household's real needs. Confess a known sin without using the prayer to accuse a particular child. Give thanks for Christ's mercy, ask for help with tomorrow's duties, and remember someone in the congregation. If you use a catechism, explain one answer instead of requiring children to recite words they have never been helped to understand.

That example isn't an appointed order every family must copy. The duty is daily household worship. The choice of passage, tune, length, and time requires ordinary judgment. Keep the elements God has given while arranging them so that the people present can take part.

Lead the Household in Worship

A father shouldn't leave the duty untouched because he feels less knowledgeable than his wife. Read a passage you can understand, prepare a short explanation, and ask your elders about what troubles you. A husband and wife can help each other prepare. Neither needs to pretend to be the household's ordained minister. Family worship supports the church's ministry and doesn't authorize a parent to administer the sacraments at home.

Parents should require children's fitting participation and teach reverence. A child who is deliberately mocking the reading needs patient correction. A tired toddler, a child who cannot sit still for long, and a teenager with an honest question need different responses. Explain the expectation and distinguish unwillingness from inability. Don't make the length of the gathering a test of whether your family is godly.

If an unbelieving spouse objects, explain what you intend and listen to the actual concern. Ask about a workable time and a way to teach the children without making a public contest of the disagreement. Seek pastoral counsel when parental responsibilities conflict. You must not renounce God's commands, but Scripture doesn't give one adult authority to force another adult to make a profession of faith.

When shifts prevent everyone gathering at once, lead those who are present and arrange another opportunity with the others. A parent away from home can support the parent who is caring for the children. Necessary care may interrupt the reading. Resume when you can. Faithfulness includes helping the sick child whose needs interrupt your plan.

Let the teaching continue outside the set time. Deuteronomy 6:6-7 places God's words in the ordinary life of the household. Speak about truthfulness after a lie, God's providence when plans change, and gratitude over a meal. Daily worship gives those conversations a steady place to begin.

How to Start and Keep Going

  1. Pick a time you already share. A meal may provide a shared time. With changing shifts, use the time when the household can gather rather than insisting on an evening schedule.
  2. Start small. Read a few verses, sing a short portion, and pray about the needs you have discussed. Let children ask questions without turning the gathering into a test.
  3. Do not wait until you feel ready. Read the passage, sing a psalm, and pray together. A short, faithful gathering is enough to begin.
  4. Resume after a missed day. If you miss a day, resume without trying to repay it with a long session. Meet immediate care needs rather than making a sick or distressed child wait for the gathering to finish.
  5. Ask your elders for help. Ask for help understanding a passage or leading prayer. If another adult in the home opposes worship, seek counsel about teaching your children without exposing anyone to threats or abuse.
