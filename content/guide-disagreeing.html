Three Kinds of Difference

Christians will disagree until Christ comes. First know what kind of disagreement you’re in, because Scripture doesn’t treat them all alike.

  1. Matters of the faith. Who God is, who Christ is, and how a sinner is saved. To deny these is heresy, and you must earnestly contend for the faith which was once delivered unto the saints (Jude 3). How to Spot Real Heresy sets out where that line runs.
  2. Serious error. A brother is wrong on something Scripture teaches that matters, such as baptism, worship, or the government of the church. But he doesn’t deny the gospel. Hold your ground, and still own him as a brother in Christ. Some questions of interpretation sit near here, such as the thousand years of Revelation 20. Faithful Reformed men have read them differently (see Prophecy).
  3. Matters of conscience and wisdom. Things Scripture neither commands nor forbids. Here God has left believers free.

God alone is Lord of the conscience, and hath left it free from the doctrines and commandments of men which are in anything contrary to his Word, or beside it, in matters of faith or worship.

Westminster Confession of Faith 20.2

To tell which you’re in, ask two questions. Does this touch the gospel itself? Does Scripture command or forbid this, either in plain words or by good and necessary consequence (WCF 1.6)? If not, it’s a matter of liberty, like whether to drink wine (Alcohol). Some questions sit where faithful believers weigh Scripture’s principles differently, such as tattoos (Tattoos) or the spacing of children (Birth Control). Wisdom still applies, but leave room where God leaves room.

Treat your opinions as gospel and you become a Pharisee. Treat the gospel as opinion and you have nothing left to stand on.

Romans 14 in Practice

Rome was divided over food and holy days, and Paul told both sides how to treat each other.

Let not him that eateth despise him that eateth not; and let not him which eateth not judge him that eateth: for God hath received him.

Romans 14:3 (KJV)

The strong brother knows the thing is lawful. His temptation is to despise the tender conscience. The weak brother thinks a lawful thing is sin. His temptation is to judge the other as worldly. Paul forbids both. Each will answer to his own master (Romans 14:4, 10-12).

Let every man be fully persuaded in his own mind (Romans 14:5). Settle it before God, and never push a brother to act against his conscience, for whatsoever is not of faith is sin (Romans 14:23).

Love sets limits on liberty. Destroy not him with thy meat, for whom Christ died (Romans 14:15). If your freedom would lead a brother to sin against his conscience, lay it down (1 Corinthians 8:9-13). Christ laid down far more for you. Yet bearing with a weaker brother isn’t the same as letting any man make his scruple a law for the church. Paul wouldn't have believers bound by Touch not, taste not, handle not, after the commandments and doctrines of men (Colossians 2:20-22).

How to Disagree Well

  1. Listen first. Be swift to hear, slow to speak, slow to wrath (James 1:19). State his view so fairly that he would agree with it.
  2. Speak the truth in love (Ephesians 4:15). Both halves. Truth without love is a club. Love without truth leaves him in his error.
  3. Don’t fight to win. Aim at his good and God’s truth.

And the servant of the Lord must not strive; but be gentle unto all men, apt to teach, patient, In meekness instructing those that oppose themselves; if God peradventure will give them repentance to the acknowledging of the truth;

2 Timothy 2:24-25 (KJV)
  1. Assume the best. Charity believeth all things, hopeth all things (1 Corinthians 13:7). The Larger Catechism counts misconstructing intentions, words, and actions as a sin against the ninth commandment (Q&A 145). Read him in the kindest way his words honestly bear.
  2. Argue the point, not the man. No mocking, no labels, no guessing at motives. Remember you may be the one who is wrong (Matthew 7:3-5).

Two Quarrels in Scripture

Paul and Barnabas

Paul and Barnabas couldn’t agree whether to take John Mark again, since he had left them before. And the contention was so sharp between them, that they departed asunder one from the other (Acts 15:39). It was a matter of wisdom, not doctrine, and Scripture doesn’t say who was right.

But later Paul names Barnabas as a fellow worker (1 Corinthians 9:6) and tells the Colossians to receive Mark (Colossians 4:10). From prison near the end of his life he writes, Take Mark, and bring him with thee (2 Timothy 4:11). Good men may part ways. They mustn't stay enemies.

Paul and Peter

At Antioch Peter stopped eating with Gentile believers out of fear of men. Paul writes, I withstood him to the face, because he was to be blamed (Galatians 2:11). He did it before them all, because the wrong was public and they walked not uprightly according to the truth of the gospel (Galatians 2:14). If Gentiles had to live as Jews to sit at the table, then Christ alone wasn't enough.

When the gospel is at stake, love speaks up, even to an apostle. Yet this quarrel didn’t end in hatred. Peter later calls him our beloved brother Paul (2 Peter 3:15).

Online, in the Church, and Letting Go

Online

All of this applies online, and more so. You can’t see his face, and an audience tempts you to perform. In the multitude of words there wanteth not sin (Proverbs 10:19). Before you post, ask whether you would say it at his table. Ask whether you’re writing to help him or to be seen winning. Often the faithful answer is a phone call, or silence.

In the Church

For an ordinary personal offense, go to your brother privately first (Matthew 18:15). This does not require confronting a dangerous person alone or delaying a report of abuse or crime. Seek protection and civil help when those are needed. If you differ with your church’s teaching or a decision of the session, take it to your elders humbly and in person. They watch for your souls, as they that must give account (Hebrews 13:17). Don’t take it to the parking lot or the group chat. Gathering a party is how churches split, and God hates him that soweth discord among brethren (Proverbs 6:19).

When to Let It Go

The discretion of a man deferreth his anger; and it is his glory to pass over a transgression.

Proverbs 19:11 (KJV)

Not every slight needs answering, and not every mistaken opinion needs correcting by you. Charity shall cover the multitude of sins (1 Peter 4:8). Let small things go, and let them go for good. But don’t let go of the gospel, or keep silent about sin that's harming someone. Take those to the elders.

Find the Point You Actually Disagree About

Ask the other person to explain the belief in his own words and the passage he thinks establishes it. Repeat the explanation back until he recognizes it. Then state the particular point you believe is mistaken. A conversation about baptism, for example, needs to identify whether the dispute concerns its subjects, its mode, or what it signifies. Moving among all three can make a genuine discussion impossible.

Read the governing passages together and explain how your conclusion follows. Distinguish what the text directly says from the steps of your argument. Good and necessary consequences carry authority because they follow from Scripture. An association or a plausible guess doesn't have that same force.

If you've discovered an error in your own account, correct it without requiring the other person to concede something first. You can acknowledge that you misquoted a source while continuing to believe your main position is true. Accuracy helps the discussion and fulfills a duty you already owe.

Set a suitable limit for the conversation. You may need time to read, consult a minister, or return to ordinary responsibilities. Agreeing to continue later can give both people a chance to consider the evidence. Don't treat the last unanswered message as proof that you've won.

Exercise Liberty with Regard for Another Person

Suppose a Christian guest believes he shouldn't drink wine, while you believe moderate drinking is lawful. Don't press him to drink in order to prove that he understands liberty. Offer something he can receive with a clear conscience. You can discuss the relevant passages at a suitable time without making his participation in a meal depend on changing his view immediately.

His conviction also doesn't create a new divine prohibition for every household in the church. Romans 14 requires patience and rejects both contempt and judgment. Instruction should help a mistaken conscience become better informed rather than leaving it forever governed by fear or turning it into a rule for everyone else.

A disagreement about something God actually commands is different. You can't use Romans 14 to make honesty, marital faithfulness, or worship optional. Determine first whether the disputed act belongs within Christian liberty. Then consider whether a particular exercise of lawful liberty would encourage someone else to sin.

In a congregation, follow lawful common arrangements even when you would've chosen another permissible option. If a decision violates Scripture, bring the concrete concern through the proper process. Peace includes patient cooperation and truthful correction, each in its appropriate place.

This Week

  1. Name one disagreement you’re in. Decide honestly which kind it is.
  2. If it’s a matter of liberty, confess any despising or judging to God, and to your brother if he knows of it.
  3. Pray for him by name, then write out his view in a few sentences he would agree with. If you can’t, you’re not ready to answer it.
  4. Walk away from one online argument.
  5. If you’ve been talking behind his back, stop. Go to him, or to your elders.
  6. If you parted from a brother like Paul and Barnabas, call or write. First be reconciled to thy brother (Matthew 5:24).
  7. Remember how you were received. Receive ye one another, as Christ also received us to the glory of God (Romans 15:7). If you’ve never come to him, start with the gospel.
