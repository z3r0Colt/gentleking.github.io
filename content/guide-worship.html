In public worship, join the congregation in hearing Scripture, prayer, and psalm singing. If you are visiting, ask for the order of service and any help you need to follow it. Speak with an elder before receiving communion. The guide below explains why Reformed Presbyterian worship follows these particular practices.

The Principle

God determines how he is to be worshiped. The acts we offer as worship must have his warrant in Scripture. The Reformed churches call this the regulative principle of worship.

But the acceptable way of worshipping the true God is instituted by himself, and so limited by his own revealed will, that he may not be worshipped according to the imaginations and devices of men, or the suggestions of Satan, under any visible representation, or any other way not prescribed in the holy Scripture.

Westminster Confession of Faith 21.1

This excerpt states the principle. A different approach permits practices that Scripture does not forbid. The Reformed approach asks for positive biblical warrant, including what follows by good and necessary consequence (WCF 1.6). It also distinguishes the acts of worship from their circumstances, explained below.

Approach the Father through the Son

Acceptable worship begins with the access Christ has opened. Hebrews 10:19–22 grounds our drawing near in his blood and continuing priesthood. A sinner doesn't become acceptable by arranging an impressive service or by keeping every outward detail correctly. We come through the Mediator who reconciles his people to God. Ephesians 2:18 describes access to the Father through Christ by the Spirit.

That access leads to reverent obedience. The Savior who opens the way also governs his church. We should seek both faith in him and conformity to his instructions. WCF 21.2 teaches worship of the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, through Christ alone as Mediator. Neither saints nor angels can supply a second route to God.

When Jesus speaks of worship in spirit and truth in John 4:21–24, he doesn't authorize whatever produces a spiritual feeling. He announces worship no longer confined to the old sacred locations and requires the worship suited to God's nature and revelation. The heart must truly engage with the God who has made himself known. Correct outward practice and sincere inward response therefore belong together.

How Biblical Warrant Is Established

The church must consider commands, the institutions Christ gave, and what follows from Scripture by good and necessary consequence. It also examines apostolic teaching and practice in their setting. WCF 1.6 doesn't require a separate verse naming every lawful circumstance, but it does deny the church authority to add new revelation or bind consciences with invented religious duties.

In 1 Corinthians 14, Paul gives a sustained example of apostolic regulation. Speech in the assembly must serve understanding and edification, and the gathering must be orderly. The chapter isn't permission to introduce anything an audience finds helpful. Paul directs the use of gifts God had actually given and subjects their exercise to his instructions.

Likewise, an Old Testament practice must be considered within its covenantal place. The temple's sacrifices, priests, and ceremonies were authorized for that administration. Their fulfillment in Christ affects what the church does now (Hebrews 9:1–14). We can't select an old ceremony merely because it was once commanded while ignoring the New Testament's explanation of its fulfillment.

The question is therefore more substantial than whether we can find a religious activity somewhere in the Bible. Ask to whom it was appointed, what purpose it served, whether it continues, and what Christ has commanded his church to observe.

Where Scripture Teaches It

  1. The second commandment. God forbids worshiping him by images or by any means of our own making (Exodus 20:4-6). The Larger Catechism says it forbids all devising, counselling, commanding, using, and any wise approving any religious worship not instituted by God himself (Q&A 109).
  2. Nadab and Abihu. Two priests offered strange fire before the Lord, which he commanded them not, and fire from the Lord devoured them (Leviticus 10:1-2). The account emphasizes that their offering lacked God’s authorization. Moses then declares that God must be sanctified by those who approach him (10:3).
  3. Add nothing, take nothing away. God told Israel to observe what he commanded, and not to add thereto, nor diminish from it (Deuteronomy 12:32).
  4. Worship that God never asked for. Jeremiah condemns the burning of children at Tophet, which I commanded them not, neither came it into my heart (Jeremiah 7:31). This was a forbidden atrocity, not merely an unspecified ceremony. The passage must be read with God’s explicit condemnation of such sacrifices (Deuteronomy 12:31).
  5. Jesus on the traditions of men. In vain they do worship me, teaching for doctrines the commandments of men (Matthew 15:9).
  6. Paul on will-worship. He warned against worship men devise for themselves, however humble or devout it looks (Colossians 2:20-23).

What God Has Commanded

The Confession lists the parts of ordinary worship, which are called its elements (WCF 21.3-5).

  1. Prayer, with thanksgiving, in the name of Christ.
  2. The reading of Scripture with godly fear.
  3. Preaching of the Word, and hearing it with understanding, faith, and reverence.
  4. Singing of psalms with grace in the heart.
  5. The sacraments, baptism and the Lord’s Supper, rightly given and worthily received.

On special occasions the Confession adds religious oaths, vows, solemn fastings, and thanksgivings. Giving to the Lord and the benediction belong to the service as well (1 Corinthians 16:1-2, Numbers 6:24-26, 2 Corinthians 13:14).

Elements and Circumstances

The principle doesn’t mean Scripture must tell you what time to meet, how long the sermon runs, or whether to sit in pews. Those are circumstances, things common to any gathering of people. The Confession says they're to be ordered by the light of nature and Christian prudence, according to the general rules of the Word (WCF 1.6).

The test is simple. An element is a thing done as worship, and it needs a command. A circumstance is only how and when the commanded thing is done, and it needs good sense and good order (1 Corinthians 14:40).

What It Means in Practice

  1. No images of God. No pictures, statues, or films of the Father, the Son, or the Spirit, in worship or out of it (WLC 109).
  2. No holy days of men’s making. God appointed the Lord’s Day. The Westminster Directory for Public Worship says no other day is commanded to be kept holy.
  3. No entertainment in place of worship. No drama, dance, light shows, or performances. Worship isn’t a show for the congregation. It’s the congregation meeting God.
  4. The Word at the center. Reading and preaching aren’t a short segment between songs. They are the heart of the service.
  5. Psalms, sung without instruments. The RPCNA describes its practice as singing the biblical Psalms without instruments. God gave his church a book of praise, the Psalms, and commanded it to be sung (Colossians 3:16, James 5:13). The instruments of the Old Testament were appointed for the temple and its sacrifices (2 Chronicles 29:25-28). They passed away with them when Christ fulfilled them. The New Testament church is told to sing, making melody in your heart to the Lord (Ephesians 5:19).

That last point is where Presbyterians who hold the same principle part ways. Many sing hymns and use instruments, and they're brothers. This site holds the Reformed Presbyterian position described here. It holds it as a conviction from Scripture, not as a matter of taste.

Why the Church Sings the Inspired Psalms

The Psalms give the church an inspired collection of praise, thanksgiving, confession, lament, and petitions. Their suitability for worship rests on God's provision and appointment. Singing them teaches the congregation to respond to him with the breadth and truth of the words he has given, including truths we might neglect if we selected only expressions that match today's mood.

Ephesians 5:18–20 and Colossians 3:16 call Christians to sing psalms, hymns, and spiritual songs. Exclusive psalmody understands these descriptions within the Bible's provision of inspired praise, rather than as an authorization to compose additional texts for worship. Christians who permit uninspired hymns read the expressions more broadly. The phrase alone shouldn't be treated as though no interpretation were involved. The Reformed Presbyterian argument joins the command to sing with the appointed inspired songs and the requirement for warrant before adding another source of worship song.

That position doesn't claim that every Christian poem is false or that singing a Psalm earns acceptance with God. It identifies the words authorized for this act of worship. Prayer and preaching also require warrant, but their appointed work includes petitions and exposition in words suited to the occasion. Their authorization doesn't settle the separate question of what God appoints the church to sing.

The Psalms are also Christian praise. Psalm 2 declares the rule of God's Son, Psalm 22 speaks of the sufferer whose words Christ took on his lips, and Psalm 110 announces the royal priesthood explained in Hebrews. Hebrews 2:11–12 applies Psalm 22 to Christ's fellowship with his brethren. Read and sing the Psalms in the light of their fulfillment in him, rather than treating them as a collection from which the church has outgrown its need.

Sing the Whole Range of Biblical Praise

Laments teach us to bring distress before God. Confessions teach us to name sin. Royal Psalms teach us to rejoice in Christ's dominion. The church needs those responses as well as thanksgiving. Understanding the words may require explanation before singing, especially when a Psalm recalls an event in Israel's history or uses an unfamiliar figure.

The prayers for judgment require particular care. We ask God to vindicate his righteousness, defend his church, and overthrow evil. We aren't given permission to baptize a personal grudge. Romans 12:19–21 commands us to leave vengeance to God and do good to our enemies. We may pray for an enemy's conversion and for the defeat of his wicked purposes without contradiction.

Revelation 5:9 describes a new song in a heavenly vision. Christians who favor new compositions appeal to passages like this. The description must be understood in its own setting, where God reveals the praise offered before his throne. It doesn't by itself give every earthly congregation authority to compose additional worship songs. The church must establish its ordinary practice from the instructions given to it, rather than reproduce selected features of a vision.

Revelation 5:8 also describes harps and golden vessels of incense in the heavenly vision, explaining the incense as the prayers of the saints. Its imagery must be interpreted as part of that revelation. It doesn't independently institute an earthly service with each of those features. The church must follow the worship Christ appointed for it.

A metrical psalter puts the inspired text into a form a congregation can sing together. Examine its faithfulness to the Psalm's meaning. A familiar tune is useful, but familiarity must serve understanding. Sing thoughtfully, and ask for help with a difficult portion instead of leaving its words unexplained.

Why Reformed Presbyterian Singing Is Unaccompanied

The objection to instruments in worship doesn't arise from a judgment that instruments are inherently evil. It concerns their place in the appointed worship of the church. Second Chronicles 29:25–28 connects the temple musicians and their instruments with God's command through the prophets and with the burnt offering. This was an authorized institution within the temple service.

The Reformed Presbyterian position understands that ceremonial institution as fulfilled with the temple order in Christ. Hebrews 9 and Hebrews 10 explain the passing of the old sanctuary and sacrificial administration. The New Testament directs the church to sing, with melody in the heart to the Lord (Ephesians 5:19). It doesn't appoint the temple's instrumental service for the gathered church.

Christians who use instruments commonly regard them as an aid or circumstance of the commanded singing. The disagreement therefore involves both covenantal interpretation and the distinction between an act of worship and its circumstances. This site judges instrumental accompaniment in worship to belong to the ceremonial service that has ceased, rather than to an indifferent arrangement like selecting a meeting time.

That conclusion requires an argument from the whole setting, rather than merely observing that one New Testament verse doesn't mention an instrument. Psalm 150 also belongs in that discussion. Its commanded instrumental praise was true and right within the worship God then appointed. Reading it as Scripture today doesn't require restoring every temple practice it describes. We retain its call to praise God while receiving the New Testament's direction for the church's worship.

How the Principle Protects Conscience and Common Order

A congregation must choose circumstances such as a place, time, tune, and order of service. Those decisions should serve intelligibility, participation, and good order. Members should ordinarily cooperate with a lawful common arrangement even when another option would suit their preferences better. Christian liberty doesn't make a gathered service impossible to arrange.

At the same time, officers can't turn their preferences into divine institutions. A particular tune isn't sacred because an elder prefers it. A service's visual atmosphere doesn't make prayer more acceptable. If someone introduces a ceremony as a spiritual duty, the congregation is entitled to ask for its biblical warrant. WCF 20.2 and 1.6 require both freedom from invented obligations and submission to the Word.

Private and family worship remain under God's authority too. WCF 21.6 distinguishes their setting from public assemblies without supplying a separate principle of acceptable worship. The church's ordinances must also be kept within their proper administration. Family prayer and Psalm singing don't authorize a parent to administer the Lord's Supper at home.

Use these distinctions to pursue faithful worship with the church. Explain questions accurately, receive instruction, and bring an unresolved error through the proper oversight. The aim is a congregation that honors God together in the means Christ has appointed.

Why It Matters

  1. It honors God. He is the one being worshiped. He has the right to say how.
  2. It frees your conscience. God alone is Lord of the conscience (WCF 20.2). No pastor or church can bind you to worship God in a way he never asked for.
  3. It keeps the church from drifting. Requiring biblical warrant gives the church a standard by which to examine proposed practices. Christians who hold another principle also appeal to Scripture, and their arguments should be represented fairly.
  4. It keeps Christ central. The things God commanded, the Word, the sacraments, and prayer, are the means by which Christ gives his grace (WSC 88). Men’s additions crowd them out.

Common Objections

  1. It’s legalistic. Trying to earn God’s favor by worship would be wrong. Acceptance with God rests on Christ, received by faith. This principle concerns how God commands us to worship him. Obeying his instructions for worship isn’t earning anything. It’s love (John 14:15).
  2. The New Testament doesn’t give a worship manual. It gives enough. It shows the apostolic church reading, preaching, praying, singing, giving, and keeping the sacraments (Acts 2:42, 1 Corinthians 11, 14, 16:1-2, 1 Timothy 4:13). Those are what Reformed worship does.
  3. God looks at the heart. He does, and he also told us what to do. Nadab and Abihu were priests offering incense to the true God. Sincerity doesn’t make strange fire acceptable.

Take Part in the Appointed Worship

When Scripture is read, attend to it as God's Word. Follow in your Bible when that helps and ask for assistance if you can't hear. During congregational prayer, listen and join your heart to petitions that agree with God's will. When the congregation sings, sing the Psalm with understanding. An unfamiliar tune may take time to learn, but the words are something you can begin attending to immediately.

During preaching, follow the passage and receive its truth with faith and a willingness to obey. At baptism, remember God's covenant promises and your own obligations, whether you're witnessing another person's baptism or receiving it. At the Lord's Supper, those admitted should receive Christ by faith according to his appointment. These acts involve the congregation's attention and response, even when one appointed person is speaking.

Prepare to participate. Learn familiar Psalm tunes at home, bring the help you need to follow the service, and ask about anything unclear. Parents should teach children what the congregation is doing and gradually expect fuller participation as they grow. Necessary care for a child or a person with a disability belongs within the actual circumstances of worship, rather than being treated as contempt for it.

Reverence concerns both outward conduct and the heart. A quiet room doesn't excuse an unbelieving response to the Word, and enthusiastic singing doesn't excuse a refusal to obey it. Ask God for attention, faith, and love in the worship he commands.

Consider a Proposed Practice Carefully

Imagine a congregation considering a different meeting time and a dramatic performance during the service. Both are changes, but they raise different questions. Choosing a time orders the gathering so people can worship together. A performance offered as worship introduces an act whose biblical warrant must be established. Calling it helpful or moving doesn't answer that question.

A circumstance must still serve the general rules of Scripture. A meeting time that needlessly prevents members from attending can be poorly chosen even though no particular hour is commanded. Seating, audibility, and access likewise require care for the people gathering. WCF 1.6 directs such arrangements by Christian prudence under the Word.

Ask what a practice actually does and what authority is claimed for it. Compare the explanation with Scripture, then discuss unresolved questions with the elders. Maintain the duty to worship and receive lawful oversight while using proper means to address an error. The liberty of conscience taught in WCF 20.2 frees you from human inventions imposed as divine commands. It doesn't free you from God's own commands or make individual preference the rule of a congregation.

What to Do

  1. Read WCF 21 with your Bible open. Check every proof text.
  2. Look at your church’s worship. For each part of the service, ask where God commanded it. Ask your elders to explain the biblical warrant for a practice you don’t understand. A sincere question is not itself a refusal to worship.
  3. Learn the Psalms. Sing them at home, in family worship, until you know many of them by heart.
  4. Come prepared on the Lord’s Day. See How to Keep the Lord’s Day.
