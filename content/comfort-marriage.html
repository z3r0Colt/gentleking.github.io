If you’re afraid of your husband or wife, or of anyone at home, read this first.

If you’re the one who broke faith, there is a word for you further down.

You may be wondering whether the marriage can be repaired, how to tell the children, or what to do when your spouse refuses to talk. Those aren't small questions. You don't have to make every decision today. Start with what is actually happening and what needs attention first. Find the kind of trouble you're facing, or go to practical help when living apart.

David knew this wound.

For it was not an enemy that reproached me; then I could have borne it: neither was it he that hated me that did magnify himself against me; then I would have hid myself from him: But it was thou, a man mine equal, my guide, and mine acquaintance. We took sweet counsel together, and walked unto the house of God in company.

Psalm 55:12-14 (KJV)

Of this friend David says, he hath broken his covenant (55:20).

On the night he was betrayed, Jesus said a psalm of David was being fulfilled in a friend who ate his bread and turned on him (John 13:18, Psalm 41:9). In the garden they all forsook him, and fled (Mark 14:50). He knows the pain of being forsaken by those closest to him.

You may grieve this. It’s a real loss, and there’s no funeral for it. If the children are gone from the house too, that’s a second loss. God himself speaks to this grief.

For thy Maker is thine husband; the Lord of hosts is his name; and thy Redeemer the Holy One of Israel; The God of the whole earth shall he be called. For the Lord hath called thee as a woman forsaken and grieved in spirit, and a wife of youth, when thou wast refused, saith thy God.

Isaiah 54:5-6 (KJV)

God pictures himself as a husband calling home a wife who was refused, which means cast off. It’s a figure, and the faithfulness it pictures is real. If you belong to his people, he won’t forsake you. He has said, I will never leave thee, nor forsake thee (Hebrews 13:5).

This is what God promises everyone who is justified by faith in Christ.

All those that are justified God vouchsafeth, in and for his only Son Jesus Christ, to make partakers of the grace of adoption; by which they are taken into the number, and enjoy the liberties and privileges of the children of God; have his name put upon them; receive the Spirit of adoption; have access to the throne of grace with boldness; are enabled to cry, Abba, Father; are pitied, protected, provided for, and chastened by him as by a father; yet never cast off, but sealed to the day of redemption, and inherit the promises, as heirs of everlasting salvation.

Westminster Confession of Faith 12.1

Vouchsafeth means graciously grants. Pitied, protected, provided for, even chastened as by a father, yet never cast off. A husband or wife can cast you off. If you’re his, your Father never will.

Scripture calls the church the bride of Christ. That’s a picture, and the union it pictures is real and can’t be broken (Larger Catechism Q&A 66). He loved the church, and gave himself for it (Ephesians 5:25). He died in the place of sinners. On the third day he rose, and now he lives to pray for all who come to God by him (Hebrews 7:25). He has never broken faith with his bride, and he never will.

You may need help sorting what has happened before you can decide what to do. A trusted elder can hear the matter carefully, while urgent questions about violence belong first to safety and the civil authorities.

In the days ahead, leave the wrong with God, as Christ committed himself to him that judgeth righteously (1 Peter 2:23). Scripture forbids revenge (Romans 12:17-19). It doesn’t forbid getting safe, or asking for justice. For your own sake, don’t send a message tonight that you’ll regret tomorrow. Keep the children out of the conflict, and never make them carry messages. There’s a word for them below.

First, Understand What Has Happened

An ordinary argument, a relationship that has grown cold, adultery, abandonment, and an unsafe home are not the same trouble. They shouldn't all receive the same counsel. If you and your spouse are disagreeing about money or household duties, there may be room for a careful conversation. If someone is using threats, violence, forced sex, or control to keep the other afraid, safety and independent help come first.

If the marriage is strained but no one is in danger, name one problem you can describe without guessing motives. Ask for a time when both of you can listen. Don't try to resolve years of unhappiness in one evening. If you get nowhere and the same argument returns, ask for wise counsel from someone who will hear both sides. The Marriage guide gives practical ways to handle ordinary conflict.

If you're frightened of your spouse's reaction, don't attempt a private confrontation because an article told you to. Contact a safe person or an abuse advocate. For United States support, the National Domestic Violence Hotline has safety-planning guidance. A safe plan can include children, transport, money, and privacy. Leaving without planning can increase danger in some situations.

If You Have Been Betrayed

Discovering adultery or another serious deception may leave you angry one hour and numb the next. You may question what else was hidden and blame yourself for not seeing it. You can ask for truthful answers without accepting responsibility for another person's choice to sin. Don't let anyone hurry you into a public announcement, a confrontation, or a decision about divorce while you are overwhelmed.

Keep a record of important facts and agreements, particularly where money, children's care, or safety is concerned. You can speak with trusted elders and obtain independent legal advice about rights and deadlines. If sexual unfaithfulness has raised medical concerns, ask a clinician about appropriate testing or care. You don't need to explain every intimate detail to the whole congregation.

Forgiveness is a Christian duty, but forgiveness doesn't make deceit true or restore trust automatically. Real repentance includes acknowledging the wrong, ending the conduct, and accepting appropriate accountability. A spouse who has been harmed may need time and distance. You may seek counsel about whether reconciliation is possible without promising it in advance. Read the distinction between forgiveness and trust if that question is troubling you.

What God has said about divorce

If you’re afraid at home, getting safe comes first, and all of this can wait. Leaving to be safe isn’t divorce, and it needs none of the grounds below.

Someone may have told you that God hates divorce, as if it were a word against you. In Malachi God says he hateth putting away, and in the same breath he condemns violence. He was rebuking husbands who dealt treacherously against the wife of their youth (Malachi 2:14-16). It’s a word against the one who breaks faith, not the one who was put away or left.

Jesus named one ground for divorce, fornication (Matthew 19:9). Paul speaks of a believer whose unbelieving spouse departs, and says God hath called us to peace (1 Corinthians 7:15). The Confession names only two grounds for divorce. One is adultery. The other is willful desertion that neither the church nor the government can remedy (WCF 24.5, 24.6). It protects the innocent party. It calls for a public and orderly course, so no one judges his own case. That’s a mercy. You don’t have to decide this alone, and this page won’t decide it for you. Your elders can guide you.

If a door is still open and it’s safe, pray for repentance. With your elders’ help, seek reconciliation, as much as lieth in you (Romans 12:18). If no one is hurting you, but you’re worn out and want out, that weariness is real. Tell God all of it. Then go to your elders before you take any step toward divorce (1 Corinthians 7:10-11). The heart is quick to find reasons, without just cause, to put asunder what God has joined (Matthew 19:6, WCF 24.6).

If Someone Pressures You to Reconcile

You may be urged to go back home quickly, to forgive before the facts are heard, or to prove that you trust your spouse again. Reconciliation requires truth and repentance. A promise that violence or adultery will stop is not proof that it has stopped. It’s right to seek protection and involve civil authorities when crimes have occurred (Romans 13:4).

A lawful separation for safety isn’t itself a divorce. If you’re weighing divorce, the original Westminster Confession names adultery and irreparable willful desertion as grounds (24.5-6). Have faithful elders help you understand and apply those standards without letting anyone rush the process or excuse harm.

If You're Living Apart or Expect to Separate

Deciding where to sleep tonight is not the same question as deciding whether a marriage may be dissolved. If you must leave because of danger, make a plan with an abuse advocate where possible. Do not announce a departure or negotiate privately with a violent spouse if doing so could expose you or your children to further harm. If danger is immediate, seek emergency services.

When it is safe to do so, gather information about housing, essential bills, insurance, identification, and care for children. Keep copies of important documents somewhere safe. Know which bills have deadlines and what income is available. Ask a qualified local lawyer about custody, support, property, protective orders, and deadlines. Laws differ by location and this page cannot decide your rights.

If communication with your spouse is safe and necessary, keep important agreements clear and in writing. If threats or harassment are occurring, ask a qualified advocate or lawyer about safer ways to communicate. You may need practical help with transport, groceries, childcare, and work. Tell a trusted church deacon what needs to be done, without expecting one person to solve the whole situation.

When Your Spouse Won't Work on the Marriage

You can't repent for another person. You can speak truthfully, acknowledge your own wrongs, seek counsel, and invite a safe conversation. You cannot force your husband or wife to listen or to become faithful. Endless pleading may leave you more exhausted without changing anything.

If the spouse has left, ask trustworthy elders for help understanding what has happened and what responsibilities remain. The Confession treats irreparable willful desertion as a grave matter requiring an orderly judgment (WCF 24.6). It doesn't authorize an automatic verdict from a few unanswered calls. Gather the facts without stalking, threatening, or trying to control the other person. Speak with a qualified lawyer when practical or legal obligations are disputed.

Bring your sorrow to God as you seek wise help from people who know the circumstances. You can be faithful in your own duties without claiming control of the other person's heart.

If You're Considering Reconciliation

Reconciliation is not proved by an apology delivered under pressure or by a short period without another incident. Look for truthful acknowledgment of the wrong, an end to the harmful behavior, a willingness to accept consequences, and a pattern of change over time. Someone who has repented should not demand a timetable for the other person's trust to return.

For ordinary conflict, a careful pastoral conversation or marriage counselor may help both people listen and make agreements. Where there has been coercive control or abuse, joint counseling may be unsafe. The National Domestic Violence Hotline advises against couples counseling with an abusive partner. Seek independent help and a safety plan first. Church leaders must not require a victim to meet privately with an abuser as proof of forgiveness.

If the marriage is being rebuilt, consider the actual matters that caused harm. How will finances be handled honestly? What help will be used when anger rises? What contact or transparency is appropriate after betrayal? Agree on realistic steps and get outside help where needed. Some marriages can be repaired. This page cannot promise that yours will be, and it cannot make your spouse's safety depend on that hope.

If you're the one who broke faith

After David took another man’s wife and had the man killed, he wrote this.

The sacrifices of God are a broken spirit: a broken and a contrite heart, O God, thou wilt not despise.

Psalm 51:17 (KJV)

A contrite heart is a crushed one. When Nathan confronted him, David said, I've sinned against the Lord. And Nathan said, The Lord also hath put away thy sin (2 Samuel 12:13). Christ died for sins like yours, and rose again.

Tonight, tell God all of it, as David did. If you turn from your sin to Christ and confess it, God’s pardon is full at once. It rests on Christ’s blood, not on how well you confess (WCF 15.3).

Your wife’s or husband’s trust is another matter. It comes back slowly, if at all, and it’s theirs to give. You can’t make them trust you, or make them stay. Never use God’s forgiveness as a lever. Let your pastor or elders help you confess to your spouse without excuse (WCF 15.6). Bear the consequences. If your sin has made them afraid of you, respect the distance they ask for. There’s more on forsaking this sin and fighting it under I have been unfaithful.

Don't call a confession complete while hiding ongoing contact, secret accounts, threats, or other conduct that caused the harm. Stop the wrongdoing and seek appropriate accountability. If you have been violent or coercive, seek specialist help for abusive behavior and accept legal or protective restrictions. Do not use a counselor or pastor to persuade your spouse to return or to overlook their safety. Repentance concerns what you do, whether or not reconciliation follows.

If shame has brought thoughts of suicide, tell someone safe near you and read the immediate help for someone thinking of ending their life. In the United States, call or text 988 for crisis support. If your sin feels too great to forgive, read What I did is too great to forgive.

If it's your parents

It isn’t your fault. Nothing you said or did caused this, and it isn’t your job to fix it. You may keep loving both of your parents. You never have to carry messages between them, or pick a side.

Tell an adult you trust how you’re really doing. That could be a parent, a teacher, a school counselor, or your pastor. If one adult doesn't listen or you aren't safe with them, tell another. You may need someone to help with transport, school arrangements, or questions about where you'll live. Those are adult responsibilities, not problems you have to solve.

David once wrote, When my father and my mother forsake me, then the Lord will take me up (Psalm 27:10). To take up is to take in, the way a family takes in a child who has nowhere else to go. David meant that even if his own parents failed him, God wouldn’t. If your parents’ divorce feels like that, and you trust him, this is God’s word to you. It isn’t a verdict on your parents.

Jesus was left alone too. The night he was arrested, all his friends ran away. He died for sinners and rose again. He never leaves anyone who trusts him. You can tell him exactly how you feel tonight, even if you’re angry. If anyone at home is hurting you, get safe first. If you ever think of ending your life, tell a safe adult immediately and read how to get immediate help. In the United States you can call or text 988.

Counsel Should Hear the Facts

An elder helping with a troubled marriage should listen carefully before reaching conclusions. Ordinary quarrels, adultery, desertion, and violence call for different responses. Someone who has committed a wrong must be called to repentance, while the person harmed shouldn’t be pressured to share that blame.

If children are involved, keep them out of adult arguments and let them speak with a trusted adult about what is happening. Don't ask a child to deliver accusations, choose a parent, or keep adult secrets. Give age-appropriate, truthful explanations about changes to their routine without sharing graphic or intimate details. If you’re the one who has done wrong, confess it without demanding immediate trust or reconciliation.

Elders should distinguish ordinary conflict from wrongdoing that needs discipline and from conduct requiring civil protection. A spouse should be heard without being forced into a joint meeting with the person who has threatened them. The church can provide pastoral counsel and practical care, but it must not substitute its own internal process for the criminal justice system or a qualified lawyer. Ask for help from another trustworthy pastor when those involved cannot be impartial.
