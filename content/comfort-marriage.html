David knew this wound.

For it was not an enemy that reproached me; then I could have borne it: neither was it he that hated me that did magnify himself against me; then I would have hid myself from him: But it was thou, a man mine equal, my guide, and mine acquaintance. We took sweet counsel together, and walked unto the house of God in company.

Psalm 55:12-14 (KJV)

Of this friend David says, he hath broken his covenant (55:20).

On the night he was betrayed, Jesus said a psalm of David was being fulfilled in a friend who ate his bread and turned on him (John 13:18, Psalm 41:9). In the garden they all forsook him, and fled (Mark 14:50). He knows the pain of being forsaken by those closest to him.

You may grieve this. It’s a real loss, and there’s no funeral for it. If the children are gone from the house too, that’s a second loss. God himself speaks to this grief.

For thy Maker is thine husband; the Lord of hosts is his name; and thy Redeemer the Holy One of Israel; The God of the whole earth shall he be called. For the Lord hath called thee as a woman forsaken and grieved in spirit, and a wife of youth, when thou wast refused, saith thy God.

Isaiah 54:5-6 (KJV)

God pictures himself as a husband calling home a wife who was refused, which means cast off. It’s a figure, and the faithfulness it pictures is real. If you belong to his people, he won’t forsake you. He has said, I will never leave thee, nor forsake thee (Hebrews 13:5).

This is what God promises everyone who is justified by faith in Christ.

All those that are justified God vouchsafeth, in and for his only Son Jesus Christ, to make partakers of the grace of adoption; by which they are taken into the number, and enjoy the liberties and privileges of the children of God; have his name put upon them; receive the Spirit of adoption; have access to the throne of grace with boldness; are enabled to cry, Abba, Father; are pitied, protected, provided for, and chastened by him as by a father; yet never cast off, but sealed to the day of redemption, and inherit the promises, as heirs of everlasting salvation.

Westminster Confession of Faith 12.1

Vouchsafeth means graciously grants. Pitied, protected, provided for, even chastened as by a father, yet never cast off. A husband or wife can cast you off. If you’re his, your Father never will.

Scripture calls the church the bride of Christ. That’s a picture, and the union it pictures is real and can’t be broken (Larger Catechism Q&A 66). He loved the church, and gave himself for it (Ephesians 5:25). He died in the place of sinners. On the third day he rose, and now he lives to pray for all who come to God by him (Hebrews 7:25). He has never broken faith with his bride, and he never will.

You may need help sorting what has happened before you can decide what to do. A trusted elder can hear the matter carefully, while urgent questions about violence belong first to safety and the civil authorities.

In the days ahead, leave the wrong with God, as Christ committed himself to him that judgeth righteously (1 Peter 2:23). Scripture forbids revenge (Romans 12:17-19). It doesn’t forbid getting safe, or asking for justice. For your own sake, don’t send a message tonight that you’ll regret tomorrow. Keep the children out of the conflict, and never make them carry messages. There’s a word for them below.

What God has said about divorce

If you’re afraid at home, getting safe comes first, and all of this can wait. Leaving to be safe isn’t divorce, and it needs none of the grounds below.

Someone may have told you that God hates divorce, as if it were a word against you. In Malachi God says he hateth putting away, and in the same breath he condemns violence. He was rebuking husbands who dealt treacherously against the wife of their youth (Malachi 2:14-16). It’s a word against the one who breaks faith, not the one who was put away or left.

Jesus named one ground for divorce, fornication (Matthew 19:9). Paul speaks of a believer whose unbelieving spouse departs, and says God hath called us to peace (1 Corinthians 7:15). The Confession names only two grounds for divorce. One is adultery. The other is willful desertion that neither the church nor the government can remedy (WCF 24.5, 24.6). It protects the innocent party. It calls for a public and orderly course, so no one judges his own case. That’s a mercy. You don’t have to decide this alone, and this page won’t decide it for you. Your elders can guide you.

If a door is still open and it’s safe, pray for repentance. With your elders’ help, seek reconciliation, as much as lieth in you (Romans 12:18). If no one is hurting you, but you’re worn out and want out, that weariness is real. Tell God all of it. Then go to your elders before you take any step toward divorce (1 Corinthians 7:10-11). The heart is quick to find reasons, without just cause, to put asunder what God has joined (Matthew 19:6, WCF 24.6).

If Someone Pressures You to Reconcile

You may be urged to go back home quickly, to forgive before the facts are heard, or to prove that you trust your spouse again. Reconciliation requires truth and repentance. A promise that violence or adultery will stop is not proof that it has stopped. It’s right to seek protection and involve civil authorities when crimes have occurred (Romans 13:4).

A lawful separation for safety isn’t itself a divorce. If you’re weighing divorce, the original Westminster Confession names adultery and irreparable willful desertion as grounds (24.5-6). Have faithful elders help you understand and apply those standards without letting anyone rush the process or excuse harm.

If you're the one who broke faith

After David took another man’s wife and had the man killed, he wrote this.

The sacrifices of God are a broken spirit: a broken and a contrite heart, O God, thou wilt not despise.

Psalm 51:17 (KJV)

A contrite heart is a crushed one. When Nathan confronted him, David said, I've sinned against the Lord. And Nathan said, The Lord also hath put away thy sin (2 Samuel 12:13). Christ died for sins like yours, and rose again.

Tonight, tell God all of it, as David did. If you turn from your sin to Christ and confess it, God’s pardon is full at once. It rests on Christ’s blood, not on how well you confess (WCF 15.3).

Your wife’s or husband’s trust is another matter. It comes back slowly, if at all, and it’s theirs to give. You can’t make them trust you, or make them stay. Never use God’s forgiveness as a lever. Let your pastor or elders help you confess to your spouse without excuse (WCF 15.6). Bear the consequences. If your sin has made them afraid of you, give them the distance they ask for. If it's your parents

It isn’t your fault. Nothing you said or did caused this, and it isn’t your job to fix it. You may keep loving both of your parents. You never have to carry messages between them, or pick a side.

Tell an adult you trust how you’re really doing. That could be a parent, a teacher, a school counselor, or your pastor.

David once wrote, When my father and my mother forsake me, then the Lord will take me up (Psalm 27:10). To take up is to take in, the way a family takes in a child who has nowhere else to go. David meant that even if his own parents failed him, God wouldn’t. If your parents’ divorce feels like that, and you trust him, this is God’s word to you. It isn’t a verdict on your parents.

Jesus was left alone too. The night he was arrested, all his friends ran away. He died for sinners and rose again. He never leaves anyone who trusts him. Counsel Should Hear the Facts

An elder helping with a troubled marriage should listen carefully before reaching conclusions. Ordinary quarrels, adultery, desertion, and violence call for different responses. Someone who has committed a wrong must be called to repentance, while the person harmed shouldn’t be pressured to share that blame.

If children are involved, keep them out of adult arguments and let them speak with a trusted adult about what is happening. If you’re the one who has done wrong, confess it without demanding immediate trust or reconciliation.
