Perhaps your spouse needs help getting out of bed, a parent is forgetting things, or a child has appointments you can barely keep track of. You might love the person dearly and still be exhausted. Caring for someone is work. It can change your sleep, your income, your time with other family members, and even the way you talk to the person you love.

If you cannot safely meet someone's care needs, tell their medical team promptly. If their health is suddenly deteriorating or there is immediate danger, seek urgent medical help. It's right to ask for more support before a crisis forces a decision.

Make the Daily Care More Manageable

Write down the care that actually has to be done. Include medication instructions, appointments, meals, mobility needs, supervision, and who can make medical decisions when needed. Keep the contact details of the care team in one place. Check instructions directly with the relevant clinician rather than guessing about a medicine or procedure.

Ask family or friends for a particular task rather than saying you need help in general. Someone might bring a meal every Thursday, drive to an appointment, or sit with the person you care for while you shop. If family members live far away, they may still be able to coordinate calls or help with paperwork.

When You Need Rest or Respite

It isn't selfish to need sleep or time away from constant responsibility. If you cannot leave the person alone, ask their care team or a local aging or disability service about respite care. It may be provided at home, through adult day services, or in a care facility. Eligibility, costs, and availability vary. Ask what care will be provided and whether the person is safe with the arrangement.

If you find yourself becoming angry, numb, or unable to manage ordinary tasks, tell a trusted person and speak with your own health professional. If you fear you may hurt the person you care for, arrange for another safe adult or appropriate service to take over their immediate care and seek help at once. Needing relief isn't a reason to hide what is happening.

The National Institute on Aging explains respite and support services. For older adults in the United States, its caregiving guide includes ways to find local help.

If Memory or Judgment Is Changing

Dementia and other cognitive difficulties can affect safety and daily judgment. Ask a clinician about changes in memory, medication management, wandering, driving, or behavior. Avoid making a diagnosis yourself. A care professional can help assess needs and explain available supports.

Speak respectfully to the person receiving care. Include them in decisions they can still make. If they require supervision, arrange it without leaving every hour of care on one exhausted relative. Ask the care team about planning ahead for legal and medical decisions. Relevant laws and paperwork differ by location, so use qualified guidance.

What the Church Can Do

Scripture calls believers to bear one another's burdens (Galatians 6:2). In ordinary terms that may mean meals, transport, practical repairs, someone to sit with a sick member, or an elder visiting a homebound family. Deacons can help a congregation respond to material needs. Ask specifically for what would make tomorrow possible.

Christ cared for his mother even as he was dying (John 19:26-27). That gives us a clear example of attention to another person's real needs. Your service matters, but you aren't able to be everywhere and do everything. A faithful church should help rather than praise you for quietly becoming exhausted.

For the patient or family member's own questions about illness and dying, see Serious Illness. For decisions about medical treatment near the end of life, ask clinicians to explain the aims, likely benefits, and burdens of care. Elders can help with biblical moral questions, but should not replace professional medical advice.
