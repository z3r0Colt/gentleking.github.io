But I trusted in thee, O Lord: I said, Thou art my God. My times are in thy hand:

Psalm 31:14-15 (KJV)

The day of the diagnosis is in his hand, and every day after it.

The night before he died, in the garden of Gethsemane, Jesus told his friends, My soul is exceeding sorrowful unto death (Mark 14:34). Then he fell on the ground and prayed.

And he said, Abba, Father, all things are possible unto thee; take away this cup from me: nevertheless not what I will, but what thou wilt.

Mark 14:36 (KJV)

In Scripture a cup often pictures the portion God hands to someone. Jesus asked for his to pass, and he asked more than once (14:39). So you may ask for healing, and keep asking. And he bowed to his Father’s will. Faith doesn’t pretend the cup is sweet. It asks, and then it trusts the Father who holds it.

He asked to be spared, and he wasn’t spared. The cup he dreaded was God’s fury against sin (Isaiah 51:17). Jesus drank all of it on the cross, and on the third day he rose. If you’re his, your cup may be bitter, but it holds no condemning wrath.

Paul was given something that tormented him (2 Corinthians 12:7).

For this thing I besought the Lord thrice, that it might depart from me. And he said unto me, My grace is sufficient for thee: for my strength is made perfect in weakness.

2 Corinthians 12:8-9 (KJV)

The answer was grace, and it was enough. Someone may tell you that with enough faith you would be healed. Remember that Paul left his friend Trophimus sick at Miletum (2 Timothy 4:20). Being sick isn’t a sign that your faith has failed.

Jesus prayed to his Father, and that Father rules over all of this.

God, the great Creator of all things, doth uphold, direct, dispose, and govern all creatures, actions, and things, from…8044 tokens truncated…d, The Lord also hath put away thy sin (2 Samuel 12:13). Christ died for sins like yours, and rose again.

Tonight, tell God all of it, as David did. If you turn from your sin to Christ and confess it, God’s pardon is full at once. It rests on Christ’s blood, not on how well you confess (WCF 15.3).

Your wife’s or husband’s trust is another matter. It comes back slowly, if at all, and it’s theirs to give. You can’t make them trust you, or make them stay. Never use God’s forgiveness as a lever. Let your pastor or elders help you confess to your spouse without excuse (WCF 15.6). Bear the consequences. If your sin has made them afraid of you, respect the distance they ask for. There’s more on forsaking this sin and fighting it under I have been unfaithful.

Don't call a confession complete while hiding ongoing contact, secret accounts, threats, or other conduct that caused the harm. Stop the wrongdoing and seek appropriate accountability. If you have been violent or coercive, seek specialist help for abusive behavior and accept legal or protective restrictions. Do not use a counselor or pastor to persuade your spouse to return or to overlook their safety. Repentance concerns what you do, whether or not reconciliation follows.

If shame has brought thoughts of suicide, tell someone safe near you and read the immediate help for someone thinking of ending their life. In the United States, call or text 988 for crisis support. If your sin feels too great to forgive, read What I did is too great to forgive.

If it's your parents

It isn’t your fault. Nothing you said or did caused this, and it isn’t your job to fix it. You may keep loving both of your parents. You never have to carry messages between them, or pick a side.

Tell an adult you trust how you’re really doing. That could be a parent, a teacher, a school counselor, or your pastor. If one adult doesn't listen or you aren't safe with them, tell another. You may need someone to help with transport, school arrangements, or questions about where you'll live. Those are adult responsibilities, not problems you have to solve.

David once wrote, When my father and my mother forsake me, then the Lord will take me up (Psalm 27:10). To take up is to take in, the way a family takes in a child who has nowhere else to go. David meant that even if his own parents failed him, God wouldn’t. If your parents’ divorce feels like that, and you trust him, this is God’s word to you. It isn’t a verdict on your parents.

Jesus was left alone too. The night he was arrested, all his friends ran away. He died for sinners and rose again. He never leaves anyone who trusts him. You can tell him exactly how you feel tonight, even if you’re angry. If anyone at home is hurting you, get safe first. If you ever think of ending your life, tell a safe adult immediately and read how to get immediate help. In the United States you can call or text 988.

Counsel Should Hear the Facts

An elder helping with a troubled marriage should listen carefully before reaching conclusions. Ordinary quarrels, adultery, desertion, and violence call for different responses. Someone who has committed a wrong must be called to repentance, while the person harmed shouldn’t be pressured to share that blame.

If children are involved, keep them out of adult arguments and let them speak with a trusted adult about what is happening. Don't ask a child to deliver accusations, choose a parent, or keep adult secrets. Give age-appropriate, truthful explanations about changes to their routine without sharing graphic or intimate details. If you’re the one who has done wrong, confess it without demanding immediate trust or reconciliation.

Elders should distinguish ordinary conflict from wrongdoing that needs discipline and from conduct requiring civil protection. A spouse should be heard without being forced into a joint meeting with the person who has threatened them. The church can provide pastoral counsel and practical care, but it must not substitute its own internal process for the criminal justice system or a qualified lawyer. Ask for help from another trustworthy pastor when those involved cannot be impartial.
