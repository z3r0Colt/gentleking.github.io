If you lost a baby, before birth or soon after, or a little child, read the part for a little one. If your baby died before birth, in a miscarriage or a stillbirth, the part after it is yours too. If your son or daughter was older, read the part for an older child.

If you lost a baby or a little child

People brought young children to Jesus, some of them infants (Luke 18:15). When his disciples rebuked those who brought them, Jesus was much displeased, and said, Suffer the little children to come unto me, and forbid them not (Mark 10:13-14). Suffer here means allow.

And he took them up in his arms, put his hands upon them, and blessed them.

Mark 10:16 (KJV)

That's his heart toward little ones. Those hands were later nailed to a cross for sinners, and on the third day he rose (John 20:25). His heart hasn’t changed.

David said to God, Thou hast covered me in my mother’s womb (Psalm 139:13).

Thine eyes did see my substance, yet being unperfect; and in thy book all my members were written, which in continuance were fashioned, when as yet there was none of them.

Psalm 139:16 (KJV)

Unperfect means not yet fully formed. David speaks of God knowing him in the womb before his body was complete. Your child’s life was known to God, however early the loss came.

When King David’s baby son died, David rose from the ground and worshipped (2 Samuel 12:20). Then he told his servants why.

And he said, While the child was yet alive, I fasted and wept: for I said, Who can tell whether God will be gracious to me, that the child may live? But now he is dead, wherefore should I fast? can I bring him back again? I shall go to him, but he shall not return to me.

2 Samuel 12:22-23 (KJV)

David stopped fasting because the time for asking was over. The time for grief wasn’t over. He spoke of his son with hope, and then he went on living. He ate that same day, and he comforted his wife (2 Samuel 12:20, 24). You may weep for your little one.

Earlier in that chapter the prophet Nathan told David that this child would die because of David’s sin. In the same breath he told him, The Lord also hath put away thy sin (2 Samuel 12:13-14). No prophet has told you that your child died for your sin, and God hasn’t said it. Without a word from God, no one may trace a sorrow to a particular sin (John 9:3, Deuteronomy 29:29).

Many mothers blame their own bodies. A body that couldn’t carry a child isn’t a sin. If you’re in Christ, there’s no condemnation for you (Romans 8:1), and your baby’s death isn’t a sentence on you. For a believer even a Father’s chastening is never payment for sin, because Christ paid it all.

A little one can’t understand a sermon or put faith into words. That’s no barrier to the Spirit of Christ, who works when, and where, and how he pleases (Westminster Confession 10.3). Grace doesn’t wait for anyone to be old enough.

The original Westminster Confession teaches that elect infants dying in infancy are regenerated and saved by Christ through the Spirit (WCF 10.3). It doesn’t tell us which particular infants are elect. David’s words about going to his son (2 Samuel 12:23) don’t establish a promise concerning every child who dies. You can entrust your child to the Judge of all the earth (Genesis 18:25), whose righteousness and mercy are sure.

If your baby died before birth, the next part is for you. If not, go on to the part for every grieving parent.

If your baby died before birth

A miscarriage or a stillbirth is the death of a child. Some people around you may not see it that way. They may say nothing at all. Or they may say it was for the best, or that you can try again. They usually mean well, but a child isn’t replaced by another child. Scripture counts the little one you carried as a person, known by God and made in his image (Jeremiah 1:5, Luke 1:41). You aren’t making too much of this. You lost a son or a daughter.

After a miscarriage, a mother may wonder whether something she did caused it. Many losses happen without anything a parent could have prevented. Your doctor can help you understand what is known about your own loss. Scripture gives no warrant to treat it as punishment for a hidden fault (John 9:3).

Fathers grieve this too, though often more quietly and later. A father may feel he has to be strong for his wife and so never mourn at all. Husbands, weep with your wives, and let your church care for you both.

If the loss came early, there may be nothing to bury, and no one outside your home may have known. That can make the grief lonelier. You may still mark it. Give your baby a name, tell your pastor, and pray together as a family. Due dates and the day of the loss may be hard for years, and that isn’t a weakness of faith.

If you’re afraid to hope for another child, or if another child never comes, bring that fear to the God who gives and takes away (Job 1:21). He isn’t cruel, and he isn’t careless with you.

If in the past you chose to end a pregnancy, you may grieve that child too. A doctor’s care after your baby had already died isn’t that choice. For that choice there's full pardon in Christ, for all who come to him. Read If You Have Had an Abortion and What I did is too great to forgive.

The next part is for parents of an older child. The part for every grieving parent is for you too.

If your son or daughter was older

Jesus once met a funeral at the gate of a town called Nain. The dead man was the only son of his mother, and she was a widow (Luke 7:12).

And when the Lord saw her, he had compassion on her, and said unto her, Weep not. And he came and touched the bier: and they that bare him stood still. And he said, Young man, I say unto thee, Arise. And he that was dead sat up, and began to speak. And he delivered him to his mother.

Luke 7:13-15 (KJV)

A bier was the open frame the dead were carried on. Jesus had compassion on her before she asked him for anything. He could tell her not to weep, because he was about to give her son back. He wasn’t rebuking her tears, or yours.

He didn’t raise every dead son or daughter in Israel. The widow’s son at Nain, and the girl of twelve he took by the hand (Mark 5:41-42), were signs. They pointed ahead to the last day, when he will call his people out of their graves. They weren’t promises for today.

When a great wind brought a house down on Job’s children, Job tore his robe, fell down on the ground, and worshipped. In all this Job sinned not (Job 1:18-22). Grief and worship stand on the same ground.

If your son or daughter trusted Christ, no man can pluck them out of his hand (John 10:28-29). Death couldn’t do it either (Romans 8:38-39).

The souls of believers are at their death made perfect in holiness, and do immediately pass into glory; and their bodies, being still united to Christ, do rest in their graves till the resurrection.

Westminster Shorter Catechism Q&A 37

If they're his, they’re with him now, and every sin they ever fought is gone.

If you don’t know where your child stood with God, that’s too heavy to carry alone tonight. You aren’t asked to settle where they are. Leave them with the Judge of all the earth, who will do right (Genesis 18:25), and bring your own soul to Christ. Read When You Fear They Did Not Know Christ, and tell your pastor this week. If your son or daughter took their own life, that sorrow has its own place here.

If someone else took your child’s life, by carelessness or by violence, your anger at that wrong isn’t sin in itself. Scripture says, Be ye angry, and sin not (Ephesians 4:26). God says, Vengeance is mine, and he promises to repay (Romans 12:19). He gave the ruler the sword for this very wrong (Romans 13:4), so pressing for justice in the courts isn’t revenge.

For every grieving parent

Many parents replay every moment, what they ate or lifted, the last night, the keys they handed over, the sign they missed.

Like as a father pitieth his children, so the Lord pitieth them that fear him. For he knoweth our frame; he remembereth that we are dust.

Psalm 103:13-14 (KJV)

You couldn’t see what only God could see.

Moses told God’s people that underneath are the everlasting arms (Deuteronomy 33:27). Those arms are a picture, since God is a Spirit, and the care they picture is real. But the Son of God took a real body, with real arms. He took up little children in those arms and blessed them. Then he died for sinners, and on the third day he rose.

Now he says he was dead and is alive for evermore, and holds the keys of death (Revelation 1:18). Keys picture his authority over death, and that authority is real. He will lose none of those the Father has given him, and he will raise them up at the last day (John 6:39).

Some parents want to speak of their child often. Others cannot bear it at first. You and your spouse may need different kinds of company. Other children may need patient answers to their questions. Let them speak about their brother or sister.

When You Blame Yourself

After a death, it’s easy to replay the last day and imagine that one different choice would have changed everything. Where you truly had a duty and failed in it, you may confess that sin to the Lord. But a terrible outcome doesn’t prove that you were negligent, and it doesn’t give anyone permission to accuse you without evidence.

Nathan was sent to David with God’s word about a particular child (2 Samuel 12:13-14). No one has received such a word about yours. If your questions are consuming your days, tell an elder or a trusted doctor how much they’re troubling you.

Let Others Help You Remember

A pastor can help you arrange a burial or memorial, including after miscarriage or stillbirth. You may want to name your baby, keep a photograph, or mark the day privately. None of these is a rule for grieving.

If your body is recovering from pregnancy, follow your clinician’s guidance and seek prompt care for heavy bleeding, fever, or other worrying symptoms. Ask someone to help with calls and meals. You can decide what to do with your child’s belongings later.
