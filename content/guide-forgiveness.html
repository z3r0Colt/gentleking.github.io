This page is about forgiving those who wrong you, and seeking forgiveness when you’ve wronged someone. If someone is hurting you, or has abused you, read If You Are Not Safe first. Forgiveness never requires you to go back into danger, and it never requires you to keep a crime from the police. The rest can wait until you’re safe. If forgiveness is being demanded as proof that you must return to a spouse after betrayal or violence, read the counsel about reconciliation.

Forgiven People Forgive

And be ye kind one to another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, even as God for Christ’s sake hath forgiven you.

Ephesians 4:32 (KJV)

Christ taught us to pray, Forgive us our debts, as we forgive our debtors. Then he added a warning, If ye forgive not men their trespasses, neither will your Father forgive your trespasses (Matthew 6:12, 15).

He told a parable to show why (Matthew 18:21-35). A king forgave his servant ten thousand talents, a debt no servant could ever repay. That servant then grabbed a fellow servant by the throat over a hundred pence and threw him into prison. So the king handed him over to the tormentors. Christ told this parable to expose the wickedness of receiving mercy while withholding it from another. Your debt to God is beyond counting, and God forgives those who turn to him through Christ. The parable warns against receiving mercy while refusing to show it. Its comparison of debts does not make abuse or another grave wrong trivial. Jesus ends, So likewise shall my heavenly Father do also unto you, if ye from your hearts forgive not every one his brother their trespasses (18:35).

You don’t earn God’s pardon by forgiving others. Christ alone paid for sin. But a heart that has received mercy learns to show it, and God gives the grace to do so.

In the fifth petition, which is, And forgive us our debts, as we forgive our debtors, we pray that God, for Christ’s sake, would freely pardon all our sins; which we are the rather encouraged to ask, because by his grace we are enabled from the heart to forgive others.

Westminster Shorter Catechism Q&A 105

When You've Wronged Someone

If you remember that thy brother hath ought against thee, Jesus says, leave your gift at the altar, first be reconciled to thy brother, and then come and offer thy gift (Matthew 5:23-24).

  1. Name it plainly. Say what you did and call it sin. I lied to you. I spoke against you behind your back. Not “if I hurt you” or “mistakes were made.” The one who offends his brother is to declare his repentance to those that are offended (WCF 15.6).
  2. Leave off the “but.” “I was wrong, but you provoked me” shifts the blame back to the person you hurt.
  3. Ask, Will you forgive me? Then let them answer in their own time.
  4. Make it right where you can. Under the law a man who wronged his neighbor was to confess, repay what he took, and add unto it the fifth part thereof (Numbers 5:7). Zacchaeus promised to restore fourfold whatever he had taken by false accusation (Luke 19:8). Pay back what you took. Correct the lie you told.

If you’ve been violent at home or made your family afraid, don't press them to forgive you or to let you come back. Read My anger frightens my family. If you broke faith in marriage, read If you are the one who broke faith.

When Someone Has Wronged You

A minor offense can often be passed over without confrontation. The discretion of a man deferreth his anger, and it is his glory to pass over a transgression (Proverbs 19:11), and charity shall cover the multitude of sins (1 Peter 4:8). The careless word and the small slight can be forgiven in your heart and never mentioned again.

Some sins are too serious to pass over, or they keep standing between you. Then Jesus says, go and tell him his fault between thee and him alone (Matthew 18:15). Go gently, to win your brother, not the argument. If he won’t hear, take one or two others, and then tell it to the church (18:16-17). If he repents, forgive him, even seven times in a day (Luke 17:3-4).

That pattern is for ordinary sins between believers. It does not require confronting an abuser privately before reporting abuse or crime. Seek protection and appropriate civil reporting, and tell trustworthy elders or an independent church authority if your own leaders are involved. The church must still address the sin through its lawful discipline. Its process does not replace the civil authority’s responsibility (WCF 30.1-3).

What Forgiveness Is and Isn't

Forgiveness requires putting away revenge and the grudge that treats another person as owing you personal punishment. When someone repents, Christ commands you to forgive him (Luke 17:3-4). The Confession says those offended ought to receive a repentant brother in love (WCF 15.6). Do not keep using a forgiven wrong to humiliate him or refuse the fellowship that repentance calls for. It doesn’t erase the facts, prevent a report, or forbid speaking about what happened when protection, counsel, or justice requires it. Recompense to no man evil for evil, and avenge not yourselves, Paul says. Instead, if thine enemy hunger, feed him (Romans 12:17-21). It isn’t first a feeling. It’s a choice made before God, and you may have to make it again each time the memory comes back.

Forgiveness doesn’t mean these things.

  1. Pretending it didn’t happen. Joseph forgave his brothers, and still told them, ye thought evil against me (Genesis 50:20).
  2. Trusting at once. Trust depends on truthful conduct and safety over time. It may not be restored, even when you put away revenge.
  3. Staying in danger. Never. Getting safe isn’t a failure to forgive.
  4. Keeping a crime from the police. God gave the ruler the sword against evildoers (Romans 13:4). Reporting a crime isn’t revenge.

Reconciliation takes two, and it may take a long time. Paul says, If it be possible, as much as lieth in you, live peaceably with all men (Romans 12:18). Some of it doesn’t lie in you. If the other person won’t repent or make peace, you cannot produce reconciliation alone. You can put away revenge, tell the truth, and keep appropriate boundaries. You don’t have to provide contact that exposes you or others to harm.

When They Never Make It Right

Some people who wronged you will never say they’re sorry. Some have died. Reformed teachers differ on how to speak of this. Some say full forgiveness waits for repentance, for Jesus said, if he repent, forgive him (Luke 17:3). Others stress that we are to forgive whenever we stand praying (Mark 11:25). Both agree on what you must do. Put away bitterness and revenge, and give the wrong to God. Vengeance belongeth unto me, I will recompense, saith the Lord (Hebrews 10:30). He will judge rightly.

Look at Christ. While they crucified him, he prayed, Father, forgive them (Luke 23:34). He committed himself to him that judgeth righteously (1 Peter 2:23). At Pentecost, Peter preached repentance and forgiveness in the city where Christ was crucified, and about three thousand received his word and were baptized (Acts 2:36-41). He died for sinners and rose again. If you’ve never come to him for your own pardon, start there.

Feeling the pain again isn’t the same as refusing to forgive. If memories are overwhelming or you keep reliving what happened, seek qualified care as well as pastoral help. But if you nurse the grudge and want them to suffer, that’s bitterness, and God says to put it away (Ephesians 4:31). A heart that flatly refuses to forgive is the servant in the parable, and it should be afraid. Don’t despair. Bring it to Christ and ask him to make you willing.

What an Ordinary Reconciliation Can Look Like

A friend tells other people something private you entrusted to him. When you raise it, he admits what he did, stops defending it, and asks forgiveness. You should receive his repentance and forgive him. You can also ask him to tell the people involved that he was wrong to share it and to stop passing it on. That correction should reach the actual audience without spreading your private matter still further.

Forgiving him means you won't keep treating the confessed wrong as a debt you can collect through insults, retaliation, or repeated demands for humiliation. It also means being willing to pursue peace and his good. You may still need time before entrusting another sensitive matter to him. Explain that honestly rather than pretending confidence has already returned or using withheld confidence to punish him indefinitely.

If he dismisses the concern and continues spreading the information, the next step changes. You can refuse revenge, pray for his repentance, and seek his good while bringing an unresolved wrong through the appropriate means. Ordinary church disputes may require the help described in Matthew 18. Serious danger and crimes require appropriate protective and civil action. Calling something forgiveness doesn't settle which authority must address it.

When the Memory Still Hurts

Remembering an injury isn't itself proof that you haven't forgiven. A place, a conversation, or an anniversary may bring back grief without your choosing it. Ask what you're doing with that memory. Are you asking God for help to act justly and mercifully, or deliberately rehearsing the injury to nourish hatred and plan repayment?

If you notice yourself using an old forgiven wrong to win an unrelated argument, stop and acknowledge that sin. Address the present disagreement on its own facts. If the earlier conduct is relevant to a current risk or an ongoing pattern, speak truthfully about that connection. You don't have to erase facts needed for protection, church discipline, or a fair decision about responsibility.

Forgiveness and trust involve distinct obligations. Receive a penitent brother rather than holding him permanently outside Christian fellowship. Restore particular responsibilities according to the requirements of that responsibility and the evidence available. A person may be forgiven while remaining unfit to supervise children or handle someone else's finances.

When pain repeatedly overwhelms ordinary life, seek patient pastoral care and appropriate professional help. You can pursue healing and necessary protection while asking God to keep you from revenge. There isn't a biblical deadline by which a serious injury must stop hurting.

When You Need to Forgive

  1. Pray the fifth petition slowly. Ask God to forgive you, and name before him the people you need to forgive. See the Lord’s Prayer as the pattern.
  2. Take responsibility for a wrong you’ve done. Confess without excuses and make restitution where possible. Respect a request for no contact, a protective order, or advice about communicating safely through an appropriate intermediary.
  3. Let one small offense go. You may choose not to pursue a minor slight. This doesn’t require concealing a pattern of harm or refusing needed counsel.
  4. If a serious sin stands between you and a brother, go to him alone and gently. If it involves abuse or a crime, seek protection and appropriate civil reporting without waiting for a private confrontation. The church must address the sin too.
  5. Pray for someone who wronged you. Bless them that curse you, and pray for them which despitefully use you (Luke 6:28).
