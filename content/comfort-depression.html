If you think you're depressed, tell a doctor or qualified mental health professional what has changed in your sleep, appetite, concentration, and daily life. Ask a trusted person to help you arrange the appointment if making the call is difficult. You can also ask your pastor for prayer and care. Depression isn't proof of a particular sin, and seeking treatment is consistent with faith.

In Gethsemane Jesus began to be sorrowful and very heavy, and he said so. My soul is exceeding sorrowful, even unto death (Matthew 26:37-38). He didn’t hide his sorrow, and you don’t need to hide yours. Matthew also tells how he treats the weak.

A bruised reed shall he not break, and smoking flax shall he not quench,

Matthew 12:20 (KJV)

A bruised reed is a cracked stalk, and smoking flax is a wick burned down to smoke. They picture someone nearly spent, and his gentleness to such a person is real. If you belong to him, he won’t snap you or snuff you out.

In Psalm 42 a man with tears for his food day and night speaks to his own soul (42:3).

Why art thou cast down, O my soul? and why art thou disquieted within me? hope thou in God: for I shall yet praise him, who is the health of my countenance, and my God.

Psalm 42:11 (KJV)

Disquieted means troubled, and countenance means face. The question comes three times (42:5, 42:11, 43:5), and each time the answer is hope in God.

Deep sadness isn’t, by itself, evidence of sin or weak faith. Elijah once lay down under a tree and asked God to take his life. The Lord sent him food and allowed him to rest before speaking further to him (1 Kings 19:4-8). That account doesn’t diagnose Elijah with a medical disorder, but it does show God’s care for a weary servant. If depression began after childbirth, tell your doctor promptly. Postpartum mental health problems can require urgent care, especially if you feel unable to stay safe or care for your baby.

Depression can affect your ability to think, pray, work, or enjoy ordinary things. Receiving professional care is consistent with trusting God, who ordinarily works through means (Westminster Confession 5.3). Rest matters, but serious or lasting symptoms may need medical treatment. When God Feels Far addresses spiritual darkness in greater detail.

Your darkness may feel like God’s wrath. The man who wrote Psalm 88 said, Thy wrath lieth hard upon me (88:7), yet he kept crying to God day and night (88:1). On the cross, in the darkness over all the land, Christ bore the weight of God’s wrath for his people (Matthew 27:45, Larger Catechism Q&A 49). If you’re his, no condemning wrath is left for you, however heavy this feels (WCF 20.1).

Christ died and rose again. His resurrection gives believers a hope beyond present suffering. Scripture gives no timetable for recovery from depression. Treatment can help, and you can ask for continuing care when symptoms persist or return. When Christ comes again, he will make his people’s lowly bodies like his glorious body (Philippians 3:21).

Distressing feelings don’t settle whether you belong to Christ. The Confession teaches that believers may lose the sense of God’s favor for a time (WCF 18.4). If you can’t tell, read Am I Really Saved? Come to Christ now. Come to Christ with the question still troubling you.

If you've been keeping the symptoms to yourself, choose one trustworthy person and tell them how your days have been going. Ask them to check on you during treatment, especially on difficult days. If thoughts of suicide appear, see I'm Thinking of Ending My Life for immediate help and a clear way to tell someone.

When Ordinary Tasks Become Too Hard

Depression may leave you staring at dishes, unopened mail, or a message you cannot answer. Begin with the needs that cannot safely wait, such as food, prescribed medicine, caring for children, and essential bills. Ask a safe person for a specific task. They might bring groceries, take you to an appointment, or help you call a doctor. You don't have to explain your whole life to ask for that help.

If you're having trouble caring for children or another dependent, tell someone who can help provide safe care while you receive support. If you can't safely manage your daily responsibilities, tell your health professional how serious it has become. Shame can make people hide the very information their clinician needs.

If you have work or school responsibilities, speak with an appropriate supervisor or counselor about what you can currently manage. You don't have to disclose all your private medical information to every person who asks. Ask a clinician about documentation or support if changes are necessary.

What to Say at a Medical Appointment

You can begin with the plain facts. Tell the clinician how long you have felt this way, whether your sleep or appetite has changed, if you've lost interest in ordinary activities, and which responsibilities have become difficult. Mention medications, substance use, and any thoughts of hurting yourself. You can write these things down or bring a trusted person to help you explain.

Ask what causes need to be assessed and which treatments fit your situation. Depression can be treated with talk therapy, medication, or both, depending on the patient. If care is too expensive or hard to reach, ask the clinic about lower-cost programs, insurance options, or community services. Don't stop or change a prescribed medicine without discussing it with the prescriber.

If an appointment is weeks away and you are getting worse, call the office again and say what has changed. A worsening crisis should not wait for an ordinary appointment. If you are thinking about suicide, tell someone safe now and read the immediate safety guidance. If you might act on it, seek emergency care.

How the Church Can Help

An elder may visit, listen, pray with you, or help you keep contact with the congregation while you are unwell. Deacons and other members may help with rides, meals, or basic needs. Ask for a definite kind of help rather than feeling you must be well enough to attend everything before anyone notices your absence.

Pastoral counsel matters, but it cannot replace clinical assessment or treatment. If someone tells you that taking medicine proves you lack faith, Scripture does not establish that claim. The Lord ordinarily uses means in his providence, and receiving medical care does not put you outside his care.

For related questions about spiritual darkness, read When God Feels Far. For worry and panic, see When Anxiety or Panic Takes Over. These concerns can overlap, but they are not all the same thing.

When Prayer Feels Beyond You

Isaiah speaks to one who fears the Lord yet walks in darkness. His words aren’t a diagnosis of depression. They do teach that God’s people may endure a season when they cannot see the way forward.

Who is among you that feareth the Lord, that obeyeth the voice of his servant, that walketh in darkness, and hath no light? let him trust in the name of the Lord, and stay upon his God.

Isaiah 50:10 (KJV)

If you can read only a few lines, read a psalm. If you cannot pray aloud, ask a member of your church to pray with you. The Spirit helps believers in their weakness (Romans 8:26). A difficult day of prayer doesn’t settle whether you belong to Christ.

Getting Help and Continuing Care

Tell a doctor how long symptoms have lasted, how you’re sleeping, and whether you’ve had thoughts of self-harm. If treatment isn’t helping, arrange a follow-up. Don’t change or stop prescribed medicine without medical advice.

If you cannot get to public worship because of illness, let your elders know and ask them to visit. If you might harm yourself, tell someone safe immediately and use the suicide crisis help page. In immediate danger, call 911.
