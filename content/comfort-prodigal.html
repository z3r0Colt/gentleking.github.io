Your child or grandchild may mock the faith, or deny God under your roof, or be lost to drink or drugs. Jesus told of a son who wasted his substance with riotous living (Luke 15:13).

And he arose, and came to his father. But when he was yet a great way off, his father saw him, and had compassion, and ran, and fell on his neck, and kissed him.

Luke 15:20 (KJV)

The father in this story pictures God, and his welcome to a sinner who comes home is real. Jesus came to seek and to save the lost, and there is joy in heaven over one sinner that repenteth (Luke 19:10, 15:7). He knows what it’s like to be refused by his own (John 1:11). You may grieve this loss, though no one sends flowers for it.

God promised this to his scattered people.

A new heart also will I give you, and a new spirit will I put within you: and I will take away the stony heart out of your flesh, and I will give you an heart of flesh.

Ezekiel 36:26 (KJV)

This is what God does for his people, and only he can do it. It gives you good reason to pray for your child without claiming to know what God has decreed. The stony heart describes a will that God himself must renew.

When God calls those he has chosen, he renews their wills and by his almighty power draws them to Christ. Yet they come most freely, being made willing by his grace (Westminster Confession 10.1). He hasn’t told us who they are, so no one may write your child off. If your child once professed faith, you don’t need to settle tonight whether it was real. The Lord knoweth them that are his (2 Timothy 2:19).

Repentance is God’s gift, and he may yet give it. Ask him. Don’t quarrel with your child, but be gentle and patient (2 Timothy 2:24-25). Keep the door open, and love your child without approving their sin. An open door never means letting danger in.

If your child was baptized, the working of baptism isn’t tied to the moment it was done. The Holy Ghost gives its grace to those it belongs to, as God wills, in his appointed time (Westminster Confession 28.6). Baptism is a sign and seal to pray on. It isn’t a guarantee.

Some read Proverbs 22:6, Train up a child in the way he should go, as a promise that your child must come back, or as proof that you failed. It’s neither, and God hasn’t broken his word. A proverb tells how things usually go, not what must happen every time. God said of Israel, I have nourished and brought up children, and they have rebelled against me (Isaiah 1:2). Their rebellion wasn’t his failure.

Where you sinned against your child, by harsh words or neglect, confess it to God, and to your child if you can. But don’t take their sin on yourself. God says a parent doesn’t bear the iniquity of a child (Ezekiel 18:20).

Christ suffered once for sins, the just for the unjust, to bring us to God. He died, and on the third day he rose again (1 Peter 3:18, 1 Corinthians 15:4). If you’re his, your sins were laid on him and are forgiven. He is able to bring your child to God too.

Pray for your child by name and speak with them when you can without financing what harms them. If you and your spouse disagree about boundaries, seek counsel together. Changing a heart belongs to the Lord.

Love Without Feeding the Sin

You can love an unbelieving adult child and still refuse threats, theft, or money that would feed an addiction. A meal or a conversation doesn’t require approval of their sin. If others at home are unsafe, protect them first. Seek appropriate help for addiction or violence.

The father in Luke 15 welcomed his returning son. This parable teaches God’s mercy toward repentant sinners. It doesn’t promise that every child will return. Pray for your own child by name. Only the Spirit can give a new heart (Westminster Confession 10.1).

Praying Without Predicting

God can save a son or daughter after years of rebellion. He hasn’t promised every parent that every child will repent. Pray with hope in his power, speak truth when you can, and protect those under your care.
