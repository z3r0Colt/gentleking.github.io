Set Sunday apart for public and private worship and rest from ordinary work and recreation. Prepare ahead where you can, and continue necessary care for people who depend on you. Necessity and mercy are part of keeping the day, not failures to keep it.

Why It Matters

God set apart one whole day in seven as holy to himself. From the creation to the resurrection of Christ it was the last day of the week. Since the resurrection it has been the first day, the Lord’s Day, the Christian Sabbath (WCF 21.7, Acts 20:7, Revelation 1:10).

Christ said the Sabbath was made for man (Mark 2:27). God gives us a whole day set apart for worship, rest from ordinary labor, and fellowship with his people.

If thou turn away thy foot from the sabbath, from doing thy pleasure on my holy day; and call the sabbath a delight, the holy of the Lord, honourable; and shalt honour him, not doing thine own ways, nor finding thine own pleasure, nor speaking thine own words:

Isaiah 58:13 (KJV)

How to Keep It

The Shorter Catechism says the day is kept by a holy resting all that day, even from such worldly employments and recreations as are lawful on other days, and by spending the whole time in the public and private exercises of God’s worship, except so much as is to be taken up in the works of necessity and mercy (Q&A 60).

  1. Prepare on Saturday. Plan meals, travel, and necessary household tasks ahead where possible. Share the preparation so one person does not carry it all. Aim for enough sleep to attend to worship. The Larger Catechism calls for this kind of foresight (Q&A 117).
  2. Worship with your church. Be at the public worship your congregation holds that day. If it meets morning and evening, plan to attend both as you are able. If illness, disability, transport, or dependent care prevents attendance, tell your elders what help is needed. Ask about visits and ways to hear the preaching while you are unable to gather. Public worship is the center of the day (Hebrews 10:25, WCF 21.6).
  3. Rest from your work. Leave ordinary business and avoidable errands for another day. Essential care and urgent protective action continue.
  4. Rest from your entertainments too. Lawful recreation has its place on other days. Give this day to the worship and rest God appoints.
  5. Fill the rest of the day. Read, pray, talk about the sermon at the table, sing psalms, visit the sick, show hospitality, and rest. See How to Lead Family Worship.

Works of Necessity and Mercy

Jesus healed on the Sabbath and defended his disciples for picking grain when they were hungry (Matthew 12:1-13). Some things must be done on any day, and some ought to be.

  1. Necessity. Feeding your family, caring for dependents and animals, and urgent work that cannot wait. Healthcare and emergency services can include such duties, but an occupation’s name does not make every task necessary.
  2. Mercy. Caring for the sick, helping someone in trouble, visiting the lonely.

Your job may require Sunday work that isn’t a work of necessity or mercy. Seek an arrangement that lets you keep the day holy. Discuss the actual duties and possible changes with your employer and elders. An employer’s demand doesn’t by itself make ordinary work necessary. If losing the job would put food, housing, or dependents at risk, ask for practical help while seeking a lawful arrangement. This page cannot settle every employment situation, and church counsel does not replace local advice about workplace rights.

Plan for the Whole Day

The Christian Sabbath is a whole day set apart to God, not only the time occupied by a service. If you arrange nothing beyond getting to church, the rest of the day can easily become ordinary work and entertainment. Decide beforehand how your household can attend worship, eat, rest, and make room for Scripture, prayer, psalm singing, and fellowship.

Preparation should be shared. A husband shouldn't call the day restful while leaving his wife an avoidable burden of cooking and clearing up. Older children can help with clothes, meals, or younger siblings according to their ability. A simple meal and a manageable invitation may serve the day better than an elaborate gathering that exhausts the person preparing it.

Hospitality, visiting a lonely member, and helping someone reach worship can express the day's purpose. Ask what would actually help the person. A visit should allow the sick to receive needed rest and care. The point is to serve God and your neighbor, rather than fill every hour with activity so you can prove that you kept the day.

When you return from public worship, choose one part of the sermon to discuss. Let a child explain something he understood. Ask how the passage corrects or encourages the household. That carries the preached Word into ordinary conversation without requiring everyone to reproduce the sermon from memory.

Judge Difficult Duties Honestly

Christ's disputes about the Sabbath expose both neglect of mercy and man-made restrictions (Matthew 12:1-13). A necessary act of care doesn't profane the day. His teaching also gives no permission to call every convenient activity necessary. Ask what need must be met now, who will bear the cost of delay, and whether reasonable preparation would have avoided the problem.

Feeding an infant and helping an injured neighbor cannot be postponed to preserve an uninterrupted devotional schedule. A routine business task that you preferred not to finish earlier is another matter. Where your own poor planning created avoidable work, meet any immediate duty you now owe and repent of the carelessness. Don't use a neighbor's need as an opportunity to lecture him while refusing help.

In a household caring for a disabled or seriously ill person, some ordinary tasks remain necessary every Sunday. Share them where possible and ask the congregation for practical assistance. Arrange opportunities for carers to hear the Word and receive pastoral care. Caregiving doesn't make public worship unimportant, and the duty of worship doesn't require abandoning the person who depends on you.

If your conscience is troubled about a recurring activity, describe it specifically to your elders. Give the circumstances, possible alternatives, and duties involved. An internet list cannot decide every household's circumstances. The guiding rule remains God's appointment of the whole day for worship and holy rest, with the duties of necessity and mercy he himself permits.

With Children

Make the day a delight, not just a list of things they can’t do. Help them take part in worship according to age and ability. Arrange needed feeding, medicine, rest, or accessibility support. Talk about something they understood from the sermon on the way home. Read Scripture together, sing a psalm, and share a meal where you can talk about what you heard in worship. Let them see that you love it.
