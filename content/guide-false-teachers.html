Christ Warned You

Beware of false prophets, which come to you in sheep’s clothing, but inwardly they are ravening wolves. Ye shall know them by their fruits.

Matthew 7:15-16 (KJV)

Jesus, Paul, Peter, John, and Jude all warned that false teachers would come from inside the church, not only from outside it (Acts 20:29-30, 2 Peter 2:1, 1 John 2:19, Jude 4). They rarely announce themselves. They're often likable, gifted, and popular. So Scripture tells you to test them, not to trust them because they’re famous or kind.

How to Test a Teacher

Do what the Bereans did. They received the word with all readiness of mind, and searched the scriptures daily, whether those things were so (Acts 17:11). Then ask these questions.

  1. What does he say about Christ? Is Jesus fully God and fully man, the only Savior, crucified for sinners and bodily raised? A man who teaches otherwise, and holds to it when he is shown the truth, isn’t a confused brother. He is a false teacher (1 John 4:1-3, 2 John 9-10). Someone who was simply taught wrong and gladly takes correction is another case. Teach him patiently, as Aquila and Priscilla taught Apollos the way of God more perfectly (Acts 18:26).
  2. What does he say about the gospel? Is a sinner saved by grace alone, through faith alone, in Christ alone? Anyone who adds works, rituals, or a church’s merit to Christ preaches another gospel (Galatians 1:6-9).
  3. What is his final authority? Scripture alone, or Scripture plus his visions, his prophecies, or his church’s tradition? God has finished speaking in his written Word. Nothing is to be added to it by new revelations of the Spirit or traditions of men (WCF 1.6).
  4. Does he handle the Bible honestly? Does he read verses in their setting, or pull them out to prove what he already wanted to say? The unlearned and unstable wrest the scriptures unto their own destruction (2 Peter 3:16).
  5. Does he call sinners to repent? A teacher who never names sin, never warns of judgment, and never calls for repentance isn’t preaching the gospel Christ preached (Mark 1:15, 2 Timothy 4:3-4).
  6. How does he live? Look at his fruit. Greed, sexual sin, pride, and love of money are marks Scripture names (2 Peter 2:2-3, 10, 14, 18, Titus 1:11).
  7. Who holds him to account? Is he under the oversight of a church and its elders, or does he answer to no one?

Common Warning Signs Today

  1. Health and wealth. Teaching that God promises money, health, and success to those with enough faith, or to those who give to the ministry.
  2. New revelation. Claims that God told him something new that you must believe or obey.
  3. No sin, no judgment, no hell. A message only about self-esteem, purpose, and blessing.
  4. Christ made small. Jesus presented only as a good example, a life coach, or one way among many.
  5. Celebrity without a church. A large following, a brand, merchandise, and no elders.
  6. Anger at being tested. A faithful teacher welcomes being checked against Scripture. A false one calls it attacking God’s anointed.

Be Careful in Both Directions

Not every teacher who is wrong about something is a false teacher. A sound minister can be mistaken on a lesser point, or say something badly. Faithful men have differed on baptism, church government, and the millennium. Some of those differences are serious errors, but they aren’t heresy. Do not equate every serious error with a denial of the gospel. Yet destructive false teaching can also appear in a persistent defense of greed, sexual sin or lawlessness (2 Peter 2:1-3, 18-19). Judge a teacher’s doctrine and conduct from the evidence, and distinguish that judgment from claiming to know his final state. How to Spot Real Heresy sets out the difference.

Don’t become a man who hunts for heresy for sport. Test what you hear, hold fast that which is good (1 Thessalonians 5:21), and speak with grief, not glee.

Test a Claim in Context

If you hear that God will make every faithful believer wealthy, find the passage offered as proof and read its context. First Timothy 6:5–10 directly addresses the mistake of treating godliness as a means of gain and teaches contentment. Verses 17–19 direct believers who are already rich to humility, generosity, and hope in God. Neither passage promises wealth as the result of sufficient faith.

Ask whether the teacher has explained the apparent conflict. Does he correct the claim, distinguish it carefully, or insist that poverty proves spiritual failure? Judge the teaching from what is actually said. A disputed summary from a hostile source isn't a substitute for the material itself.

The same method applies to claims about Christ, salvation, or authority. Identify the proposition, compare it with clear Scripture, and consider the explanation in context. You don't need to master every book a speaker has written before rejecting a plainly false claim. You do need enough evidence to describe that claim truthfully.

If you're unsure, take the source and your question to an elder. Ask for help understanding the doctrine rather than asking him to endorse a conclusion you've already announced. Further study may confirm the concern, reveal a misunderstanding, or show that the issue is a serious but different kind of error.

Consider Doctrine, Conduct, and Church Office

A minister's teaching and life are both subject to Scripture. First Timothy 3:1–7 and Titus 1:5–9 require sound doctrine and a character suited to oversight. A speaker can state orthodox sentences while greed, sexual wrongdoing, or domineering conduct makes him unfit for office. Correct theology doesn't excuse those sins or remove the duty to address them.

Distinguish the questions you're answering. Is a particular statement false? Is there a sustained pattern of destructive teaching? Is an officer qualified to continue serving? The answers may require different evidence and different church action. You shouldn't have to prove that someone denies the Trinity before taking credible misconduct seriously.

Ordinary listeners can stop receiving harmful teaching and seek sound instruction. Church officers have responsibilities for investigation, discipline, and the protection of the congregation. If a concern involves danger or crime, appropriate protective and civil action also belongs in the response. Church procedures mustn't be used to prevent it.

Help a friend who has trusted the teaching by opening the relevant passages with him. Explain the error and offer faithful material on the same subject. Mocking him for having been persuaded can make an honest reconsideration harder. Your aim should be his understanding and spiritual good, with a truthful account of the danger.

What to Do

  1. Stop following him. Unsubscribe, stop buying, stop giving. Paul says to mark them and avoid them (Romans 16:17).
  2. Warn those you love, gently and with Scripture.
  3. If he is in your own church, go to your elders. Don’t start a campaign on your own.
  4. Sit under faithful preaching. The best protection from counterfeit is to know the real thing well. See How to Find a Faithful Church.
