What Repentance Is

Repentance unto life is a saving grace, whereby a sinner, out of a true sense of his sin, and apprehension of the mercy of God in Christ, doth, with grief and hatred of his sin, turn from it unto God, with full purpose of, and endeavour after, new obedience.

Westminster Shorter Catechism Q&A 87

It’s God’s gift (Acts 11:18), and it’s still your own turning, which he commands of you (Mark 1:15). The answer holds five things.

  1. A true sense of sin. Not only its danger but its filthiness, because it is against God’s holy nature and law (WCF 15.2). A man who only fears hell is sorry about the danger.
  2. An apprehension of God’s mercy in Christ. Without it, a sight of sin drives a man to hiding or despair. There is forgiveness with thee, that thou mayest be feared (Psalm 130:4).
  3. Grief and hatred of sin. You grieve that you did it, and you hate the thing itself, not only what it cost you.
  4. Turning from it unto God. Away from the sin and toward him, and from all your sins, not only the one that shamed you (WCF 15.2).
  5. Full purpose of, and endeavour after, new obedience. You mean to obey, and you begin. The purpose is whole even when the obedience is weak.

Repentance isn’t penance. It’s no satisfaction for sin and no cause of pardon. Pardon is the act of God’s free grace in Christ (WCF 15.3). Your tears don’t wash you. Christ’s blood does (1 John 1:7). Yet none may expect pardon without repentance. Except ye repent, ye shall all likewise perish (Luke 13:3). A man who says he trusts Christ while he means to keep his sin doesn’t yet know whom he is trusting.

Repentance and Faith Belong Together

In Acts 20:21, Paul summarizes his testimony as repentance toward God and faith toward the Lord Jesus Christ. These are inseparable graces of conversion. Faith receives and rests on Christ. Repentance turns from sin to God with a purpose of obedience. A sinner doesn't first make himself acceptable through repentance and then receive permission to trust the Savior.

Mark 1:15 calls hearers to repent and believe the gospel. Both are commanded now. Acts 11:18 also describes repentance unto life as God's gift. Our dependence on grace doesn't excuse delay, and the command doesn't imply that an unchanged heart can produce its own renewal. Ask God for the mercy he freely offers and come to Christ as the sinner you are.

Faith and repentance should remain distinct in our understanding. Repentance doesn't replace faith's receiving of Christ's righteousness, and faith doesn't allow a person to reserve a sin he intends to keep. The same grace that brings a sinner to the Savior turns his heart against the sins from which the Savior delivers him.

You don't need to reconstruct an exact sequence of feelings to come. Hear what God requires, acknowledge your need, and turn to him through Christ. A clear account of conversion can help the church understand your profession, but an impressive story isn't the ground of salvation.

Why Repentance Is Necessary without Earning Pardon

Justification is God's declaration that a sinner is righteous on the ground of Christ's obedience and satisfaction, received through faith alone (WCF 11.1–2). Repentance isn't an additional payment or a second instrument that supplies what faith lacks. Even our best repentance is imperfect and can't satisfy divine justice.

WCF 15.3 nevertheless says that none may expect pardon without repentance. The point is the inseparable relation of God's saving gifts. He doesn't justify people through a faith that leaves them permanently resolved to remain his enemies. Turning from sin accompanies receiving Christ, though neither the intensity of sorrow nor the success of a recent reform earns his verdict.

This protects the troubled sinner from trying to make his grief sufficient, and it warns the complacent sinner against using grace to defend disobedience. If you're counting tears as payment, look to Christ's finished work. If you're using that work as permission to cherish sin, repent and receive the Savior as he is offered.

The necessity is real, but the invitation is immediate. You aren't told to complete a period of moral improvement before seeking pardon. Repentance includes the beginning of new obedience, grounded in the mercy already set before sinners in Christ.

Turn from All Sin while Confessing Particular Sins

WCF 15.2 describes a turning from all sins to God, and WCF 15.5 requires particular repentance for particular sins. Those statements belong together. You mustn't confess the fault that became embarrassing while deliberately reserving another known wrong as something God has no right to address.

Turning from all sin doesn't mean you've already discovered every sinful motive or achieved perfect obedience. It means you accept God's judgment against sin and intend to walk in all his commandments. As his Word exposes something you hadn't understood, receive the correction and turn from that too. Psalm 119:128 joins esteem for God's precepts with hatred of every false way.

Confess what you actually know. Someone who has cheated should identify the dishonest conduct and begin the repair. Someone convicted of contempt should acknowledge the way he has treated his neighbor. A general statement about human weakness mustn't be used to avoid either responsibility.

Neither should confession become an endless search for a forgotten detail that might prevent mercy. God knows the depth of our corruption more fully than we do. Psalm 19:12–13 asks for cleansing from hidden faults and preservation from presumptuous sins. Use that prayer with honesty about known sin and dependence on his mercy for what you haven't yet understood.

Daily Repentance Doesn't Mean Being Justified Again

A believer's justification isn't revoked each time he sins. WCF 11.5 teaches that those justified can't fall from that state, while their sins can bring real fatherly displeasure and a loss of the light of God's countenance until they humble themselves, confess, seek pardon, and renew faith and repentance. The relationship is secure in Christ, and the offense still needs to be addressed.

Together, 1 John 1:8–10 and 1 John 2:1–2 hold both truths before the church. Denying our sin is false, confession belongs in Christian life, and Christ remains the advocate of those who sin. John writes so that believers won't sin, then directs the fallen believer to the righteous Savior. Neither complacency nor despair agrees with that teaching.

Daily confession therefore seeks the pardon of daily failings and renewed fellowship with the Father. It doesn't ask Christ to offer himself again or require you to create a new ground of acceptance. Return on the same sufficient foundation and receive the correction your Father gives.

That distinction also helps you accept consequences without interpreting every painful result as proof that God has rejected you. Some repairs take time, and some consequences remain after forgiveness. Your continuing duty is to trust Christ, tell the truth, and pursue the obedience now required.

Look for the Fruit That Fits the Sin

John the Baptist called for fruit fitting repentance (Luke 3:8–14). When people asked what they should do, he addressed actual duties. Those with resources should share with the needy, tax collectors should stop taking more than appointed, and soldiers should refuse extortion and false accusation. The response reached each person's ordinary calling.

That doesn't establish an identical outward program for every repentant person. It shows why repentance should affect the conduct the sin involved. Truthfulness answers deceit, generosity answers a grasping refusal to help, and patient, honorable speech answers contempt. Ask what positive obedience God requires in the place where you've been disobeying.

Fruit also takes the form of receiving correction without continuing to shift blame. Listen to the person you've wronged, admit what is true, and accept lawful limits while trust is rebuilt. WCF 15.6 requires the offended to receive a penitent brother in love. It also requires the offender to declare repentance appropriately, rather than demand that everyone accept an unsupported assurance while he protects the wrongdoing.

God alone knows the heart completely. The church must judge a profession and visible conduct with the evidence available, without demanding sinless maturity or ignoring deliberate resistance. Seek genuine change and patient accountability rather than either instant certification or a sentence of permanent suspicion.

Depend on Grace for the Continuing Fight

WCF 13.1–3 describes sanctification as a real work of Christ's Spirit throughout the believer, still imperfect in this life. Remaining corruption creates a continuing conflict. That conflict doesn't make obedience optional. Romans 6:12–14 tells believers to refuse sin's reign and present themselves to God because they live under grace.

Use the ordinary means through which Christ strengthens his people. Hear the Word, pray, receive the sacraments lawfully, and seek the help of the church. A particular pattern may also require removing an opportunity, correcting a habit, and giving an honest account to someone who can help. Those efforts depend on grace and are part of the obedience grace produces.

The size of your sin doesn't make Christ's mercy insufficient. WCF 15.4 says that every sin deserves condemnation, while no sin can bring condemnation on those who truly repent. First Timothy 1:12–16 describes Paul's former blasphemy and persecution in order to magnify the mercy shown him. Your past is a reason to seek that mercy, rather than a qualification you must overcome before approaching.

When you fall, return promptly. When you see genuine growth, thank God and continue watching. Neither failure nor progress changes the foundation on which a sinner comes. Christ is sufficient, repentance is commanded, and his grace supplies strength for the obedience before you.

Two Kinds of Sorrow

For godly sorrow worketh repentance to salvation not to be repented of: but the sorrow of the world worketh death.

2 Corinthians 7:10 (KJV)

Paul was glad the Corinthians sorrowed to repentance, because it made them earnest to set things right (verses 9-11).

Judas and Peter both sinned against Christ on the same night. Judas repented himself, brought back the silver, and said, I have sinned in that I have betrayed the innocent blood (Matthew 27:3-4). The words were true. But he took his sorrow to the priests and not to Christ, and he went out and hanged himself (Matthew 27:5). Peter denied his Lord three times. Then the Lord turned, and looked upon Peter, and Peter went out, and wept bitterly (Luke 22:61-62). His sorrow brought him back, and Christ restored him (John 21:15-17).

The difference wasn’t how much they hurt. It was where they carried the hurt. Worldly sorrow grieves over loss and shame and turns in on itself. Godly sorrow runs to God for mercy. If you’re in that darkness now, hear this carefully. Thoughts of ending your life don’t prove that your repentance is false or that you aren’t his. Deep depression is often an illness of body and mind, and true believers have been crushed by it (read When You Are Depressed). But any voice that tells you Christ won’t have you, or that death is the way out, is lying to you about him. Christ looked at Peter to call him back, and he hasn’t stopped looking for you. Tell someone today, and if those thoughts press on you, call 988 or text 988 in the United States.

Repent of Sins by Name

The Confession calls every man to repent of his particular sins particularly (WCF 15.5). Saying “I am a sinner” costs nothing. Saying what you did costs something. David’s prayer after Bathsheba is the pattern. He begins with God’s mercy, not his record (Psalm 51:1). Then he owns the sin.

For I acknowledge my transgressions: and my sin is ever before me.

Psalm 51:3 (KJV)

He had wronged Uriah terribly, yet he says, Against thee, thee only, have I sinned (verse 4), because every sin is first against God. He asks to be washed (verse 2), for a clean heart (verse 10), and for the joy of salvation to come back (verse 12). And he knows God won't despise a broken and a contrite heart (verse 17).

Confess to God first. Then confess to those you’ve wronged, who are to receive you in love (WCF 15.6, Matthew 5:23-24). Where you took something, give it back. Zacchaeus said, if I have taken any thing from any man by false accusation, I restore him fourfold (Luke 19:8). The law asked for the thing returned and a fifth added (Numbers 5:7). If you lied about someone, tell the truth to those who heard it. Some wrongs can’t be undone. Some confessions would only wound again or put someone in danger. Ask an elder how and when.

Coming Home, and Coming Home Again

Christ taught us to ask for forgiveness in the same prayer as our daily bread, Forgive us our debts (Matthew 6:11-12). A believer keeps falling and keeps returning. That doesn’t prove you never repented. It’s what repentance looks like while sin remains (WCF 13.2). If the same sin keeps winning, read When the Same Sin Keeps Coming Back, and for the long fight, Struggling With Sin. Mortify can help you confess daily and fight alongside friends from your church.

And he arose, and came to his father. But when he was yet a great way off, his father saw him, and had compassion, and ran, and fell on his neck, and kissed him.

Luke 15:20 (KJV)

Jesus told the story of the prodigal son because the Pharisees murmured that he received sinners (Luke 15:1-2). Jesus uses the son’s return to show the Father’s welcome for repentant sinners. The son came to himself and went home to say, Father, I have sinned (verses 17-18). He meant to ask to be a hired servant, but his father never let him finish. He called for the robe, the ring, and the feast, for this my son was dead, and is alive again (verses 19-24). That’s how the Father receives a sinner who comes home through his Son.

If You Fear You've Repented Too Many Times

Christ told his disciples that if a brother sins against them seven times in a day, and seven times turns again saying, I repent, they must forgive him (Luke 17:4). He doesn’t ask more mercy of us than he shows. Your pardon never rested on your count. It rests on his blood, and he is able also to save them to the uttermost that come unto God by him (Hebrews 7:25). Fear by itself doesn’t establish that you’ve repented. Bring the particular sin to Christ, turn from it, and trust his promise to receive those who come. Come again tonight. Don’t wait until you’ve stopped falling.

When You've Committed the Same Sin Again

Return to God with the truth about this occurrence. Repetition doesn't make confession pointless, and it doesn't make the sin harmless. Ask what you did, whom you injured, and what you've continued to permit that helps the pattern. If you keep lying about spending, repentance includes truthful accounts and a workable arrangement for accountability. If anger repeatedly becomes cruel speech, stop the exchange before you begin insulting, then return to the unresolved matter when you can speak rightly.

Remove opportunities you can lawfully remove. Seek help from a mature believer or elder who can ask about actual conduct. Don't substitute an elaborate plan for carrying out today's duty. A promise to overhaul your whole life next month can become another way to avoid an apology, a repayment, or a difficult truthful conversation now.

Second Corinthians 7:8–11 describes the Corinthians' earnest response to Paul's correction. Their grief produced concern to put matters right. This doesn't establish a required emotional intensity for every repentant believer. It does show why an apology that leaves the wrong deliberately untouched is inadequate. Ask God for both sorrow over sin and willingness to obey.

Put Right What You Can

If you've taken money, return it and make the matter clear to the person concerned. If you can't repay immediately, give an honest account and seek a realistic arrangement rather than disappearing. If you've spread a false report, correct it with the people you misled. Share enough to repair the wrong without unnecessarily spreading private details to a larger audience.

Some injuries can't be undone. Repentance then includes acknowledging the lasting harm and accepting lawful consequences. God's forgiveness doesn't automatically restore a position of trust, end a church censure, or cancel civil responsibility. Where legal obligations are involved, obtain qualified advice and tell the truth. Don't use religious language to pressure an injured person into withdrawing a report or restoring access to someone vulnerable.

Where direct contact would violate a protective boundary or cause further harm, seek a lawful way to make necessary restitution through an appropriate intermediary. You aren't entitled to a meeting, a reply, or reassurance from the person you injured. Your duty is to repent and do what is right. His response isn't a condition you can impose before obeying.

None of these repairs earns pardon. They follow from taking God's judgment of the sin seriously and receiving his mercy in Christ. The freeness of forgiveness gives a sinner reason to come honestly into the light and begin repairing what he can.

Steps for Today

  1. Come to Christ first. Repentance without him is only regret. If you’ve never trusted him, read the gospel and come today.
  2. Name the sin. Before God, say what you did and against whom. How to Examine Yourself can help you see it.
  3. Pray Psalm 51 aloud. Then believe the promise. If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins (1 John 1:9).
  4. Make it right this week. Confess to the person you wronged and pay back what you took. If direct contact would violate a protective order or endanger someone, get help arranging lawful confession and restitution.
  5. Cut off the supply. Make not provision for the flesh (Romans 13:14). Change what made the sin easy, and tell an elder or a trusted brother (James 5:16).
  6. Do it again tomorrow. A few minutes each night, looking back over the day, keeps short accounts with God.
