Worry can make it hard to sleep, work, eat, think clearly, or enjoy a conversation. Sometimes fear comes with a racing heart, dizziness, shortness of breath, or a strong feeling that something terrible is about to happen. Don't assume that it is a spiritual failure. The symptoms deserve proper attention, especially if they are new or keep returning.

If you have new or severe chest pain, trouble breathing, fainting, or symptoms that could be a medical emergency, seek urgent medical assessment. Don't assume an unfamiliar physical symptom is only a panic attack. If you are thinking of ending your life, read I'm Thinking of Ending My Life and seek immediate help.

When Panic Hits

Move somewhere you can sit safely if possible. Tell someone nearby, "I'm frightened and having trouble settling down. Can you stay with me?" Breathe gently and naturally rather than forcing large breaths. Notice your feet on the floor and a few ordinary things around you. These steps may help with distress, but they are not a way to rule out a medical emergency.

Afterward, write down when it happened, what you felt, how long it lasted, and whether it has happened before. Bring that account to your doctor. A clinician can check for other possible causes and discuss treatment if panic or anxiety is affecting your life.

If Fear Follows You Every Day

Start with a concrete question. Is there a danger you can address now, a decision you can plan for, or a worry you cannot resolve today? If an actual problem needs action, take the next reasonable step. If worry keeps returning without leading to action, tell your doctor what it is doing to your sleep, concentration, work, and relationships.

Therapy and, in some cases, medication can help with persistent anxiety and panic. Your clinician can help you consider options. Avoid abruptly stopping prescribed medication. If the treatment isn't helping or causes problems, tell the prescriber. Ask someone you trust to help you arrange the appointment if even making a call feels overwhelming.

Does Anxiety Mean You Aren't Trusting God?

Christ teaches his disciples not to be consumed with anxiety about tomorrow (Matthew 6:25-34). He calls us to trust our Father's provision and to seek his kingdom. But that passage wasn't written to diagnose every frightening physical sensation or medical condition. You can heed Christ's teaching and still need treatment for an anxiety disorder.

Scripture also gives examples of people praying through fear. David said, "What time I am afraid, I will trust in thee" (Psalm 56:3). Fear was present when he spoke of trust. You can tell God the truth about what frightens you and ask for help without waiting until your feelings settle.

If your fear centers on election, blasphemous thoughts, or repeated checking of your faith, read Doubts and Unwanted Thoughts. Tell a qualified clinician if intrusive thoughts or rituals are disrupting your life. Your pastor can help explain Scripture, but he should not be expected to diagnose you.

Ask for the Help You Actually Need

You might ask a church member to sit beside you during worship, help you get to an appointment, or call you once during the week. If you cannot attend because of illness, tell your elders and ask them to visit. It's reasonable to seek a quieter place or a practical adjustment when crowds or noise become overwhelming.

Use the NIMH guide to anxiety or its panic disorder guide to prepare questions for your clinician. These are educational resources, not diagnoses. If you're struggling with ordinary duties, ask for specific help rather than waiting until everything becomes impossible.
