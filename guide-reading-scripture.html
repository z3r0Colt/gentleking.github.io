How to Read the Bible Rightly

All of it is true. What reading literally means, the kinds of writing in Scripture, how to tell a figure of speech, and Christ at the center.

  1. All of It Is True

About 8 minutes to read

The Short Answer

Read a passage according to its context and kind of writing. Figures of speech communicate truth, and clear passages help explain difficult ones.

All of It Is True

All scripture is given by inspiration of God, and is profitable for doctrine, for reproof, for correction, for instruction in righteousness:

2 Timothy 3:16 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

Given by inspiration means breathed out by God. The Bible doesn’t merely contain the Word of God. It is the Word of God, and Jesus said to his Father, thy word is truth (John 17:17 (opens in a new tab)). The Confession says we believe it not because a man or a church vouches for it. We believe it because God, who is truth itself, is its Author (WCF 1.4 (opens in a new tab)). And his Spirit bears witness by and with the Word in our hearts (WCF 1.5 (opens in a new tab)).

You don’t sit over the Bible as its judge. You sit under it as a child before his Father. For many of us the hard part isn’t believing it’s true. It’s knowing what each part means. This page is for that.

What Reading Literally Means

Taking the Bible literally means taking each part as it was meant to be taken. A psalm is a song, a law is a law, and a proverb is a proverb. The meaning of a passage is the sense its author intended. Behind every human author stands God, who meant it first.

That guards against two errors. A wooden reading makes the words say what no writer meant, as if the floods must grow hands because the psalm tells them to clap (Psalm 98:8 (opens in a new tab)). A loose reading turns plain statements into pictures whenever they become uncomfortable. The first mistakes the form. The second refuses the content.

The infallible rule of interpretation of Scripture is the Scripture itself; and therefore, when there is a question about the true and full sense of any scripture (which is not manifold, but one), it must be searched and known by other places that speak more clearly.

Westminster Confession of Faith 1.9 (opens in a new tab)

Scripture interprets Scripture, so a hard place is read in the light of plainer ones. And the true sense of a passage is one, not many. There is a right reading, and it’s worth the work of finding.

Take heart. Not everything in Scripture is equally plain. But what you must know, believe, and do to be saved is set out clearly. An ordinary reader, using ordinary means, can understand it (WCF 1.7 (opens in a new tab)). A hard chapter in Daniel can’t take Christ from you.

Know What Kind of Writing It Is

God spoke at sundry times and in divers manners (Hebrews 1:1 (opens in a new tab)). Each kind of writing is read by its own rules, and every one is wholly true.

  1. History tells what happened. Luke wrote that Theophilus might know the certainty of those things (Luke 1:3-4 (opens in a new tab)).
  2. Law commands. Thou shalt not steal (Exodus 20:15 (opens in a new tab)) means what it says. Israel’s ceremonial and civil laws have ended with Christ and that nation, but the moral law still binds (WCF 19.3-5 (opens in a new tab)).
  3. Poetry sings in pictures. The Lord is my shepherd (Psalm 23:1 (opens in a new tab)) is a figure, and the care it pictures is real.
  4. Proverb tells how God has ordered life to go as a rule. A soft answer turneth away wrath (Proverbs 15:1 (opens in a new tab)), though not every angry man softens. See Is Proverbs 22:6 a Promise?
  5. Prophecy speaks for God, often in vivid pictures. Micah named Bethlehem as the Ruler’s birthplace centuries ahead (Micah 5:2 (opens in a new tab)), and there Christ was born (Matthew 2:5-6 (opens in a new tab)). The guide to reading prophecy says more.
  6. Parable is a story Jesus told to teach. In the sower, Jesus explains that the seed is the word of God (Luke 8:11 (opens in a new tab)), and that lesson is true.
  7. Letter was written to a real church about real problems. Now concerning the things whereof ye wrote unto me (1 Corinthians 7:1 (opens in a new tab)). Ask what the apostle was answering, then hear it as written to you.
  8. Apocalyptic vision shows heavenly things in symbols. John hears of the Lion of the tribe of Juda, turns, and sees a Lamb as it had been slain (Revelation 5:5-6 (opens in a new tab)). Both pictures show one real Christ, who conquered by dying.

How to Tell a Figure of Speech

When you’re unsure, ask these questions.

  1. Would the plain sense be absurd? Jesus said, I am the door (John 10:9 (opens in a new tab)). He isn’t wood on hinges. He is the one way into God’s fold, and that’s no less true for being said in a picture.
  2. Would it contradict other Scripture? Under his wings shalt thou trust (Psalm 91:4 (opens in a new tab)). But God is a Spirit (John 4:24 (opens in a new tab)), without body or parts (WCF 2.1 (opens in a new tab)). So the wings are a figure, and what they picture is real, his near and sheltering care.
  3. Does the Bible explain it? Jesus said, Destroy this temple, and John tells us he spake of the temple of his body (John 2:19-21 (opens in a new tab)).
  4. Is it overstated on purpose? When Jesus says to pluck out the offending eye (Matthew 5:29 (opens in a new tab)), he isn’t asking you to maim yourself, since sin comes from the heart (Mark 7:21 (opens in a new tab)). He means deal with sin without mercy, because hell is real.

When none of these applies, take the words at face value. A figure always points to something real. The Lamb was truly slain. A figure makes a real thing easier to see and harder to forget.

The opposite error

Some readers, once they learn that the Bible uses figures, begin to treat its history as myth. That doesn’t read the author’s sense. It overrules it, and Christ himself closes that door.

He grounded marriage on what God did when he which made them at the beginning made them male and female (Matthew 19:4-5 (opens in a new tab)). He compared his coming to the days of Noah, when the flood came, and took them all away (Matthew 24:37-39 (opens in a new tab)). A pretend flood is no warning of a real judgment. He set his three days in the grave beside Jonah’s three days in the fish (Matthew 12:40 (opens in a new tab)), and said the men of Nineveh will rise in the judgment (Matthew 12:41 (opens in a new tab)). Paul sets Adam beside Christ, one real man beside another (Romans 5:12-19 (opens in a new tab)). If Jesus read these chapters as history, his disciples may not do less. And the God who raised his Son from the dead isn’t strained by a great fish.

Read for Christ, and With the Church

On the road to Emmaus the risen Lord taught his disciples to read their Bibles. Beginning at Moses and all the prophets, he expounded unto them in all the scriptures the things concerning himself (Luke 24:27 (opens in a new tab)). Later he showed them that the law, the prophets, and the psalms were all written concerning him. Then opened he their understanding, that they might understand the scriptures (Luke 24:44-45 (opens in a new tab)).

Christ is the center of the whole Book. The Passover lamb whose bones were not broken (Exodus 12:46 (opens in a new tab), John 19:36 (opens in a new tab)) and the servant wounded for our transgressions (Isaiah 53:5 (opens in a new tab)) lead to him. If you finish a passage and haven’t seen your need of him, read it again. If you’ve never come to him, the gospel is where to start.

Context and the church

Read every verse in its paragraph and its book. Most errors start with a verse torn from its setting, the way the unlearned and unstable wrest the scriptures (2 Peter 3:16 (opens in a new tab)).

And don’t read alone. How can I, except some man should guide me? the Ethiopian asked (Acts 8:31 (opens in a new tab)). The Confession and Catechisms are the church’s careful summary of what Scripture teaches. They keep you from inventing a reading no faithful Christian has held. Trusted commentaries like Matthew Henry help too. Read the text first, then the help, and bring your questions to your pastor.

Where faithful teachers differ

On some questions godly, confessional men have differed. The Confession says God made all things of nothing, in the space of six days, and all very good (WCF 4.1 (opens in a new tab)). The Reformed Presbyterian Testimony adds that Genesis 1 (opens in a new tab) and 2 are history, not mythology, and that man did not evolve from any lower form of life (RP Testimony 4.1, 4.4). Most have held the days to be ordinary days, while some confessional teachers have read their length another way. Faithful men differ too on the millennium of Revelation 20 (opens in a new tab) (see The Thousand Years). On questions like these, study, hold your view honestly, and hold it humbly. The secret things belong unto the Lord our God, but those things which are revealed belong unto us (Deuteronomy 29:29 (opens in a new tab)).

Steps for This Week

  1. Pray before you read. Open thou mine eyes, that I may behold wondrous things out of thy law (Psalm 119:18 (opens in a new tab)).
  2. Name the kind of writing before you read each passage.
  3. Work through one hard passage. Read the whole chapter, ask the four questions above, and follow the cross references in your margin.
  4. Ask where Christ is. Write one sentence on how the passage points to him.
  5. Check yourself against the church. Read Matthew Henry on it, or find where the Confession speaks to it. Sojourner puts both beside the text.
  6. Obey what is plain. Don’t let a hard verse keep you from what a clear one commands today. For a plan to read the whole Bible, see How to Read the Bible.
