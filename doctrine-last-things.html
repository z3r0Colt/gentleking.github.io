Doctrine

The End Times

Christ’s return, the resurrection, and the judgment.

About 3 minutes to read

How It Ends, and What Is Settled

Eschatology

Start with what every Reformed believer confesses. It’s more than most people expect, and it’s enough to live and die on.

Christ is reigning now, seated at the right hand of the Father, with all authority in heaven and on earth. He will return bodily, visibly, personally. The dead will be raised bodily, the righteous and the wicked alike. There will be a final judgment, real and just, and the wicked will be punished forever. Those in Christ will be acquitted openly and welcomed. Then come the new heavens and the new earth, where righteousness dwells and God himself lives with his people.

And God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes; and there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying, neither shall there be any more pain: for the former things are passed away.

Revelation 21:4 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

From here on, faithful men differ. The question is how the thousand years of Revelation 20 (opens in a new tab) relates to the present age and to Christ’s return. Two common answers within confessional Reformed Christianity are amillennialism and postmillennialism. Historic premillennialism is included below as an older Christian view for comparison. It must not be treated as another way of stating the Confession’s account of a general resurrection at the last day and final judgment.

Amillennialism

Held widely in Reformed churches

The thousand years is the whole present age between Christ’s first and second comings. Satan is bound from deceiving the nations so the gospel can go out. The souls of the departed reign with Christ now. The kingdom advances through the Word while the world stays a mix of wheat and weeds. Christ returns once, and judgment and the new creation follow immediately.

Postmillennialism

Held by many Puritans and by Edwards

The same gospel that saves one sinner will, in God’s time, bring an age of widespread blessing. The nations will be discipled in great numbers, and Christ’s rule will be honored broadly on earth before he returns. That age is the fruit of ordinary preaching and the Spirit’s power, not a separate dispensation. Christ returns at the close of it to judge and to make all things new.

Historic Premillennialism

An old view, distinct from dispensationalism

Christ returns first, and a real reign on earth follows his coming. The church passes through tribulation and isn't removed from it. This view is ancient, and it isn’t built on a separation between Israel and the church.

The man who writes this site holds the first view above. He’s amillennial. The original Westminster Confession does not use the later labels, but it does give a sequence. At the last day, the living are changed and all the dead are raised (WCF 32.2-3 (opens in a new tab)). All people then appear before Christ in the appointed judgment (WCF 33.1 (opens in a new tab)). A scheme that separates the resurrection of the righteous from that of the wicked by a future earthly millennium differs from that account. The comparison above does not make all three views the confessional position of this ministry.

Two cautions, though. Don't treat dispensational premillennialism, with its sharp split between Israel and the church and its secret rapture, as the Reformed position. It isn’t, and it came late. And don't let curiosity about the end replace readiness for it. The last things were given to make you holy and to comfort you while you wait.
