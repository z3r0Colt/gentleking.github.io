How to Leave a Church Well

For an ordinary transfer, discuss your reasons and seek continuing care. If there is abuse or danger, protection comes first and does not require church permission.

  5. When a Church Has Hurt You
  6. This Week

About 8 minutes to read

For an ordinary transfer, explain your reasons to the elders, ask how membership will be transferred, and seek a faithful receiving congregation. If the concern is danger or abuse, begin with protection rather than a transfer meeting. Whether a church is a true church at all is answered in How to Tell a True Church From a False One. If a church has wounded you, read When Your Church Has Hurt You for a fuller discussion, or go to When a Church Has Hurt You below.

If someone in your church is abusing you or your children, you do not need church permission to seek protection. If danger is immediate, contact emergency services. An advocate can help plan safer steps when leaving may bring retaliation. Read If You Are Not Safe. Abuse and other crimes belong with the police, whoever has done them. For suspected child abuse, contact the appropriate police or child-protection authority. Reporting rules differ by jurisdiction. Do not delay urgent protection for a church investigation. No elder, no church process, and no membership vow can require you to stay in danger.

Membership Is a Covenant

Church membership carries commitments before God and his people. Understand the actual promises you made and the lawful oversight of your elders. They also answer to God for their care of the flock.

Obey them that have the rule over you, and submit yourselves: for they watch for your souls, as they that must give account, that they may do it with joy, and not with grief: for that is unprofitable for you.

Hebrews 13:17 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

God tells his people to consider one another to provoke unto love and to good works, not forsaking the assembling of ourselves together (Hebrews 10:24-25 (opens in a new tab)). That’s a body, not a club. Leaving isn’t a sin in itself, and sometimes it’s a duty. An ordinary transfer deserves honest discussion. That does not require publishing private information or warning an unsafe person before seeking protection.

Good and Bad Reasons to Leave

Reasons That Aren't Enough

  1. Preferences and programs. The length of the sermon, the size of the church, the manner of the pastor, a busier calendar somewhere else. A preference by itself is not a reason to treat membership lightly. But sermon access, language, disability, transport, or household care may present real barriers. Discuss those needs rather than dismissing them as tastes. Your soul is fed by the Word, the sacraments, and prayer, under elders who know you.
  2. An offense you haven’t taken to the person. Go and tell him his fault between thee and him alone (Matthew 18:15 (opens in a new tab)). That instruction addresses an ordinary dispute. It does not require confronting an abuser alone or violating a protective order. See forgiveness and safe boundaries.
  3. Avoiding discipline. Leaving doesn’t end the matter. See If You Are Under Discipline.
  4. Church-hopping. Paul warns of those who heap to themselves teachers, having itching ears (2 Timothy 4:3 (opens in a new tab)). Moving merely to avoid sound teaching can prevent accountable care. Repeated moves caused by housing, work, safety, or caregiving need different counsel.

Reasons That Can Be Enough

  1. The church has abandoned the gospel. Then you must leave. See What Makes a Church False.
  2. Serious error the elders won’t correct. False teaching on a weighty matter, worship God hasn’t commanded, or open sin left undisciplined for years. Raise the concern through an appropriate safe route, as below. If nothing changes, you may need to leave.
  3. You can’t worship there in good conscience. God alone is Lord of the conscience (WCF 20.2 (opens in a new tab)), and whatsoever is not of faith is sin (Romans 14:23 (opens in a new tab)). The church may require what you believe Scripture forbids. If patient talks with the elders haven’t settled it, you may go. Be sure it’s conscience and not a preference dressed up as one.
  4. A move or a providential call. A new job, a marriage, the care of a parent, or a call to help plant a church. Seek your elders’ counsel, and go with their blessing.

How to Leave Well

  1. Pray, and don’t hurry. Search me, O God, and know my heart (Psalm 139:23 (opens in a new tab)).
  2. Talk with the elders first. For an ordinary disagreement or transfer, tell them plainly what troubles you and give them time to answer. If they are implicated in abuse or intimidation, use an independent safe contact instead.
  3. Use the proper courts where it’s safe. For an ordinary personal offense, follow Christ’s directions in Matthew 18:15-17 (opens in a new tab). Abuse, threats, and possible crimes require protection and appropriate civil reporting. A church process must not delay those steps. If the elders won’t hear a serious matter, a Presbyterian church lets you bring it to the presbytery, as What to Do explains.
  4. Ask for a letter of transfer. When Apollos went to Achaia, the brethren wrote, exhorting the disciples to receive him (Acts 18:27 (opens in a new tab)). A letter can help the receiving elders understand your membership and care needs. Apollos’s letter is a biblical example of commendation, not a complete modern transfer procedure. If a letter is refused or records are unavailable, explain the circumstances to the receiving church.
  5. Guard your mouth on the way out. Let no corrupt communication proceed out of your mouth (Ephesians 4:29 (opens in a new tab)). The Larger Catechism counts backbiting and tale bearing as sins against the ninth commandment. But it also forbids undue silence in a just cause (Q&A 145). Real abuse should be told plainly to those who must know.
  6. Keep the friendships you can. Don’t make anyone choose sides. Be kind one to another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, even as God for Christ’s sake hath forgiven you (Ephesians 4:32 (opens in a new tab)).
  7. Join a faithful church soon. Seek a safe congregation and continuing care. Recovery from abuse, illness, or difficulty traveling may take time. Ask a trustworthy pastor for help rather than treating immediate attendance as a test of recovery. See How to Find a Faithful Church.

If You're Under Discipline

If your elders have admonished you or kept you from the Lord’s Table, moving does not settle whether the censure was just or what repentance is needed. Church censures are for the reclaiming and gaining of offending brethren (WCF 30.3 (opens in a new tab)). A lawful censure does not cease merely because a member moves or asks another church to receive him. The duty is repentance and restoration, not finding a congregation that will ignore the matter. This applies to lawful discipline, not an assumption that every action taken by an elder is just. Tell a prospective receiving church about the matter rather than concealing it. Its elders need to distinguish a lawful censure from a disputed or abusive action. Confess what is true and seek restoration in the spirit of meekness (Galatians 6:1 (opens in a new tab)).

If you believe the discipline is unjust, ask about the church’s appeal process and seek independent counsel if necessary. An appeal does not require remaining in danger or meeting an unsafe leader alone. And if you’re being disciplined for reporting abuse or for fleeing danger, that isn’t Christ’s discipline. Get safe, and tell the presbytery or another faithful pastor.

When a Church Has Hurt You

Some of the deepest wounds come from God’s own people. David describes betrayal by a close companion. But it was thou, a man mine equal, my guide, and mine acquaintance. We took sweet counsel together, and walked unto the house of God in company (Psalm 55:13-14 (opens in a new tab)). Your grief is real, and you may pour it out to God as David did.

The temptation then is to give up on the church altogether. Please don’t. God is against shepherds who harm his flock. He says of his sheep, I will seek that which was lost, and bring again that which was driven away, and will bind up that which was broken (Ezekiel 34:16 (opens in a new tab)). He does that through faithful shepherds in faithful churches.

Husbands, love your wives, even as Christ also loved the church, and gave himself for it; That he might sanctify and cleanse it with the washing of water by the word, That he might present it to himself a glorious church, not having spot, or wrinkle, or any such thing; but that it should be holy and without blemish.

Ephesians 5:25-27 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

Paul pictures a bride washed and made ready, and the picture is true. Christ loves his church, he died for her, and he is cleansing her by his Word. He will remove every spot you’ve seen. He will not forsake his people. If you’re not sure you're his, start with the gospel. Then find a faithful church, and let its elders earn your trust over time.

This Week

  1. If you’re in danger, get safe today and call the police. Read If You Are Not Safe.
  2. Write down why you want to leave. Distinguish a preference, a serious doctrinal concern, and a practical or safety barrier. Discuss the actual issue with a trustworthy person.
  3. Ask to meet with your pastor or an elder. For an ordinary offense, a safe private conversation may help. Use an independent contact for concerns about abuse or intimidation.
  4. If you go, ask for a letter of transfer, where appropriate. Tell the receiving church if a letter is unavailable.
  5. Be in worship this Lord’s Day, in a safe, faithful congregation as you are able. If you cannot attend, ask for pastoral contact and practical help.
  6. Pray by name for the church you’re leaving.
