When Your Church Has Hurt You

If you have been mistreated or betrayed by church leaders, find practical help with safety, reporting, accountability, and faithful pastoral care.

On this page

  1. Put the Facts Into Words
  2. Where to Turn for Help
  3. Does Their Sin Mean Christ Has Failed You?
  4. What Faithful Leaders Should Do

About 4 minutes to read

You may have trusted a pastor, elder, or congregation and then found that you were mistreated. Perhaps someone used Scripture to silence you, spoke about you dishonestly, ignored a serious complaint, or failed to protect a child. You may find it hard to enter another church building now. We need to distinguish a painful disagreement from actual wrongdoing, but neither should be dismissed without hearing the facts.

If someone has assaulted, threatened, exploited, or abused you or a child, seek safety and contact the appropriate civil authority. You don't need permission from church leaders to report a crime or suspected child abuse. If making contact with the person who harmed you could expose you to danger, do not arrange a private meeting with them. See If You Are Not Safe.

Put the Facts Into Words

Write down what happened while details are still clear. Keep copies of messages or relevant documents. Separate what you saw or heard from what you suspect someone intended. If a leader is accused of wrongdoing, he should not be the only person deciding whether the accusation deserves attention.

Church discipline and pastoral authority have a lawful purpose. They are not unlimited power. Elders must govern under Christ's Word and may themselves be held accountable. Being told to keep a serious wrong secret, accept danger, or obey an unbiblical demand is not the same as being called to biblical submission.

Where to Turn for Help

If the concern is a misunderstanding or ordinary disagreement, you can ask for a fair conversation with the appropriate elders. For disputed discipline or serious failures by an elder, ask about review or appeal through the church's presbytery or other proper governing body. If those involved are not safe or impartial, seek independent counsel and protect yourself first. A church process cannot replace the civil response to a crime.

A qualified counselor experienced with trauma may help if you have nightmares, panic, fear of religious authority, or trouble trusting anyone. You can ask a trustworthy Christian or pastor outside the situation to listen without urging you to return to an unsafe setting.

For ordinary transfers or questions about church membership, read Leaving a Church and Finding a Church. Those guides explain membership duties, but they do not require you to put yourself or your children at risk.

Does Their Sin Mean Christ Has Failed You?

A pastor's office doesn't make his every action righteous. Scripture warns elders not to act as lords over God's heritage (1 Peter 5:2-3). The church must hear complaints fairly and correct real wrongdoing. If people who should have helped you instead caused harm, you may grieve that without pretending it was small.

Christ doesn't approve of every deed done in his name. He is the head of his church and will call its shepherds to account (Ezekiel 34:1-10, 1 Peter 5:4). But don't let another person's misuse of the Bible persuade you that the Bible commanded his sin. Ask a careful, independent pastor to help you distinguish the two.

You may need time, safe counsel, and gradual contact with a faithful congregation. Don't make a promise about when you'll feel ready. Seek public worship as your circumstances permit, and ask for accommodations or a pastoral visit when needed. Your suffering shouldn't become another excuse for a church to neglect its duty of care.

What Faithful Leaders Should Do

They should listen carefully, preserve relevant evidence, protect those who may be at risk, and follow appropriate civil reporting and legal requirements. They should avoid premature promises about outcomes or confidentiality. They must not use forgiveness to suppress the facts, cancel just consequences, or press someone to return to danger. They should also teach the duty to turn from revenge and forgive a repentant brother (Luke 17:3-4, WCF 15.6 (opens in a new tab)). Protection and truthful accountability belong alongside that duty. Repentance calls for truthful confession and fitting correction, not just a public apology.

God's people should not be forced to choose between seeking justice and trusting Christ. A safe, faithful congregation can be one means through which the Lord gives care, but it must actually practice the duties Scripture places upon it.
