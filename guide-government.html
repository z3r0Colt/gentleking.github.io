What the Bible Says About Government

Rulers are ordained of God and owe allegiance to his Son. Obeying, praying, paying taxes, and when to obey God rather than men.

About 18 minutes to read

This page is about rulers and nations, and about the King who rules them all. If someone in authority is commanding you to do what God forbids, read When We Must Obey God Rather Than Men. If the news fills you with fear or anger, read Put Not Your Trust in Princes. If you’ve served as a soldier or an officer and carry the weight of it, read the part for you. If someone with power over you is hurting you, get safe first.

Government Is God’s Ordinance

Government wasn’t invented by kings or voted into being by the people. God made it. Paul wrote this to Christians living under Rome, a pagan empire that would later put many of them to death.

Let every soul be subject unto the higher powers. For there is no power but of God: the powers that be are ordained of God. Whosoever therefore resisteth the power, resisteth the ordinance of God: and they that resist shall receive to themselves damnation.

Romans 13:1-2 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

God, the Supreme Lord and King of all the world, hath ordained civil magistrates to be under him, over the people, for his own glory and the public good, and to this end hath armed them with the power of the sword, for the defense and encouragement of them that are good, and for the punishment of evil-doers.

Westminster Confession of Faith 23.1 (opens in a new tab)

Rulers are over the people, and they're under God. The proudest king on earth had to learn that the most High ruleth in the kingdom of men (Daniel 4:17 (opens in a new tab)).

The Minister of God With the Sword

Peter says governors are sent for the punishment of evildoers, and for the praise of them that do well (1 Peter 2:14 (opens in a new tab)). Paul says it more sharply.

For he is the minister of God to thee for good. But if thou do that which is evil, be afraid; for he beareth not the sword in vain: for he is the minister of God, a revenger to execute wrath upon him that doeth evil.

Romans 13:4 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

Minister means servant. The magistrate serves God as truly as a pastor does, though in a different work. The sword signifies lawful authority to use force and punish evildoers, even with death. God gave it after the flood. Whoso sheddeth man’s blood, by man shall his blood be shed, for in the image of God made he man (Genesis 9:6 (opens in a new tab)). Private men may not avenge themselves (Romans 12:19 (opens in a new tab)). God repays by the hand of the ruler.

So a Christian may hold the sword. The Confession teaches that Christians may accept the office of a magistrate when called to it. It also teaches that rulers may wage war upon just and necessary occasion (WCF 23.2 (opens in a new tab)). John the Baptist didn’t tell soldiers to leave the army. He told them, Do violence to no man, neither accuse any falsely (Luke 3:14 (opens in a new tab)). Cornelius was a centurion who feared God with all his house (Acts 10:1-2 (opens in a new tab)). A believer may serve as a judge, a soldier, or a police officer to the glory of God.

If You Have Borne the Sword

Some who read this have used force in war or in the line of duty, and some have taken a life. Lawful force used for justice and in defense of others isn’t murder. Yet many carry it for years. The weight may come from grief over what sin has done, even where the use of force was lawful. If there was real sin in it, cruelty or rage or harm to the innocent, Christ’s blood is enough for that too. Confess it to him plainly, and tell your pastor. If the weight has brought you to thoughts of ending your life, call 988 or text 988 now, and read the page on getting safe.

Christ Is King of Kings

Above every ruler stands a King. In the second psalm the nations rage against the Lord and his anointed, and God laughs, for he has set his King on his holy hill (Psalm 2:1-6 (opens in a new tab)). Then he speaks to the rulers.

Be wise now therefore, O ye kings: be instructed, ye judges of the earth. Serve the Lord with fear, and rejoice with trembling. Kiss the Son, lest he be angry, and ye perish from the way, when his wrath is kindled but a little. Blessed are all they that put their trust in him.

Psalm 2:10-12 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

A subject kissed his king as a sign of homage, and a worshiper kissed his idol (1 Kings 19:18 (opens in a new tab)). The kiss signifies allegiance. God commands kings and judges, as kings and judges, to bow to his Son.

That Son is Jesus Christ. After he rose he said, All power is given unto me in heaven and in earth (Matthew 28:18 (opens in a new tab)). As the eternal Son he always ruled with the Father. This is power given to him as Mediator. He is the God-man who died and rose again, crowned and set over everything for his people’s sake.

Which he wrought in Christ, when he raised him from the dead, and set him at his own right hand in the heavenly places, Far above all principality, and power, and might, and dominion, and every name that is named, not only in this world, but also in that which is to come: And hath put all things under his feet, and gave him to be the head over all things to the church,

Ephesians 1:20-22 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

Under his feet is a picture of a conquering king, and the rule it pictures is real. Every court and congress is beneath him now. John calls him the prince of the kings of the earth (Revelation 1:5 (opens in a new tab)). In John’s vision he rides out with a name written on his robe, KING OF KINGS, AND LORD OF LORDS (Revelation 19:16 (opens in a new tab)). The vision is full of pictures, but that name is the plain truth about him.

So a nation isn’t neutral ground. Its rulers owe Christ their allegiance, and its laws ought to honor his law. The original 1647 Confession expressly gives civil magistrates duties concerning the truth of God, his worship, and the peace of the church (WCF 23.1-3 (opens in a new tab)).

The Hope for the Nations

Scripture promises, Yea, all kings shall fall down before him, all nations shall serve him (Psalm 72:11 (opens in a new tab)). Reformed believers differ on how much of that we'll see before Christ returns.

Some believe the gospel will so prevail in this age that nations as nations will confess Christ before he comes. They point to the leaven that worked till the whole was leavened (Matthew 13:33 (opens in a new tab)), and the earth shall be filled with the knowledge of the glory of the Lord (Habakkuk 2:14 (opens in a new tab)). Others believe Christ gathers his people out of every nation while this age stays mixed, wheat and tares growing together until the harvest (Matthew 13:30 (opens in a new tab)). In this view the church is often small and suffering, and every knee will bow on the day he appears (Philippians 2:10-11 (opens in a new tab)).

Both hold that Christ reigns now, that the nations owe him obedience now, and that he must reign, till he hath put all enemies under his feet (1 Corinthians 15:25 (opens in a new tab)). Both pray, Thy kingdom come. We see not yet all things put under him. But we see Jesus (Hebrews 2:8-9 (opens in a new tab)).

The Magistrate's Duty Toward True Religion

The original Westminster Confession of 1647 teaches more than the duty of rulers to keep public order. It also says they must protect true religion and oppose public corruption in the church. We mustn't quote only the first sentence of chapter 23.3 and leave out what follows.

Read the full confessional statement

The civil magistrate may not assume to himself the administration of the Word and sacraments; or the power of the keys of the kingdom of heaven: yet he hath authority, and it is his duty, to take order, that unity and peace be preserved in the Church, that the truth of God be kept pure and entire; that all blasphemies and heresies be suppressed; all corruptions and abuses in worship and discipline prevented or reformed; and all the ordinances of God duly settled, administered, and observed. For the better effecting whereof, he hath power to call synods, to be present at them, and to provide that whatsoever is transacted in them be according to the mind of God.

Westminster Confession of Faith 23.3 (opens in a new tab) (1647)

This is the establishment teaching of the original Confession. It doesn't treat the civil ruler as religiously neutral. The magistrate is answerable to Christ for public justice and for the protection of true religion. The language about suppressing blasphemy and heresy is part of the original doctrine. It mustn't be hidden or exchanged for the later American revision.

Chapter 20.4 goes further. It says that certain teachings and practices contrary to the known principles of Christianity or destructive of the church's peace may be proceeded against by church censures and by the power of the civil magistrate. That's what the 1647 text teaches. It doesn't follow that every personal disagreement is heresy, or that a private person may take the sword into his own hands. Romans 13 (opens in a new tab) places the sword in lawful public office, and rulers themselves remain accountable to God's Word.

The Ruler Isn't a Minister of Word and Sacrament

The same paragraph that gives the magistrate these duties forbids him to preach by virtue of his civil office, administer baptism or the Supper, or exercise the keys of the kingdom. Christ gave church government and discipline to church officers, distinct from the civil magistrate (WCF 30.1-4 (opens in a new tab)). A ruler's duty to uphold true religion doesn't make him a pastor or an elder.

Chapter 31.2 permits magistrates to call synods. It also says that when magistrates are open enemies to the church, ministers may assemble by virtue of their office. And chapter 31.3 gives synods their own ministerial responsibility to decide controversies and matters of church government according to Scripture. The church isn't merely a department of the state.

God Alone Is Lord of the Conscience

God alone is Lord of the conscience, and hath left it free from the doctrines and commandments of men which are in anything contrary to his Word, or beside it, in matters of faith or worship.

Westminster Confession of Faith 20.2 (opens in a new tab) (1647)

The magistrate may require lawful outward obedience. He can't make a sinner believe, declare a human invention to be God's command, or take Christ's place as Lord of the conscience. Yet chapter 20.2 must be read alongside chapter 20.4. In the original Confession, liberty of conscience never meant a civil government had no duty concerning religion.

Read the full text and its Scripture proofs at The Westminster Standard (opens in a new tab). These paragraphs are the original Westminster position, not the altered teaching of the 1788 American revision.

Honor, Pray, Pay, and Obey

The fifth commandment reaches to all who are over us by God’s ordinance, whether in family, church, or commonwealth (WLC 124 (opens in a new tab)).

It is the duty of people to pray for magistrates, to honor their persons, to pay them tribute and other dues, to obey their lawful commands, and to be subject to their authority, for conscience’ sake. Infidelity or difference in religion doth not make void the magistrate’s just and legal authority, nor free the people from their due obedience to him

Westminster Confession of Faith 23.4 (opens in a new tab)

A ruler who doesn’t believe, or believes falsely, is still a ruler. Peter wrote under a pagan emperor, Honour all men. Love the brotherhood. Fear God. Honour the king (1 Peter 2:17 (opens in a new tab)).

I exhort therefore, that, first of all, supplications, prayers, intercessions, and giving of thanks, be made for all men; For kings, and for all that are in authority; that we may lead a quiet and peaceable life in all godliness and honesty.

1 Timothy 2:1-2 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

First of all. Before we complain about rulers, we pray for them, and even give thanks. If a ruler seems foolish or wicked, pray for him anyway. Ask God to give him wisdom and to save his soul. Jeremiah told the exiles to seek the peace of Babylon, the city that had burned Jerusalem, and pray unto the Lord for it (Jeremiah 29:7 (opens in a new tab)).

Render therefore to all their dues, tribute to whom tribute is due (Romans 13:7 (opens in a new tab)). Cheating on taxes is stealing, even when the money will be spent badly. Obey the laws, not only for fear of being caught but for conscience sake (Romans 13:5 (opens in a new tab)). And Paul says to speak evil of no man (Titus 3:2 (opens in a new tab)). That includes the man in office you didn’t vote for.

When We Must Obey God Rather Than Men

Our obedience to rulers has a limit, and the limit is God. When the council commanded the apostles to stop preaching Jesus, Peter and John answered, Whether it be right in the sight of God to hearken unto you more than unto God, judge ye. For we cannot but speak the things which we have seen and heard (Acts 4:19-20 (opens in a new tab)). Brought back again, they answered in fewer words.

Then Peter and the other apostles answered and said, We ought to obey God rather than men.

Acts 5:29 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

The Hebrew midwives feared God, and did not as the king of Egypt commanded them, but saved the men children alive (Exodus 1:17 (opens in a new tab)), and God dealt well with them. Daniel kept praying three times a day, as he did aforetime, after a law forbade it (Daniel 6:10 (opens in a new tab)). And three young men wouldn't bow to a golden image.

If it be so, our God whom we serve is able to deliver us from the burning fiery furnace, and he will deliver us out of thine hand, O king. But if not, be it known unto thee, O king, that we will not serve thy gods, nor worship the golden image which thou hast set up.

Daniel 3:17-18 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

Look at how they disobeyed. They refused only what would have been sin, and they did it openly. Daniel still said, O king, live for ever (Daniel 6:21 (opens in a new tab)). They took up no sword. They left the outcome with God. If a ruler commands you to deny Christ, to worship falsely, or to take part in killing the innocent, you must refuse. Refuse humbly, and refuse firmly.

What about rulers who become tyrants? Calvin, at the close of his Institutes, taught that private persons aren't to rise up against their rulers on their own. But lesser magistrates, those who also hold lawful office, ought to restrain a ruler who oppresses the people. The Reformed tradition after him has held that resistance to tyranny belongs first to such lawful authorities and to lawful means. Paul, denied justice, used the law and said, I appeal unto Caesar (Acts 25:11 (opens in a new tab)). Bring hard questions of this kind to your elders before you act.

The Christian Citizen

Most of us are citizens, not rulers. Where the law gives us a voice, a Christian may vote, petition, and speak in public with a conscience shaped by Scripture. Our rulers are to maintain piety, justice, and peace (WCF 23.2 (opens in a new tab)). They're subject to God's law even when they don't acknowledge him. When choosing a magistrate, consider his duties before God, what his office requires, and whether your own participation would compromise obedience to Christ. Don't let party loyalty settle a matter that belongs to conscience. Ask your elders for counsel where you're unsure. The 1647 Confession sets out the underlying duties, though it doesn't prescribe the voting rules of any modern republic.

Righteousness exalteth a nation, but sin is a reproach to any people (Proverbs 14:34 (opens in a new tab)). It’s right to ask rulers to protect life and punish evil. See What Christians Should Do about abortion for one plain case.

Put Not Your Trust in Princes

Put not your trust in princes, nor in the son of man, in whom there is no help. His breath goeth forth, he returneth to his earth; in that very day his thoughts perish.

Psalm 146:3-4 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

Every leader you hope in will die. If your peace rises and falls with an election, your trust is in a prince. Ask yourself. Do I feel more joy when my side wins than when a sinner repents? Do I despise the people who vote the other way?

The wrath of man worketh not the righteousness of God (James 1:20 (opens in a new tab)). Rage at the news and contempt for opponents are sins, not zeal. Your political opponent is a neighbor made in God’s image, and perhaps a brother in Christ. Love him.

Keep the church above party. She must preach the whole Word, including what God says to rulers. And the Confession keeps her assemblies from meddling in civil affairs beyond humble petition and advice (WCF 31.5 (opens in a new tab)). Her message is, Be ye reconciled to God (2 Corinthians 5:20 (opens in a new tab)). When she becomes the chaplain of a party, she trades it for a smaller one.

The Throne That Matters Most

On the morning of the day he died, Jesus stood before a Roman governor who asked whether he was a king.

Jesus answered, My kingdom is not of this world: if my kingdom were of this world, then would my servants fight, that I should not be delivered to the Jews: but now is my kingdom not from hence.

John 18:36 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

Not of this world means his kingdom doesn’t come from here. It isn’t won by votes or held by soldiers. It spreads by the Word and the Spirit. Yet he rules over all. He told Pilate, Thou couldest have no power at all against me, except it were given thee from above (John 19:11 (opens in a new tab)).

Then the soldiers crowned him with thorns and mocked him, Hail, King of the Jews (Matthew 27:29 (opens in a new tab)). Pilate put a title on the cross, THE KING OF THE JEWS (John 19:19 (opens in a new tab)). They meant it as a joke, but it was true. The King was lifted up on a cross, bearing the wrath of God for rebels. He won his kingdom not by taking his enemies’ lives but by giving his own for them. On the third day he rose, and now he sits at the right hand of God.

Every one of us has been a rebel against this King. The command to kiss the Son is spoken to you before any president. Turn from your sin and trust him. Blessed are all they that put their trust in him (Psalm 2:12 (opens in a new tab)). He is the prince of the kings of the earth, and in the same breath John says he loved us, and washed us from our sins in his own blood (Revelation 1:5 (opens in a new tab)). If you don’t know him yet, read the gospel.

The throne that matters most isn’t in any capital. It’s the one Christ sits on now, where he gathers and defends his church, subdues her enemies, and makes intercession for his people (WLC 54 (opens in a new tab)). He removeth kings, and setteth up kings (Daniel 2:21 (opens in a new tab)). But of the increase of his government and peace there shall be no end (Isaiah 9:7 (opens in a new tab)). We have received a kingdom which cannot be moved (Hebrews 12:28 (opens in a new tab)).

What Christians Should Do

  1. Kiss the Son yourself. Before you ask anything of your rulers, bow to Christ. Confess where politics has ruled your heart more than he has.
  2. Pray for your rulers by name. Pray this week for the president, your governor, and one local official, and give thanks for them. See How to Pray.
  3. Pay and obey with a clean conscience. If you’ve cheated on taxes or broken a just law, confess it and set it right.
  4. Watch your mouth. Speak of officials with respect, even in disagreement. Stop passing on what mocks or slanders.
  5. Take part with care. Vote, write, and speak as a servant of Christ, with the counsel of your elders.
  6. Know your line. Settle now what you will not do, whatever a ruler commands. Ask God for Daniel’s courage and Daniel’s respect.
  7. Turn off the noise. If the news feeds fear or rage, put it down and read Psalm 2 (opens in a new tab) instead.
