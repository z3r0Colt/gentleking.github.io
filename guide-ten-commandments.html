The Ten Commandments for a Believer

Why a forgiven Christian still keeps God’s law, how to read it, what each commandment requires and forbids, and how to use them this week.

Why a Believer Keeps the Law

You aren’t saved by keeping the commandments. Christ kept them in your place, and he bore the curse for every one you broke (Galatians 3:13). A believer is free from the law as a way to earn life.

But he isn’t free from the law as a rule for how to live. The Confession says the moral law binds all men forever, believers included. It's a rule of life that shows them the will of God and their duty (WCF 19.5-6). The law tells a forgiven man what love looks like.

If ye love me, keep my commandments.

John 14:15 (KJV)

So you obey because you’re loved, never to become loved. And when you fail, the law sends you back to Christ. That’s one of the things it’s for.

How to Read Them

The Larger Catechism gives rules for reading the commandments (Q&A 99). Four of them do most of the work.

  1. They reach the heart. The law governs thoughts and desires, not only actions. Jesus said lust is adultery in the heart and hatred is murder in the heart (Matthew 5:21-28).
  2. Every command has two sides. Where a sin is forbidden, the opposite duty is commanded. Where a duty is commanded, the opposite sin is forbidden. Thou shalt not steal also means work, and give (Ephesians 4:28).
  3. One sin stands for a whole family. Murder covers anger, contempt, and reckless driving. Adultery covers lust, pornography, and immodesty.
  4. The causes and occasions are forbidden too. Whatever leads you toward the sin is part of what the command forbids.

Each Commandment in Plain Words

The Shorter Catechism sets out what each one requires and forbids (Q&A 45-81). This is a short version.

  1. Thou shalt have no other gods before me. Know, worship, trust, and love the true God alone. Forbidden are atheism, false gods, and anything you love or trust more than him, including money, comfort, and approval.
  2. Thou shalt not make unto thee any graven image. Worship God only in the ways he has commanded in his Word. Forbidden are images of God and any worship men invent for themselves. This is where the Reformed regulative principle of worship comes from (WCF 21.1). See How God Wants to Be Worshiped.
  3. Thou shalt not take the name of the Lord thy God in vain. Use God’s names, titles, Word, and works with reverence. Forbidden are cursing, careless use of his name, false oaths, and mocking holy things.
  4. Remember the sabbath day, to keep it holy. Set apart one whole day in seven for God, now the Lord’s Day, the first day of the week. Rest from ordinary work and recreation for public and private worship, with works of necessity and mercy (WCF 21.8). See How to Keep the Lord’s Day.
  5. Honour thy father and thy mother. Give honor and obedience to parents and to everyone God has set over you, in the home, the church, and the state, in all lawful things. No human authority may command disobedience to God. Care for those under you. Forbidden are contempt, rebellion, and abuse of authority.
  6. Thou shalt not kill. Preserve your own life and the lives of others. Forbidden are murder, abortion, suicide, unjust anger, hatred, and neglect of your body or anyone in your care.
  7. Thou shalt not commit adultery. Keep yourself and others chaste in heart, speech, and behavior. Husband and wife must give each other conjugal love and live together faithfully (WLC 138). Forbidden are sex outside of marriage, pornography, lustful thoughts, and immodest dress or talk.
  8. Thou shalt not steal. Get and keep goods honestly, and work to provide for yourself and others. Forbidden are theft, cheating, laziness, waste, and gambling away what God gave you.
  9. Thou shalt not bear false witness against thy neighbour. Speak the truth and guard your neighbor’s good name. Forbidden are lies, slander, gossip, and flattery.
  10. Thou shalt not covet. Be content with what God gave you and glad for your neighbor’s good. Forbidden are envy, discontent, and wanting what is his.

The full text is in Exodus 20:1-17 and Deuteronomy 5:6-21.

The God Who Commands Is the God Who Redeems

Exodus 20 begins with God's identity and his deliverance of Israel from Egypt. He had already brought his people out of bondage when he declared the commandments. His authority rests on who he is, and his redemption supplies a further reason for their obedience. WLC 101 draws out both truths. We owe obedience to our Creator, and those he redeems owe him grateful service.

The law's demand is perfect. James 2:10–11 teaches that the same God stands behind every commandment, so obedience in one matter can't excuse rebellion in another. Our failure therefore exposes a deeper need than a better routine. We need forgiveness and a righteousness we haven't achieved. Christ fulfilled the law and bore its curse for his people (Galatians 3:13, Romans 5:18–19).

Through faith in him, believers are justified freely and receive the Spirit who renews them in obedience. Romans 8:1–4 joins freedom from condemnation with the Spirit's work in those who belong to Christ. God's law continues to identify the good they should pursue. Love for God and neighbor summarizes the commandments rather than replacing their particular requirements (Matthew 22:37–40, Romans 13:8–10).

Read the explanations below in that order. They show real duties, expose real sins, and give direction for a life of gratitude. They aren't a ladder by which an unbeliever can climb into God's favor. If the law shows that you stand guilty, come to Christ for salvation rather than postponing faith until you've improved.

1. Know and Love the True God

The first commandment requires the whole person to acknowledge the Lord as God. Knowing that he exists is only the beginning. We owe him trust, love, fear, praise, and willing obedience. Deuteronomy 6:4–5 calls Israel to love the one Lord with the whole heart, soul, and strength. WLC 104 explains the breadth of that devotion.

This requires learning who God has revealed himself to be. We can't replace his holiness, justice, or mercy with a version that suits us. It also requires a confession that reaches our decisions. When telling the truth threatens your standing at work, God's command still governs. When providence disappoints your plans, you should bring your grief to him while refusing the conclusion that he has ceased to be wise or good.

WLC 105 includes both using unlawful means and placing ultimate trust in lawful means among the sins against this commandment. Working, saving, and receiving medical care are ordinary responsibilities. Depending on a job, savings, or a person as though God weren't Lord of your life is a different matter. Receive his gifts thankfully and use them under his authority.

Ask what you're willing to disobey God to obtain or preserve. That question can expose an affection that has become disordered. Repentance includes returning to the particular duty you've displaced, while asking God to teach you to delight in him.

2. Receive God's Appointed Worship

The first commandment concerns the God we worship. The second also governs how we worship him. In Exodus 32:1–8, the making of the calf and the announced feast to the Lord didn't produce acceptable worship. Attaching God's name to a human invention couldn't authorize it. The Lord's own revelation must govern our approach.

Receive the worship he has appointed and keep it whole. Read and hear Scripture, pray, sing his praise, and receive the sacraments according to Christ's institution. WLC 108 includes church government, discipline, and the support of the ministry among these duties. You can violate this commandment by neglecting God's ordinances as well as by introducing false worship.

The prohibition includes representations of any person of the Godhead (WLC 109). Christ's incarnation doesn't authorize an invented portrait of his person. We know and receive him through the means he gave, especially the Word and sacraments. Sincerity and an intention to help children don't establish authority for a representation Scripture forbids.

A lawful arrangement for a service, such as its meeting time, differs from an added act offered as worship. Both require judgment, but only the latter introduces a new religious act. The worship guide explains that distinction and the biblical case for singing the inspired Psalms without instruments.

3. Honor God's Name in Word and Life

God makes himself known through his names, attributes, Word, works, and ordinances. The third commandment requires reverent use of all of them (WLC 112). You honor his name when you speak truthfully about him, receive his Word seriously, keep lawful promises, and live consistently with the faith you profess.

Profanity is one violation, but the command reaches much further. Quoting Scripture to excuse a lie, claiming God's authority for your private preference, or making a religious profession to gain customers also abuses his name. Romans 2:21–24 warns that a profession contradicted by conduct brings dishonor to God. Examine what your conduct tells the people who know you're a Christian.

Christ's warning about careless swearing in Matthew 5:33–37 requires ordinary speech to be truthful. It doesn't abolish a solemn lawful oath on a proper occasion. WCF 22 distinguishes reverent swearing by God's name from rash or sinful oaths. A lawful vow binds you to what is good, just, and within your ability to perform. A promise to do evil must be repented of rather than fulfilled.

Take care when explaining providence too. You can confess that God rules an event without claiming to know his undisclosed reason for it. Don't tell a suffering neighbor that a particular calamity proves a particular hidden sin. Speak what God has revealed and leave his secrets with him.

4. Keep the Whole Lord's Day Holy

God claims one whole day in seven for holy rest and worship. The command gives a rhythm to our labor and places a limit on treating every available hour as ours to spend. After Christ's resurrection, the Christian Sabbath is the first day of the week (WCF 21.7). Public worship belongs at its center, with private and family worship occupying the day alongside duties of necessity and mercy.

This includes resting from ordinary recreation as well as ordinary employment. Something can be lawful on another day and unsuitable for the Lord's Day. WLC 117–119 also names careless worship, avoidable business, and needless worldly conversation among the ways people profane the day. Merely staying away from work doesn't fulfill the positive duty to set the day apart to God.

Prepare your affairs so you and those under your care can participate. Exodus 20:10 expressly includes the household and its servants. WLC 118 therefore places a particular duty on those in authority, who can hinder others by demands for their own convenience. Don't arrange your rest by unnecessarily burdening someone else.

Christ defends necessary provision and mercy in Matthew 12:1–13. Care for a sick person and help for someone in danger belong within obedience to the command. Plan ordinary work beforehand where you can, meet genuine needs when they arise, and seek practical help when recurring care limits attendance. Keeping the Lord's Day develops those decisions.

5. Honor Authority and Exercise It Faithfully

God begins with the relationship of children and parents, then teaches duties within other lawful relationships of authority. Children should receive instruction and correction and obey their parents in the Lord. Ephesians 6:1–4 places that command beside the father's duty to bring children up in the Lord's nurture and admonition. An authority's failure doesn't erase every duty of those under him, and their duty doesn't excuse his failure.

WLC 127–130 requires willing obedience to lawful commands and care from those who govern. A parent, elder, employer, or magistrate must use the authority of his particular office for its proper end. He should instruct, protect, provide, encourage what is good, and correct what is wrong. Harshness and a refusal to correct are both failures of responsibility.

Obedience isn't limited to directions you would've chosen. Receive lawful correction humbly and carry out lawful duties faithfully. Where a command requires sin, obey God rather than man (Acts 5:29). Where the dispute concerns facts or judgment, give a truthful account and use the proper process rather than assuming disagreement releases you from submission.

Honor also continues when parents grow old and depend on their children. First Timothy 5:4, 8 requires practical care within the family. Adults aren't placed under every childhood arrangement indefinitely, but maturity doesn't authorize contempt or abandonment. Give the help genuinely needed according to your responsibilities and ability. Parenting and church membership explain these duties in their respective settings.

6. Preserve Life and Seek Peace

Human life belongs to God, and people bear his image (Genesis 9:6). The command forbids unjust killing and requires active care for our own life and our neighbor's. WLC 135 includes food, rest, medicine, lawful defense, compassionate speech, and help for the distressed. Ordinary provision and kindness are moral duties, even when no dramatic emergency is present.

Christ exposes murderous anger and contempt in Matthew 5:21–24. You must put away hatred, deliberate humiliation, and the desire to repay injury with harm. Where you've wronged someone, pursue a truthful repair. Peace requires more than avoiding a physical blow while continuing to threaten or degrade a person.

Necessary defense differs from private revenge. WLC 136 distinguishes murder from public justice, lawful war, and necessary defense. Those exceptions don't give individuals unrestricted authority to punish people they dislike. The actual responsibility, necessity, and lawful limits matter. Seek protection when danger is present and leave civil judgment to the proper authorities.

Care for the life entrusted to you also limits excessive work and reckless self-neglect. Receive needed help and make responsible provision for dependents. Illness isn't itself a moral failure, and preserving life doesn't mean that every sickness can be prevented. The duty is to use lawful and appropriate means, with qualified help where needed, rather than deliberately withholding necessary care.

7. Guard Chastity and Marital Faithfulness

God requires sexual purity in thought, affection, speech, and conduct. Christ teaches that deliberately looking with lust already violates the command in the heart (Matthew 5:27–28). Pornography, flirtation that cultivates an unlawful attachment, and sexually corrupt conversation therefore deserve repentance before an outward act of adultery occurs.

The positive duties include faithful marriage, conjugal love, and living together as husband and wife (WLC 138–139). First Corinthians 7:3–5 gives husband and wife a reciprocal marital duty and forbids defrauding one another. Abstinence for prayer is ordinarily by mutual agreement and for a time. Neither spouse should use affection or intimacy as a weapon, or treat the other's needs as unimportant.

That obligation doesn't authorize forced sex or threats. Pain, illness, disability, trauma, and postpartum recovery can create genuine impediments requiring patience and appropriate care. Persistent neglect and genuine inability aren't the same thing. The marriage guide explains the full duty and its application more carefully.

Unmarried believers should practice chastity and consider lawful marriage honestly, without treating marriage as either a universal requirement or something to delay irresponsibly when it is needed. Guard the opportunities that encourage sin and pursue wholesome relationships and useful work. Temptation should lead you to seek help and resist it, rather than conclude that obedience must wait until desire disappears.

8. Deal Justly with What Belongs to Others

God requires honest work, just contracts, faithful handling of another person's property, and generosity according to ability and need (WLC 141). Ephesians 4:28 tells the thief to stop stealing, labor honestly, and have something to share. The change concerns both how he obtains goods and what he does with them.

An employer must pay what is due and avoid exploiting a worker's vulnerability. An employee must perform the agreed work honestly. A borrower should repay what he owes, and someone entrusted with money must use it for its proper purpose. A small theft doesn't become lawful because the owner is wealthy or the loss is difficult to detect.

The command also forbids waste and greed that injure your own household. Taking unnecessary financial risks with money needed for dependents violates stewardship as well as prudence. Conversely, responsible provision isn't the same as hoarding everything while refusing a neighbor's genuine need. The money guide discusses giving, debts, and necessary household provision together.

When poverty, disability, or unemployment limits what you can earn, seek lawful work within your ability and receive appropriate help. Second Thessalonians 3:10 rebukes unwillingness to work, rather than declaring every person unable to earn a living idle. The church's duty of material care belongs beside the individual's duty of diligence. Neither should erase the other.

9. Speak Truth and Guard a Good Name

The command governs our care for truth and for our neighbor's reputation. In a dispute, give an accurate account, distinguish what you witnessed from what you inferred, and hear a person's answer before judging him. WLC 144–145 forbids both false accusation and the silence that allows injustice to continue when a reproof or complaint is required.

Truthfulness includes correcting an impression you've knowingly created. If you omitted the fact that would clear an innocent colleague, a technically accurate sentence may still have served deception. Tell the relevant people what they need to know to judge the matter fairly. When you've spread a false report, correct it with its actual audience.

Not every true fact belongs in every conversation. Private weaknesses shouldn't become entertainment, and a malicious retelling can misuse something that actually happened. Ask whether the audience has a proper reason to know and whether your purpose serves truth, justice, and your neighbor's good.

Those limits don't justify covering up wrongdoing. A truthful report to someone responsible for protection, church discipline, or civil justice can fulfill the command. Don't call it gossip merely because the facts are damaging. Likewise, don't use the possibility of wrongdoing to excuse unsupported public accusations. Justice requires both a willingness to hear a serious concern and care with the evidence.

10. Be Content and Rejoice in Your Neighbor's Good

The final commandment names desires before they become outward acts. You may refrain from taking a neighbor's property while resenting that he has it. You may speak politely while wishing his success would collapse. WLC 147–148 requires contentment with your condition and a charitable heart toward all the good that belongs to him.

Paul says that the command against coveting exposed sin he hadn't rightly understood (Romans 7:7–13). God's law reaches beneath respectable conduct to our inward opposition to him. That discovery should destroy self-righteous confidence and lead us to Christ. It shouldn't make us abandon the fight against envy.

Contentment doesn't forbid lawful improvement, asking for fair treatment, or seeking relief from hardship. Paul learned contentment in both abundance and need (Philippians 4:11–13), while continuing his work and receiving help. You can seek a better job without hating someone who has one, and you can resist injustice without charging God with wrongdoing.

When another person's good exposes your envy, confess it specifically. Thank God for the mercy he has shown that person and refuse to undermine it through speech or conduct. Attend to the opportunities and responsibilities actually before you. A heart increasingly satisfied in God can work diligently and rejoice when a neighbor prospers.

Apply the Commandments to a Real Decision

Suppose your employer asks you to describe a product in a way you know is false. The fifth commandment ordinarily requires diligence and lawful obedience in your work. The ninth requires truthfulness, and the eighth forbids gaining money through deception. Honoring an employer can't make the dishonest claim lawful. Explain the problem respectfully and offer an accurate description you can give instead.

Now consider a different temptation. You've made a mistake at work and want to blame a colleague. Truthfulness requires acknowledging your part. Love for your neighbor requires protecting his reputation from the false impression. Faithfulness in work requires helping repair the damage. Avoiding one direct lie while allowing everyone to blame him still fails these duties.

The commandments help you see the whole obligation. They don't supply a license to choose whichever duty is most convenient. When circumstances are complicated, identify what is actually commanded, what is forbidden, and who has authority over the decision. Seek wise counsel about the facts without asking someone else to make deliberate wrongdoing acceptable.

Our obedience remains imperfect. The law exposes that imperfection and directs us to Christ, while continuing to show the life we owe God. WCF 19.6 explains these uses together. A believer should neither try to purchase justification by obedience nor dismiss God's instructions because justification is free.

Learn the Duties as Well as the Prohibitions

Take the eighth commandment as an example. It forbids taking what belongs to someone else, but it also calls for honest work, fair dealing, responsible use of what you have, and appropriate help for a neighbor in need. Ephesians 4:28 joins the thief's departure from stealing with labor and generosity. Merely avoiding theft doesn't exhaust the command.

The ninth commandment likewise requires more than avoiding statements you know are false. You should be willing to receive a truthful correction, speak in defense of someone falsely accused, and refuse to pass on an unverified damaging report. Guarding a good name never requires suppressing a truthful report to an authority responsible for addressing harm. Truth, justice, and the proper audience matter together.

Use the Larger Catechism's detailed treatment when a short summary leaves you uncertain. Its rules in question 99 include duties suited to our places and callings and the principle that every particular duty isn't required at every moment. A parent, an employer, and a civil officer have different responsibilities. Each must obey the same moral law within the responsibilities God has actually given.

Choose a neglected positive duty alongside a sin you need to leave. Someone fighting covetousness can practice contentment and thank God for a neighbor's good. Someone resisting contempt can learn to speak honorably and provide needed care. This makes examination a way toward actual obedience.

How to Use Them This Week

  1. Learn them. Memorize the ten, then the Shorter Catechism’s questions on each. Teach them to your children.
  2. Examine yourself by them. Take one commandment each evening for ten days. Ask what it requires and what it forbids, and where you stand. See How to Examine Yourself.
  3. Pray them. Turn each one into a request for help to obey, and a confession where you haven’t.
  4. Run to Christ. No mere man since the fall is able in this life perfectly to keep the commandments of God (WSC 82). When the law shows you your sin, it’s pointing you to the Savior who kept it for you.

If one commandment keeps exposing the same sin, Struggling With Sin is written for that fight.
