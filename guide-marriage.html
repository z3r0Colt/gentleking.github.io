What the Bible Says About Marriage

Husband and wife owe faithful love, mutual marital duty, and their distinct biblical responsibilities. Guidance on headship, submission, disagreement, singleness, and protection from abuse.

About 23 minutes to read

Marriage calls husband and wife to faithful love, shared responsibilities, and honesty about problems. Start with the section that fits your circumstances. If you're afraid of your husband or wife, read this first, and get safe. If your marriage is breaking, read When Your Marriage Is Breaking. If you’re married to someone who doesn’t believe, read the part for you. If you’re single and wish you weren’t, there’s a word for you. If the pain at home has led to thoughts of suicide, tell someone safe and use the immediate help for those thoughts. In the United States you can call or text 988. Seek emergency care if you're in immediate danger.

For an ordinary disagreement, choose a time when you can both listen. Name the actual decision, such as an expense or a household duty, and hear each other before deciding what happens next. If the same conflict keeps returning, ask for trustworthy counsel. Fear, threats, or coercion require protection rather than another attempt at a private conversation.

God Made Marriage

Marriage wasn’t invented by the church or the state. God made it in the garden, before there was any sin in the world.

And the Lord God said, It is not good that the man should be alone; I will make him an help meet for him.

Genesis 2:18 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

This was the first thing in creation God called not good. Adam had no sin and walked with God, and still he shouldn’t be alone. So God made the woman from the man’s own side and brought her to him.

Therefore shall a man leave his father and his mother, and shall cleave unto his wife: and they shall be one flesh.

Genesis 2:24 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

This is history, not a fable. Jesus pointed back to it as something that happened. He which made them at the beginning made them male and female, he said. Then he added, What therefore God hath joined together, let not man put asunder (Matthew 19:4-6 (opens in a new tab)). In a wedding it’s God who joins.

Marriage is to be between one man and one woman: neither is it lawful for any man to have more than one wife, nor for any woman to have more than one husband at the same time.

Westminster Confession of Faith 24.1 (opens in a new tab)

Some patriarchs and kings took more than one wife, and the grief in the homes of Jacob and David shows what came of it. In answering a question about divorce, Jesus pointed back to the beginning (Matthew 19:4-8 (opens in a new tab)). His argument establishes the created pattern rather than treating every marriage described in biblical history as an example to follow. One man and one woman, for as long as they both live, is God’s design. For the same reason, the union of two men or two women isn’t a marriage, whatever a law may call it. That’s taken up with care in What the Bible Says About Homosexuality and Gender.

Why God Gave It

Marriage was ordained for the mutual help of husband and wife; for the increase of mankind with a legitimate issue, and of the Church with an holy seed; and for preventing of uncleanness.

Westminster Confession of Faith 24.2 (opens in a new tab)

The first purpose is help. A help meet is a helper fit for him. Help is no small word. God uses it of himself. He is our help and our shield (Psalm 33:20 (opens in a new tab)). The second purpose is children, raised as a godly seed for God (Malachi 2:15 (opens in a new tab)). The third is purity. To avoid fornication, let every man have his own wife, and let every woman have her own husband (1 Corinthians 7:2 (opens in a new tab)). God calls the marriage bed undefiled (Hebrews 13:4 (opens in a new tab)), and there's more on it in What the Bible Says About Sex.

A Picture of Christ and the Church

Paul quotes Genesis 2 (opens in a new tab) to husbands and wives, and then says something astonishing.

For this cause shall a man leave his father and mother, and shall be joined unto his wife, and they two shall be one flesh. This is a great mystery: but I speak concerning Christ and the church.

Ephesians 5:31-32 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

A mystery in Scripture is something long hidden and now made known. God made marriage to point past itself. Christ is the Bridegroom, and his people are his bride (John 3:29 (opens in a new tab), 2 Corinthians 11:2 (opens in a new tab)). A husband and wife made one flesh are a living picture of Christ and his church made one.

The Larger Catechism says believers are spiritually and mystically, yet really and inseparably, joined to Christ as their head and husband (WLC 66 (opens in a new tab)). That union is closer and more lasting than any husband’s with his wife. An earthly marriage is real in its own right, and it also points to Christ's covenant love for his church.

Paul directs us to Christ's sacrifice as the model for a husband's love. Christ also loved the church, and gave himself for it (Ephesians 5:25 (opens in a new tab)). He didn’t find a lovely bride. He found one stained with sin, and he bore the wrath of God in her place on the cross. On the third day he rose, that he might present it to himself a glorious church, not having spot, or wrinkle (Ephesians 5:27 (opens in a new tab)). If you’ve never come to him, read The Gospel, and come as you are.

Marriage Is for This Age

Because marriage is a picture, it won’t last forever. In the resurrection they neither marry, nor are given in marriage, but are as the angels of God in heaven (Matthew 22:30 (opens in a new tab)). Love doesn’t end there. Marriage ends, because what it pointed to will have come.

Let us be glad and rejoice, and give honour to him: for the marriage of the Lamb is come, and his wife hath made herself ready.

Revelation 19:7 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

Blessed are they which are called unto the marriage supper of the Lamb (Revelation 19:9 (opens in a new tab)). Christ will come for his people, and they’ll be with him forever. Every Christian wedding is a small rehearsal for that day. So no Christian should make marriage his god, and no single believer has missed the one marriage that lasts.

The Husband’s Love and the Wife’s Submission

Wives, submit yourselves unto your own husbands, as unto the Lord. For the husband is the head of the wife, even as Christ is the head of the church: and he is the saviour of the body. Therefore as the church is subject unto Christ, so let the wives be to their own husbands in every thing.

Ephesians 5:22-24 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

Many readers stop at the word submit, but Paul goes straight on. Husbands, love your wives, even as Christ also loved the church, and gave himself for it (Ephesians 5:25 (opens in a new tab)). The wife is to follow as the church follows Christ. The husband is to love as Christ loved, all the way to a cross. Both bear God’s image (Genesis 1:27 (opens in a new tab)), and both are heirs together of the grace of life (1 Peter 3:7 (opens in a new tab)). They differ in calling, not in worth.

What Headship Is

A husband’s headship gives him responsibility and lawful authority to lead his household. His wife owes respect and submission to that lawful leadership. He must use his authority for her good, listen to her counsel, and bear responsibility rather than leaving every hard decision to her. Jesus said the princes of the Gentiles exercise dominion over others, but it shall not be so among you (Matthew 20:25-26 (opens in a new tab)). He came not to be ministered unto, but to minister (Matthew 20:28 (opens in a new tab)). That’s a husband’s pattern. He leads his home in the worship of God (see How to Lead Family Worship). He provides for his house (1 Timothy 5:8 (opens in a new tab)). He nourishes and cherishes his wife as his own flesh (Ephesians 5:28-29 (opens in a new tab)), and honors her, that his prayers be not hindered (1 Peter 3:7 (opens in a new tab)). And he isn’t bitter against her (Colossians 3:19 (opens in a new tab)).

Headship is never tyranny. It gives no right to use threats, coercive control, isolation, or force against a wife. A man who does those things isn’t using too much headship. He is breaking the very command that gives it, and God will judge it. If your husband or wife hits you, threatens you, forces you, or makes you afraid, seek protection. Leaving can require a safety plan, especially if the person may retaliate. An abuse advocate can help you consider safer options without waiting for church permission. Read If someone is hurting you.

What Submission Is

A wife’s submission is a willing yielding to her own husband’s lead, given as unto the Lord. Peter says her true beauty is a meek and quiet spirit, which is in the sight of God of great price (1 Peter 3:4 (opens in a new tab)). Meek doesn’t mean weak. It means strength at rest in God.

Submission doesn’t mean every woman under every man. Paul says your own husbands. It never means joining a husband in sin. In every thing means everything that's his to direct, never disobeying God. Sapphira went along with her husband’s lie, and she was judged with him (Acts 5:1-10 (opens in a new tab)). We ought to obey God rather than men (Acts 5:29 (opens in a new tab)). Nor does it mean staying under violence. A wife who flees danger hasn’t stopped honoring her husband. Responsibility for violence belongs to the person committing it. A victim who cannot yet leave safely is not to blame for that person’s actions.

Marrying Only in the Lord

It is lawful for all sorts of people to marry who are able with judgment to give their consent. Yet it is the duty of Christians to marry only in the Lord.

Westminster Confession of Faith 24.3 (opens in a new tab)

The excerpt above gives the opening of WCF 24.3 (opens in a new tab). A widow, Paul says, is free to marry whom she will, only in the Lord (1 Corinthians 7:39 (opens in a new tab)). And to every believer, Be ye not unequally yoked together with unbelievers (2 Corinthians 6:14 (opens in a new tab)). A yoke joins two animals to pull one plow, and marriage is the closest yoke there is. Don’t begin a courtship with someone who doesn’t love Christ, hoping they’ll come around. Look for faith that shows in a life, not only in words.

If You're Married to an Unbeliever

Perhaps you came to Christ after you were married. Perhaps you married an unbeliever when you knew better, and have repented of it. A difference of belief alone does not dissolve a marriage. Paul addresses an unbelieving spouse willing to live with the believer. This instruction does not require remaining in danger, excusing abuse, or refusing civil protection. If any brother hath a wife that believeth not, and she be pleased to dwell with him, let him not put her away (1 Corinthians 7:12-13 (opens in a new tab)). The same is said to the wife. The unbelieving husband is sanctified by the wife (1 Corinthians 7:14 (opens in a new tab)). Sanctified here means set apart, not saved. Your children are holy, set apart to God in his covenant. But your spouse still needs saving. What knowest thou, O wife, whether thou shalt save thy husband (1 Corinthians 7:16 (opens in a new tab))? So keep praying.

Likewise, ye wives, be in subjection to your own husbands; that, if any obey not the word, they also may without the word be won by the conversation of the wives; While they behold your chaste conversation coupled with fear.

1 Peter 3:1-2 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

Conversation means way of life. Peter doesn’t tell such wives to preach at their husbands. He tells them to live a holy life in front of them, and that holds for a believing husband too. If your unbelieving spouse leaves and won’t come back, let him depart. A brother or a sister is not under bondage in such cases (1 Corinthians 7:15 (opens in a new tab)). If you feel alone in your own home, read If You Are Married and Alone.

Divorce and Remarriage

God says he hateth putting away, and in the same breath he rebukes men who dealt treacherously with the wife of their youth (Malachi 2:14-16 (opens in a new tab)). His anger falls on the one who breaks the covenant.

And I say unto you, Whosoever shall put away his wife, except it be for fornication, and shall marry another, committeth adultery: and whoso marrieth her which is put away doth commit adultery.

Matthew 19:9 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

The Confession warns that the corruption of man is apt to study arguments for putting a marriage asunder. In plain words, we’re quick to hunt for reasons, and every unhappy heart can find them. So it names only two causes that can dissolve the bond. One is adultery, which Jesus names here. The other is willful desertion that neither the church nor the government can remedy (WCF 24.6 (opens in a new tab)), which Paul speaks of in 1 Corinthians 7:15 (opens in a new tab). After a divorce for adultery, the innocent party may marry another, as if the offending party were dead (WCF 24.5 (opens in a new tab)).

Even where a ground exists, divorce is allowed, not commanded. Many who were wronged have forgiven a spouse who truly repented, and rebuilt. Safety comes first. In immediate danger, contact emergency services. An abuse advocate and qualified local lawyer can explain protective options and the risks of leaving. Civil protection does not require church permission. Divorce raises additional questions. The Confession calls for a public and orderly course rather than private self-judgment. Seek faithful elders’ counsel about biblical grounds and qualified legal advice about filings and deadlines. Don’t delay urgent legal protection while waiting for a church meeting. Faithful Reformed teachers differ on how far desertion reaches. Some limit it to a spouse who walks out and stays gone. Others count one who drives the other out by cruelty as a deserter too.

If you divorced or remarried wrongly in the past, confess it as sin. Christ’s blood covers it too. Don’t try to undo it by breaking the marriage you’re in now.

If your own marriage is coming apart, don’t stop here. Read What God has said about divorce, written for the hurting. If you’re the one who broke faith, read the part for you on that page.

Singleness as a Gift and a Calling

The apostle Paul wasn’t married, and neither was the Lord Jesus. No one may call a single life a half life.

For I would that all men were even as I myself. But every man hath his proper gift of God, one after this manner, and another after that. I say therefore to the unmarried and widows, It is good for them if they abide even as I.

1 Corinthians 7:7-8 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

Singleness is a gift because it frees a believer for the Lord. He that's unmarried careth for the things that belong to the Lord, how he may please the Lord (1 Corinthians 7:32 (opens in a new tab)). A single believer may give to the church and the gospel strength that a married man rightly gives his family. Paul says he writes this not to cast a snare upon you, but that ye may attend upon the Lord without distraction (1 Corinthians 7:35 (opens in a new tab)).

Both callings are good. The Larger Catechism warns against entangling vows of single life, and also against undue delay of marriage (WLC 139 (opens in a new tab)). Don’t put marriage off for years out of fear, or to chase comfort or a career.

If you long to marry and God hasn’t given it, the longing isn’t sin. God put that desire in Adam before the fall. Bring it to him honestly. He hasn’t forgotten you, and he’s no less your Father because you have no husband or wife. To eunuchs who feared they were a dry tree, cut off from family, he promised a place and a name better than of sons and of daughters (Isaiah 56:5 (opens in a new tab)). Read If You Are Single.

Living Together Before Marriage

Many couples now share a home and a bed before they marry. Some call it a sensible test. Some say they’re married in their hearts.

But marriage is a covenant made before God and witnesses, not a feeling in two hearts. Scripture calls a wife the wife of thy covenant (Malachi 2:14 (opens in a new tab)). To live as husband and wife without that covenant is fornication, and God says plainly, Flee fornication (1 Corinthians 6:18 (opens in a new tab)). A covenant is a promise made before you know how it will go. A trial run teaches two people to keep a way out.

If this is you, Jesus hasn’t turned his face from you. He met a woman at a well and offered her living water. Then he named her five husbands and told her plainly, he whom thou now hast is not thy husband (John 4:10 (opens in a new tab), 18). He told her the truth because he had come to save her. Confess it to God. Turn from sexual relations outside marriage and seek pastoral counsel about your next steps. Don’t rush into a dangerous or unsuitable marriage to solve a housing problem. If children, a lease, disability, or lack of money makes separate housing difficult, ask your church for practical help making a lawful and safe plan.

Living Together in Ordinary Days

A marriage is lived in mornings before work, bills that must be paid, meals, illness, and days when both people are tired. Loving your spouse is more than having a correct view of Ephesians 5 (opens in a new tab). It includes noticing what needs to be done and taking responsibility for it. The command to love your neighbor as yourself applies first to the neighbor who lives with you (Matthew 22:39 (opens in a new tab)).

Work, Money, and Time

Talk about who is handling the bills, cooking, appointments, and care for children or aging relatives. Don't assume an arrangement is fair merely because it has lasted for years. If one spouse's paid work hours change or health declines, revisit the arrangement together. A husband who provides financially must still care for his wife as a person. A wife who keeps the home deserves real appreciation for that work.

Set aside a regular time to look at the household money together. Be truthful about debts, income, purchases, and obligations. Secrecy about money can damage trust even before a bill becomes overdue. Choose a workable plan for necessary expenses and what you can afford to save or give. If bills are already past due, the money guide gives a place to begin. Don't make commitments in the other person's name without their knowledge or lawful consent.

Make room for time together that isn't entirely about problems or children. A walk, a meal, or a conversation after work may be more realistic than a costly evening out. Ask what helps your spouse feel heard, and make room for their answer. When schedules keep you apart, decide when you'll actually speak instead of leaving that time to chance.

When You Keep Having the Same Argument

Most couples disagree about something. A disagreement becomes destructive when people stop listening, hide the truth, mock one another, or use old wounds to win the next quarrel. Scripture calls us to be swift to hear and slow to speak (James 1:19 (opens in a new tab)). That applies when you believe you're right.

If an argument is getting heated and neither of you is in danger, say that you need a break and agree on a definite time to return to the discussion. Coming back matters. Walking away whenever a difficult question is raised can become another way to avoid responsibility. If your spouse is threatening or frightening you, don't negotiate a return to the conversation. Go to the safety guidance.

When you talk again, describe what happened without claiming you know every motive. Ask what your spouse understood and listen to the answer. Stay with one issue long enough to reach a real decision. If you have spoken harshly or lied, confess that particular sin without adding an excuse. If an apology is followed by the same conduct, ask what concrete change and accountability are needed. Repentance bears fruit over time (Matthew 3:8 (opens in a new tab)).

Some problems won't be resolved in a single conversation. You may need help from faithful elders or a qualified marriage counselor who will hear both people carefully. Counsel for ordinary conflict is different from dealing with coercion, threats, or abuse. Don't insist on joint counseling with an abusive spouse as the price of receiving help.

Affection, Sex, and Different Needs

Husband and wife owe one another the ordinary duties of marital intimacy. Paul tells both spouses to render what is due and not to defraud one another (1 Corinthians 7:3-5 (opens in a new tab)). Desire will not always be equal, and neither spouse should treat a passing lack of interest as a standing exemption from this obligation. The seventh commandment requires conjugal love and cohabitation, not merely the avoidance of adultery (Westminster Larger Catechism Q&A 138-139 (opens in a new tab)).

When one spouse keeps saying no while refusing to discuss or address the problem, the other spouse may rightly feel neglected. That concern deserves a serious hearing. Sexual closeness is one of the purposes for which God ordained marriage (WCF 24.2 (opens in a new tab) and 1 Corinthians 7:2 (opens in a new tab), 9). It isn't selfish for a husband to desire his wife or for a wife to desire her husband. Nor does Scripture make the continuation of ordinary marital intimacy dependent on both people feeling equally eager every time.

But the duty is to love and give, never to overpower or take. Illness, pain, exhaustion, postpartum recovery, disability, trauma, and significant relational harm can affect what is possible. One refusal does not establish a pattern of sinful withholding, and a spouse's activities elsewhere aren't enough by themselves to tell you what sex feels like for that person. Yet neither spouse should use an unexplained difficulty to dismiss the question indefinitely. They should look for the cause and seek appropriate care together.

Talk at a calm time about the longer pattern, not as a demand after a refusal. Explain how it affects the marriage and ask what is making closeness difficult. Listen to the answer and consider a realistic plan for affection, time together, or medical and pastoral help where needed. Don't assume the other spouse will infer the problem from silence. Marriage asks both people to consider the other's needs and to make a sincere effort for the other's good.

Affection outside the marriage bed also matters. A touch, an ordinary kindness, a conversation, or an expression of gratitude shouldn't always carry an expectation of sex. But affectionate gestures don't cancel the duty of sexual intimacy either. For a full explanation of 1 Corinthians 7 (opens in a new tab), including mutually agreed temporary abstinence and the problem of withholding, read Mutual Duty and Tenderness in the Marriage Bed. Sexual coercion or threats are sins, not a lawful exercise of marital rights, and someone facing them may seek protection.

When Life Changes the Marriage

Marriage is affected by sickness, infertility, disability, bereavement, children leaving home, lost employment, and caring for aging parents. Sometimes a spouse needs more practical help than either expected when vows were made. The promise to remain faithful matters during these seasons, but the care required can change. Ask what is actually possible rather than measuring today's marriage against a healthier or easier year.

If one spouse is unwell, don't use Scripture to demand the same level of work or intimacy from them as before. Ask doctors about real limitations and treatment, and ask the church whether members can help with food, transport, or household tasks. If caregiving is taking over your days, read When You Are Caring for Someone. If infertility has become a source of sorrow or conflict, see the infertility and IVF guide.

When one spouse is grieving and the other doesn't understand, listen before trying to correct the sorrow. You may not grieve the same way. A faithful marriage requires patience with a wounded person, not a deadline by which they must be well. If the relationship has grown distant, When You're Married and Alone addresses that particular pain.

When to Ask for Help

Ask for counsel when a problem keeps returning, you cannot speak without contempt, you suspect dishonesty, or you cannot agree on responsibilities that affect the household. It's better to seek help while both spouses are able to listen than after years of resentment. Tell a pastor or counselor the particular problem rather than asking them to decide who is the better Christian.

For repeated ordinary conflict, both spouses may benefit from careful pastoral or professional marriage counseling. Each person should be heard, and a counselor should not treat the husband's office as permission to dismiss the wife's concerns. If one person is being harmed or controlled, a separate safety assessment is needed. When Your Marriage Is Breaking deals with betrayal, separation, and difficult decisions. Threats and violence belong first to immediate safety.

Counsel for Your Marriage

For Husbands

  1. Lead your home in worship. Start tonight with a chapter of Scripture, a psalm, and prayer.
  2. Ask your wife how she is, and listen. Dwell with her according to knowledge (1 Peter 3:7 (opens in a new tab)). Pursue closeness and fulfill your marital duties without threats or selfishness.
  3. Repent of harshness. Confess bitterness or coldness by name, to God and to her, without excuses.
  4. Share the work of caring for the household. Ask what is most burdensome and take responsibility for a definite task. Account for illness, paid work, and the need for rest rather than treating her time as endlessly available.

For Wives

  1. Honor your husband with your words. Speak truthfully and kindly (Proverbs 31:26 (opens in a new tab)). This never requires hiding abuse, lying about conduct, or keeping a concern from someone who can help.
  2. Follow his lead gladly where it’s right to follow, and tell him gently and plainly where you can’t.
  3. Pray for him by name every day. If he doesn’t know Christ, pray for that above all. Take his need for affection and marital intimacy seriously, just as he must take yours seriously.
  4. If you’re afraid of him, none of this means staying in danger. Get safe first.

For Those Preparing to Marry

  1. Make sure you’re both in the Lord, in life and not only in name. Ask people who know you both.
  2. Ask your pastor for counsel before the wedding. Talk through faith, money, children, and how you will settle a quarrel.
  3. Keep yourselves pure until the wedding night. If you’ve already fallen, confess it and begin again today.
  4. Learn what you’re promising. Read Ephesians 5:22-33 (opens in a new tab) together, and chapter 24 of the Confession, before you make your vows.

Married or single, set your hope on the Bridegroom. He is faithful, and he will come for his bride.
