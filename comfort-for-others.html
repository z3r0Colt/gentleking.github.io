For pastors and friends

If You Came Here for Someone Else

You want to help, and you are afraid of saying the wrong thing. Most of the help is simply being there.

In danger, or thinking of ending your life? Call 911, or call 988 or text 988, now. If someone at home is hurting you, read If You Are Not Safe. Outside the United States, call your local emergency number.

So they sat down with him upon the ground seven days and seven nights, and none spake a word unto him: for they saw that his grief was very great.

Job 2:13 (KJV)

Job’s friends did well while they were silent. When they began to explain his suffering, God rebuked them (Job 42:7). Weep with them that weep (Romans 12:15). You do not have to explain anything. Leave out the easy sayings, such as everything happens for a reason, or God needed another angel. Say I am so sorry, and I am here.

The comfort you carry is not your own. It is Christ’s, who died and rose again for his people, and who comforts us so that we can comfort others.

  1. Go and sit with them. Bring food if you like, but mostly bring yourself. Pray with them briefly, if they want you to.
  2. Say the name. Speak of the one who died by name. Most mourners long to hear it.
  3. Keep coming. The meals stop after a few weeks, and the hardest months come after that. Put the birthday and the day they died in your calendar.
  4. Send only what fits. Find their sorrow in the list on the Comfort page, open it, then copy the address from the top of your browser and send it. Send the page that fits what they have told you, not what you suspect. Do not send the pages about uncertain faith or suicide unless they raised them themselves.

People who lose someone to suicide carry a higher risk themselves, and so do people in long pain. If you fear they are thinking of ending their life, ask them plainly. Asking does not put the thought there. If you fear for anyone’s safety, stay with them and call 988 or text 988 together.

For pastors and elders, the longer visit

These pages are only a first word for the hard hour. The longer work belongs to the church, and first to her elders (Acts 20:28). What follows is a layman’s summary, offered as a help and not as a rule for any session. The Westminster Directory for Public Worship, in its counsel for visiting the sick, joins comfort with a search of the heart and ways, and then sets before the troubled the freeness of God’s grace in Christ.

  1. See that they are safe. Ask plainly whether they have thought of ending their life. If they have, do not leave them alone. Call 988 or text 988 with them, and help them give anything they might use to harm themselves to someone to keep. Ask whether anyone at home is hurting them, but only where no one from their home can hear. If someone is, help them get safe, and never bring them to face the one who hurt them. If a child is being hurt, report it to the police or child protective services that day, before anyone at church acts. In many states the law requires it. Abuse is a crime, and the church does not handle it alone (Romans 13:4).
  2. Set Christ before them. Read the Scripture with them, and pray with them (James 5:14). Speak of his tears at the grave of Lazarus (John 11:35), his finished work on the cross, his prayers for all who come to God by him (Hebrews 7:25), and his coming to wipe away the tears of his people (Revelation 21:4). Comfort comes before searching.
  3. Then help them search, gently. Each Comfort page for a sorrow, except If You Are Not Safe, has a word called When you are ready to search your heart. Read the one they have read, so you know what they have heard. The aim is to put sin to death by the Spirit (Romans 8:13), not to guess at a cause God has not shown (John 9:3). Do not break a bruised reed (Matthew 12:20). End every search at the cross, with Christ freely offered.
  4. Give the church its work. First ask them what may be shared, and with whom. Keep the rest in confidence, unless a life is in danger or a child is being hurt. Then bring what they allow to your session, and decide who will visit, who will pray, who will bring meals or keep the children, and who will simply sit with them. Send the deacons to the outward needs. If they fled someone who hurt them, never say where they are staying.
  5. Leave a plan and a date. Give them a psalm for each day and a time to pray morning and evening. Look for them at worship on the Lord’s Day. Before you go, set the day you will come again.

Some searching must wait, and some must never be done. If they have thought of ending their life, or are not safe at home, leave the searching until they are safe and cared for. Do not press long searching on one whose mind has gone dark. Scripture tells such a one to trust in the name of the Lord, and stay upon his God (Isaiah 50:10). Never look for the cause of someone’s violence in the one they hurt. Never treat grief itself as unbelief. Jesus wept.

For the whole church

Carrying the burden is not the elders’ work alone. The Confession lays it on every member.

All saints that are united to Jesus Christ their head, by his Spirit and by faith, have fellowship with him in his graces, sufferings, death, resurrection, and glory: and being united to one another in love, they have communion in each other’s gifts and graces, and are obliged to the performance of such duties, public and private, as do conduce to their mutual good, both in the inward and outward man.

Westminster Confession of Faith 26.1

To conduce to their good is to serve it. Bear ye one another’s burdens, and so fulfil the law of Christ (Galatians 6:2). Paul tells all the brethren to comfort the feebleminded, support the weak (1 Thessalonians 5:14). Feebleminded there means fainthearted, not slow. The Larger Catechism counts comforting and succoring the distressed among the duties of the sixth commandment (Q&A 135). A sufferer left alone is in danger. If they have no church, bring them to yours, and sit with them the first Sunday.

For the longer work, the Puritans are good company. Richard Sibbes wrote The Bruised Reed for the weak, and John Flavel wrote A Token for Mourners for those who grieve.

The church

Do Not Carry It Alone

This page can hand you Scripture. It cannot sit with you, pray over you, or bring you a meal. Christ gave those things to his church.

Call your pastor or an elder today. You do not have to explain it well. Say that something has happened and ask him to pray with you. If the need is money or meals or rides, tell your deacons. That is what the office is for. Peter told the elders, Feed the flock of God which is among you, taking the oversight thereof (1 Peter 5:2). Letting them do it is not a burden you lay on them. It is the work Christ gave them. When an elder comes, ask when he will come again.

Go to worship on the Lord’s Day, even if you only sit and weep. Sit under the Word, and pray with God’s people. If you are a communing member, come to the Lord’s Table. It was given for the relief of weak and doubting Christians too (Larger Catechism Q&A 172). The Word, the sacraments, and prayer are the means by which Christ gives his people the benefits of his redemption (Shorter Catechism Q&A 88), and by them weak faith is strengthened (Westminster Confession 14.1). Yours may be weak this week. Where you can, put off large decisions for a while, and do not make any of them alone.

If you have no church, find a faithful one near you and go this Sunday. Look for one where Christ is preached from the whole Bible, where baptism and the Lord’s Supper are given as he commanded, and where elders watch over the flock. Then ask how to join it. Christ never meant for one of his sheep to walk alone. If you do not know where to start, write to me. I keep this site. I am not a pastor, and I cannot always answer quickly. In an emergency, do not wait on me. Call 911, or call 988 or text 988. But I will pray for you, and I will help you look for a church.

Blessed be God, even the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Father of mercies, and the God of all comfort; Who comforteth us in all our tribulation, that we may be able to comfort them which are in any trouble, by the comfort wherewith we ourselves are comforted of God.

2 Corinthians 1:3-4 (KJV)

One day you may sit with someone else in this same dark. The comfort God gives you now is meant to be passed on.

If you do not know him

Believest Thou This

Maybe you came here because of a death, a diagnosis, a broken home, or a lost job, and you are not a Christian. The promises here belong to those who belong to Christ. They can be yours.

Jesus spoke these words to a grieving woman on the road outside her village, four days after her brother was buried (John 11:17, 20). Then he asked her a question. He asks you the same one.

Jesus said unto her, I am the resurrection, and the life: he that believeth in me, though he were dead, yet shall he live: And whosoever liveth and believeth in me shall never die. Believest thou this?

John 11:25-26 (KJV)

If you came here grieving someone, this is not a word about them. Leave them with God. It is a word about you. Death came into the world through sin, and one day you will stand before God yourself (Romans 5:12, Hebrews 9:27).

But Christ died in the place of sinners and bore the punishment their sins deserved, and on the third day he rose again. Everyone who turns from sin and trusts him alone is forgiven and counted righteous for his sake (Shorter Catechism Q&A 33, 87). He said that whoever comes to him he will in no wise cast out (John 6:37). Call on him tonight, in your own words. Whosoever shall call upon the name of the Lord shall be saved (Romans 10:13). Do not wait until the grief lifts. Come as you are, with the grief.

The gospel page sets it out in full. Read it slowly.
