Discernment report

Philip Anthony Mitchell

Pastor of 2819 Church in Atlanta and founder of the ACCESS prayer gatherings. Many true things, and errors that reach the person of Christ.

Safe to sit under? No Philip Anthony Mitchell is not a safe teacher to sit under. A grave error on the person of Christ, with serious errors besides.

The Short Answer

Philip Anthony Mitchell preaches many true things with real force. He calls sinners to repent. He says plainly that no one can earn acceptance with God. He warns against the prosperity gospel and against sexual sin, and he presses the return of Christ on the conscience of a young generation. Believers should be glad whenever Christ is truly preached.

What then? notwithstanding, every way, whether in pretence, or in truth, Christ is preached; and I therein do rejoice, yea, and will rejoice.

Philippians 1:18 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

Even so, he is not a safe teacher to sit under. His own published sermons teach that the Son of God emptied himself “of some of his divine nature.” He speaks often of God charging him and telling him things directly. He taught his church to make an arena “a portal from earth to heaven” and to “put a demand” on God. He told a congregation that fasting can “move God” and that “your bodies are going to be healed.” On perseverance he hangs final salvation on the believer staying in God’s will, which leaves weak believers without the anchor Scripture gives them. His Lord’s Day services and stadium events are built as produced experiences that lift the preacher, and signs of idolizing him are plain to see.

Verdict. Not a safe teacher. Don’t sit under his teaching or recommend it to other believers.

So the counsel of this report is simple. Don’t make him your teacher, and don’t hand his sermons to new or struggling believers. If a brother or sister already listens to him, don’t mock the man or slander him. Show them his own words, show them where those words depart from Scripture, and point them to sounder shepherds, beginning with the elders of their own church.

How This Report Was Made

The standard used here is Holy Scripture, which alone binds the conscience. The Westminster Confession of Faith and its catechisms are used as the church’s faithful summary of what Scripture teaches.

The evidence comes first from Mitchell’s own words. That means sermon transcripts published from the 2819 Church podcast feed, the church’s statement of beliefs, his personal website, and his long interviews. Reporting from the Associated Press, Christianity Today, Premier Christianity, Charisma, and others was used for the facts of his life and ministry. Critics were read for leads but never treated as proof. A charge that could not be traced to his own words or to a reliable report was left out.

The sermon transcripts are made by machine from the church’s audio, so small slips in spelling appear in them. Where a quotation carries great weight, this report names the sermon and about what minute it comes at, so any believer can listen for himself. That’s the Berean way.

These were more noble than those in Thessalonica, in that they received the word with all readiness of mind, and searched the scriptures daily, whether those things were so.

Acts 17:11 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

Who He Is

Philip Anthony Mitchell was born and raised in Queens, New York, to parents who came from the Caribbean. He grew up in a Christian home, but by his own account he was dealing drugs by age twelve and carrying a gun in his early twenties. He has spoken often of time at Rikers Island, of paying for abortions, and of a dark season around 2003 that brought him near to taking his own life. That crisis turned him back to Christ. He says he sensed a call to preach while reading Proverbs 3:5-6 (opens in a new tab), and that he ran from that call for four years before he gave in.

He is an honors graduate of North Carolina Central University. He also holds a degree in Pastoral and Church Leadership from Valor Christian College in Columbus, Ohio, the school the Pentecostal preacher Rod Parsley founded at World Harvest Church. In one sermon he held up the Bible his college gave him, inscribed “Valedictorian of Valor Christian College, May 15, 2010.” He has been married to his wife Lena since August 2004, and they have two sons and two daughters.

For about ten years he pastored a small Atlanta congregation called Victory Church. Looking back on those years, he has said he wanted to be rich and famous. The congregation was scattered during COVID. In December 2018 he traveled to Israel and, by his own account, had an encounter with God there that changed the direction of his ministry. In January 2023 he relaunched the work as 2819 Church, named for Matthew 28:19 (opens in a new tab), with 183 people.

The growth since then has been very large. The church reports about 6,000 people in person each week and about 75,000 more watching online, and its YouTube channel is near two million subscribers. He preached through Matthew for about three years and has since moved through Acts and into Ephesians. He appears on TBN, co-hosts a touring podcast called Street Preachers with Florida pastor Tim Timberlake, and released his first book, Contend, on October 13, 2026.

The ACCESS Gatherings

ACCESS is billed as “That Powerful Hour of Prayer.” Mitchell says it began more than twelve years ago as a family prayer hour in his parents’ home in Rex, Georgia, while his father was dying of cancer. After his father’s death the family kept the hour going, and it grew into church gatherings. In October 2025 it filled State Farm Arena in Atlanta and an overflow hall, with about 40,000 people reported.

On October 10, 2026, ACCESS came to AT&T Stadium in Arlington, Texas. The church said all 80,000 free tickets were claimed within eight hours. The evening moved through three parts called The Cross, The Warning, and The Cry, and it closed with the prayer “Maranatha! Come, Lord Jesus!” Mitchell says the event cost about three million dollars and that no admission or offering was taken.

Two days later he posted on Facebook, “Just learned that a teenage girl was HEALED of deafness and removed her hearing aids last night at ACCESS in TX,” and added, “This to the glory of Christ.” No name, no family statement, and no medical record has been offered, and no independent report confirms it.

A great crowd gathered to pray and to hear of the cross and the coming of Christ is no small thing, and no Christian should sneer at it. But the way Mitchell taught his church to understand ACCESS raises real problems, which the part on prayer below takes up.

What He Gets Right

Fairness comes first. A man should be judged by the whole of what he teaches, and Mitchell teaches a good deal that is true.

Salvation by grace

He denies salvation by works in plain words. His church’s statement says salvation is a gift of grace and is not earned by works. On The Breakfast Club he said, “You can never be enough to be accepted by God.” In a sermon on Matthew 15 (opens in a new tab), about thirty-eight minutes in, he said of Christ, “He never broke the law,” spoke of him “exchanging his righteousness for your unrighteousness,” and told his people, “you’re not righteous because you have good behavior.” He went on, “we are only righteous if we stand in Christ.” That’s the heart of imputed righteousness, and he preaches it with conviction.

For by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God: not of works, lest any man should boast.

Ephesians 2:8-9 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

Sin, election, and judgment

He affirms election. In October 2026 he told his church, “God in eternity past chose you saved you before the foundation of the world.” He preaches hell as real. He calls sin by its name, and his sermon on Matthew 18 (opens in a new tab) that passed two million views told hearers, “Stop treating sin like a pet. Kill it before it kills you.” Readers of John Owen will hear the echo. In his sermon on the ten virgins he taught that the foolish virgins “represent every man and woman who thinks they are Christians, but they are really not.” That’s a needed word in a land full of false assurance.

Money

He rejects the prosperity gospel by name. Preaching on mountain-moving faith from Matthew 21 (opens in a new tab) he said, “this ain’t prosperity gospel” and “this ain’t name it or claim it,” and he added, “we have no guarantees, yours is to just pray with faith.” He has taught that tithing “is not structured to be bound in the New Testament” and that “we give out of the generosity of our heart.” Preaching on Acts 2 (opens in a new tab) he warned that the early church’s generosity “is not permission for gospel grifting.”

The Holy Spirit

On the Spirit he is more careful than many charismatics. He teaches that Spirit baptism is “that salvific moment when the Holy Spirit unites a person with the Lord Jesus Christ,” which ties it to conversion. He noted that some traditions place it later, but he did not press that view. He also teaches that the tongues of Pentecost were real human languages.

Humility and counsel

He tells his people, “He’s just a man. Come feel God’s presence,” and he has urged them not to “idolize a preacher.” He has told visitors from other cities to go home and “find a Bible teaching church where the word of God is being communicated.” In April 2025 a sermon of his told Black parents to “stop blaming white cops for the killing of Black kids,” and it caused deep grief. He publicly apologized and said his framing “was and is harmful.” Not every preacher with his following is willing to do that.

These strengths are real, and they help explain why so many young people have been stirred by his preaching. They also explain why his errors are dangerous. Error rarely comes to the table alone. It usually comes on the same plate as truth, and the truth makes it easier to swallow.

The Person of Christ

The most serious finding concerns the doctrine of Christ. In “Stay Awake,” a sermon on Matthew 24:36-51 (opens in a new tab) published August 11, 2025, Mitchell took up the question of how Jesus could say he did not know the day of his return. About seventeen minutes in, the published transcript records these words.

His own words. “Christ, when he was on the earth, was fully God and fully human at the same time. … if he’s God, he is all-knowing. … I think the canosis of Philippians 2 (opens in a new tab) solves this question. … he emptied himself, the canosis in Greek, of some of his divine nature, so that he can fully step into human. … The evidence that he emptied himself is that he needed to pray just like you.”

“Stay Awake,” Matthew 24:36-51 (opens in a new tab), about 16 to 18 minutes. “Canosis” is the transcript’s spelling of the Greek word kenosis.

A few minutes later, about twenty-two minutes in, he adds, “I do believe now that he’s seated at the right hand of the father he probably knows the day and the hour.” Taken at face value, these statements describe a temporary loss of divine knowledge rather than distinguishing Christ’s human knowledge from his unchanged deity. That is the teaching being assessed here, not a claim to know his private intention.

This view is called kenotic Christology. It teaches that the eternal Son laid aside some of his divine attributes in order to become man. The text Mitchell leaned on doesn’t say that.

Let this mind be in you, which was also in Christ Jesus: who, being in the form of God, thought it not robbery to be equal with God: but made himself of no reputation, and took upon him the form of a servant, and was made in the likeness of men:

Philippians 2:5-7 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

The words “made himself of no reputation” stand for the Greek word Mitchell reached for, which many newer Bibles put as “emptied himself.” But look at how Paul says it was done. The Son made himself of no reputation, and took upon him the form of a servant. He humbled himself by taking something. He added a true human nature to the divine nature he always had. He veiled the outward blaze of his glory and stooped to the lowest place, all the way to the cross. He didn’t subtract any part of his Godhead, and he couldn’t have. Deity has no parts to give away, and God doesn’t change. If the Son gave up some of his divine nature, then the One who walked in Galilee was something less than God.

For in him dwelleth all the fulness of the Godhead bodily.

Colossians 2:9 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

The Westminster Confession states the church’s settled faith with great care.

The Son of God, the second person in the Trinity, being very and eternal God, of one substance, and equal with the Father, did, when the fulness of time was come, take upon him man’s nature, with all the essential properties and common infirmities thereof, yet without sin; being conceived by the power of the Holy Ghost, in the womb of the Virgin Mary, of her substance. So that two whole, perfect, and distinct natures, the Godhead and the manhood, were inseparably joined together in one person, without conversion, composition, or confusion. Which person is very God, and very man, yet one Christ, the only Mediator between God and man.

Westminster Confession of Faith 8.2 (opens in a new tab)

So how could the Son say he did not know the day? The Reformed answer, with Calvin and the older church, is that Christ spoke according to his human nature. His human mind was a true human mind. It grew and learned, just as Luke tells us.

And Jesus increased in wisdom and stature, and in favour with God and man.

Luke 2:52 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

But of that day and hour knoweth no man, no, not the angels of heaven, but my Father only.

Matthew 24:36 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

As God, the Son knew all things, even while he lay in the manger. As man, he learned and grew. Both are true of one Person, and neither nature was traded away for the other. That’s how Jesus could pray, hunger, and grow weary while he upheld all things by the word of his power. Mitchell is right that Christ’s prayer life shows his true humanity. He is wrong that the Son emptied himself “of some of his divine nature,” and that error sits at the foundation of the faith.

Charity requires us to notice that Mitchell said in the same breath that Christ was fully God, and his church’s written statement confesses the same. The fairest reading is that he is confused, and not that he means to deny the deity of Christ. But a confused account of who Jesus is, preached to a vast audience and left standing, is a grave matter all the same. A man who titled his book Contend ought to be the first to contend here. This research found no public correction of this teaching.

Because the transcript is made by machine, any believer who wants to confirm the wording can listen to “Stay Awake” on the 2819 Church podcast at about the seventeen minute mark. Check the audio against the machine transcript before repeating the quotation. The later line about knowing the day “now” also needs to be heard in that context.

Claims of Direct Words From God

Mitchell often speaks of God charging him and telling him things directly. The clearest example is his account of Israel in December 2018. In a sermon titled “A Clarion Call” he describes a man in Israel who laid hands on him and prayed over him.

His own words. “He says, receive a double anointed [anointing] and lays his hands on me. And prays over me. … God charge you. Go back to your nation. … I’ve been charged by God almighty in the holy land of Israel.”

“A Clarion Call,” about 28 to 29 minutes.

Later in the same sermon he says, “And I can hear God telling me, man, there’s a work for you to do over here, my son.” The pattern runs through other sources too. The Associated Press reported in December 2025 that Mitchell “said God told him to preach without bringing prepared notes onstage.” In a sermon on faith he described “using my mouth in prayer and prophecy to agree with everything God already told me.” Speaking of the worship planned for ACCESS, he said, “I feel that in my spirit.”

To his credit, he also warns against careless talk. In that same Israel sermon he said, “God said, two dangerous words,” and added, “Anytime I hear somebody say, God said, I’ve got a question, an eyebrow raised.” The trouble is that he doesn’t hold his own claims to that warning.

God, who at sundry times and in divers manners spake in time past unto the fathers by the prophets, hath in these last days spoken unto us by his Son, whom he hath appointed heir of all things, by whom also he made the worlds;

Hebrews 1:1-2 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

All scripture is given by inspiration of God, and is profitable for doctrine, for reproof, for correction, for instruction in righteousness: that the man of God may be perfect, throughly furnished unto all good works.

2 Timothy 3:16-17 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

The Westminster Confession says Holy Scripture is most necessary, “those former ways of God’s revealing his will unto his people being now ceased” (WCF 1.1 (opens in a new tab)). It also says that to the whole counsel of God set down in Scripture “nothing at any time is to be added, whether by new revelations of the Spirit, or traditions of men” (WCF 1.6 (opens in a new tab)).

Reformed Christians don’t deny that God guides his people today. He guides by his Word, by his providence, and by wise counsel, and the Spirit opens our eyes to the Scriptures. A man may come home from Israel with a heavy burden for his nation, and God may use that burden greatly. What Scripture does not give us is fresh speech from God that carries his authority. When a preacher says “God told me,” his hearers rightly take him to mean that God has spoken. If God did not speak, then the claim puts words in God’s mouth. If God did speak, then his word binds the hearer just as the Bible does. Either way the claim is far heavier than charismatic preachers usually treat it.

The practical danger is authority. In a 2025 sermon Mitchell told his church, “If you don’t like the vessel, pray for the vessel, especially if God sent the vessel.” A ministry whose founding story rests on a personal charge from God is very hard to correct, because a brother who questions the preacher seems to be questioning the God who sent him. Scripture gives every believer a better rule.

Despise not prophesyings. Prove all things; hold fast that which is good.

1 Thessalonians 5:20-21 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

Prayer, Portals, and a Demand on God

In “Expectations for ACCESS,” published in October 2025, Mitchell prepared his church for the Atlanta gathering. He rightly said that ACCESS “is a gathering of prayer” and that he named it for the access believers have in prayer. Then, about thirty-seven minutes in, he described the event this way.

His own words. “We’re going to make the arena a portal from earth to heaven. … we will put a demand on him to come down.”

“Expectations for ACCESS,” about 36 to 39 minutes.

He also told them to expect “spiritual healing” and “even physical healing,” and he prayed for “signs and wonders and miracles and healing and deliverance.”

Talk of portals and of placing a demand on heaven comes from the charismatic revival culture of open heavens and spiritual atmospheres. It has no place in Christian prayer. Believers already have access to the Father, and they have it in every place, because Christ opened the way by his own blood.

Having therefore, brethren, boldness to enter into the holiest by the blood of Jesus, by a new and living way, which he hath consecrated for us, through the veil, that is to say, his flesh;

Hebrews 10:19-20 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

A stadium is no nearer to heaven than a farmhouse kitchen. Jesus said as much to the woman at the well.

Jesus saith unto her, Woman, believe me, the hour cometh, when ye shall neither in this mountain, nor yet at Jerusalem, worship the Father. … But the hour cometh, and now is, when the true worshippers shall worship the Father in spirit and in truth: for the Father seeketh such to worship him.

John 4:21 (opens in a new tab), 23 (KJV)

Neither prayer, nor any other part of religious worship, is now, under the gospel, either tied unto, or made more acceptable by any place in which it is performed, or towards which it is directed: but God is to be worshipped everywhere, in spirit and truth.

Westminster Confession of Faith 21.6 (opens in a new tab)

This is an excerpt from the original Confession.

No man puts a demand on God. We come as children who ask, and our Father gives according to his will.

But when ye pray, use not vain repetitions, as the heathen do: for they think that they shall be heard for their much speaking. Be not ye therefore like unto them: for your Father knoweth what things ye have need of, before ye ask him.

Matthew 6:7-8 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

And this is the confidence that we have in him, that, if we ask any thing according to his will, he heareth us:

1 John 5:14 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

The irony is painful. The gathering is named ACCESS, and access to God is exactly what Christ purchased for every believer in every room at every hour. In the surrounding transcript Mitchell also urges private prayer and pure motives, and he isn’t teaching occult ritual. Those qualifications matter. They do not establish that an arena becomes a special spiritual tunnel or that gathered believers may demand God’s response. But the words he chose teach a wrong idea of prayer, and the crowd hears the words.

Fasting and Promises God Did Not Make

In January 2025 Mitchell called 2819 Church to a week of fasting in a sermon titled “So We Fasted,” from Ezra 8:21-23 (opens in a new tab). He said, “For the majority of us, 85%, 90%, I’m calling you to what’s called a total fast,” and for himself, “water only.” He did offer a partial fast as another path.

Fasting is biblical, and the Westminster Confession counts “solemn fastings” among the proper parts of worship upon special occasions (WCF 21.5 (opens in a new tab)). A pastor may rightly call his flock to humble themselves before God. The trouble lies in what Mitchell said fasting does.

His own words. “I will weaken you so that who I really am, a spirit inside of a body can grow strong in intimacy with Christ. … When you fast, you hear God more clearly. … When you fast, you can move God to intervene in circumstances. … You’re going to be radically transformed. Your bodies are going to be healed.”

“So We Fasted,” Ezra 8:21-23 (opens in a new tab), about 15 and 48 minutes.

Several problems stand out. God made man body and soul, and the body is his good creation. Scripture itself speaks of the body without the spirit as dead (James 2:26 (opens in a new tab)), so the phrase “a spirit inside of a body” alone does not establish a false account of human nature. The relevant question is whether bodily weakness is being treated as a means that necessarily produces spiritual power.

Fasting also doesn’t “move God” as though our hunger pulls a lever in heaven. God answers prayer freely, out of his own fatherly goodness. Worst of all, Mitchell promised a congregation that their bodies would be healed. God made no such promise. When that promise fails, the weak are left to wonder whether their faith or their fast was too small.

Scripture warns that hard treatment of the body can look like wisdom and still leave sin untouched. Sin is put to death by the Spirit, through faith in Christ.

Which things have indeed a shew of wisdom in will worship, and humility, and neglecting of the body; not in any honour to the satisfying of the flesh.

Colossians 2:23 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

For if ye live after the flesh, ye shall die: but if ye through the Spirit do mortify the deeds of the body, ye shall live.

Romans 8:13 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

Mitchell did say that God “can do anything he desires to do in the context of his will.” That’s true, and it should have governed the whole sermon. A pastor should also use great care before calling most of his church to a seven-day water fast, since any large congregation holds many people with medical reasons that make such a fast unwise.

Signs, Tongues, and Healing Reports

Mitchell is a continuationist, meaning he believes the miraculous gifts continue in the church today. Preaching on Acts 2:42-47 (opens in a new tab) he said, “the Holy Spirit still does signs and wonders through people,” and “We still lay hands and see healing.” In his sermon on Acts 2:1-13 (opens in a new tab) he said the tongues of 1 Corinthians 14 (opens in a new tab) are different from Pentecost, that “Paul tells that this is a gift for prayer,” and that it “requires a gift of interpretation.” In a sermon reported by Charisma he said, “Suddenly, He can heal a physical body.” He has also published healing reports, like the deaf girl at ACCESS, with no evidence beyond the report itself.

He isn’t a classic Pentecostal. He doesn’t teach tongues as the required sign of Spirit baptism, and he ties Spirit baptism to conversion. He has also spoken tenderly of a church member who died, saying, “although she was not physically healed in this life, she is completely healed right now.” That’s the right comfort for grieving saints, and it shows he doesn’t teach that God always heals.

Reformed churches confess that God still heals, and that he answers the prayers of his people for the sick. James 5 (opens in a new tab) calls the elders to pray over the sick, and believers should do so with faith. What the Confession denies is that the revealing gifts and sign gifts that marked the apostolic age go on as an ordinary part of church life. Healing claims announced from a pulpit or on social media, with no names and no proof, also put the honor of Christ at risk. When such reports prove false, unbelievers mock, and believers who were not healed grow confused about God.

A man isn’t a false teacher simply because he is a continuationist. Many godly brothers hold that view. But for a Reformed believer this is a real line, and in Mitchell’s case it feeds the larger problem described under claims of direct words from God.

Assurance and Perseverance

Mitchell affirms election, yet he speaks of the believer’s final salvation as hanging on the believer. In “Blessed in Christ,” preached in October 2026 from Ephesians 1 (opens in a new tab), he said, “the one who adopted you will bring you all the way into the kingdom so long as you stay in his will.” A clip that circulated in early 2025 carries his line, “Too many of you think once saved always saved.” In “Woe to You” he warned that “people who used to be on fire are no longer on fire.”

His burden is partly right. Many Americans presume they are saved because they once prayed a prayer, and Scripture warns them. The foolish virgins thought they were ready. The Reformed faith has never taught that a man may live in open rebellion and rest on a past decision.

But the Reformed faith also doesn’t hang final salvation on the believer’s staying power.

They whom God hath accepted in his Beloved, effectually called and sanctified by his Spirit, can neither totally nor finally fall away from the state of grace; but shall certainly persevere therein to the end, and be eternally saved.

Westminster Confession of Faith 17.1 (opens in a new tab)

And I give unto them eternal life; and they shall never perish, neither shall any man pluck them out of my hand. My Father, which gave them me, is greater than all; and no man is able to pluck them out of my Father’s hand.

John 10:28-29 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

Being confident of this very thing, that he which hath begun a good work in you will perform it until the day of Jesus Christ:

Philippians 1:6 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

The sinner who truly believes will persevere, and he perseveres because God keeps him. Warnings are one of the means God uses to keep him, so a faithful preacher holds the warning and the promise together. Mitchell gives the warning loudly and the promise faintly. A doubting saint who hears “so long as you stay in his will” will look inward at his own record and find no rest there. The Reformed answer turns his eyes to Christ, who loses none of those the Father gave him.

This research found no evidence that Mitchell teaches sinless perfection or that a single sin undoes salvation. His view is best described as unsettled and leaning toward conditional security. Believers who struggle with assurance should be especially careful here, and may find help in Will God Keep Me to the End?

Handling the Text

Mitchell calls himself an expository preacher, and he does preach through whole books of the Bible. That’s a good practice. But at times his application goes beyond what the text says. In “Be a Witness,” preached in April 2026, he said Jesus told the parable of the ten virgins to show that at his return “he would find half of his people ready for his return and another half of his people who was not ready for his return.”

This is a good place to think about what in Scripture is meant literally and what is meant as a figure. A parable is a true lesson told through a story. Its details serve the main point, and they aren’t always meant to be read as hard facts. Five wise and five foolish sets the two kinds side by side. It isn’t a count of how many professing Christians will be ready on the last day. Notice also that both groups slept.

While the bridegroom tarried, they all slumbered and slept.

Matthew 25:5 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

The wise were not wiser because they stayed awake. They were wiser because they had oil. In his earlier sermon on this parable, “A Cry for Readiness,” Mitchell taught it better. He noted that “all of the women fell asleep” and said the foolish “represent every man and woman who thinks they are Christians, but they are really not.” That’s sound. The later line about “half of his people” shows a habit of loose application that careful hearers need to watch for, whether or not a preacher uses written notes.

Money, Tickets, and Answering Critics

In 2026 Mitchell and Tim Timberlake, senior pastor of Celebration Church in Jacksonville, Florida, took their Street Preachers podcast on an eight-city tour. General admission ran about $60 to $70. A $200 VIP ticket added a meet-and-greet with the hosts, a free book, and merchandise discounts. Every stop sold out.

When Christians objected, Mitchell answered from the pulpit. He said the tour was paid for from his own pocket and not by his church, that venues and security cost money, and that “We’re not doing this for a bag.” He also called his critics a “brood of vipers,” the words John the Baptist used for the Pharisees, and he pointed to a child he said was healed at one of the events. On The Breakfast Club he said he takes no offering at Sunday services and that he is “not a grifter.”

This research found no evidence that Mitchell preaches a prosperity gospel, and ACCESS itself was free. Scripture also allows those who preach to be paid for their labor.

Even so hath the Lord ordained that they which preach the gospel should live of the gospel.

1 Corinthians 9:14 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

Yet selling time with a preacher is a different thing from covering the cost of a room. Paul set himself apart from those who made a trade of the Word.

For we are not as many, which corrupt the word of God: but as of sincerity, but as of God, in the sight of God speak we in Christ.

2 Corinthians 2:17 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

Premier Christianity noted that Mitchell’s long rebuttal never addressed the VIP ticket, which drew the strongest objection. Branding fellow believers who raise a concern as a “brood of vipers” also isn’t the gentle answer a shepherd owes. When a celebrated preacher answers criticism by naming his critics Pharisees, he teaches a great many young believers that any question about their preacher is an attack from the devil.

Feed the flock of God which is among you, taking the oversight thereof, not by constraint, but willingly; not for filthy lucre, but of a ready mind; neither as being lords over God’s heritage, but being ensamples to the flock.

1 Peter 5:2-3 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

By comparison, his giving appeals to his own church have been measured. In early 2025 he asked for a special offering toward a five million dollar building over thirty-six months. He used the language of sowing, but he named no amount and promised no harvest, and he said, “if this makes you feel uncomfortable, then don’t do it.” That’s to his credit.

Training, Associations, and Influences

A man’s teachers don’t settle his doctrine, but they do shape the way he thinks. Mitchell’s formal Bible training came from Valor Christian College, founded by Rod Parsley at World Harvest Church in Columbus, Ohio. He graduated as valedictorian in 2010, and in a sermon on honor he said that to throw the Bible his school gave him on the floor would “dishonor Pastor Parsley.” Parsley’s ministry is a classic Pentecostal seed-faith ministry. A current appeal on his website promises “seven powerful blessings of atonement for you when you bring an offering,” including “FINANCIAL overflow” and “A DOUBLE portion from the Lord.” Critics have also tied Parsley to dominionist politics.

Mitchell has not taken up Parsley’s money teaching, and he openly rejects it. Even so, the “double anointing” prayer in Israel, the talk of portals and demands, the fasting promises, and the pulpit healing reports all fit the world in which he was trained. This research found no public statement in which Mitchell distances himself from that school or its founder.

His other ties are mixed. In one sermon he said, “Thank God for Jakes,” speaking of T.D. Jakes, a preacher long tied to Oneness Pentecostalism and prosperity teaching, and he gave no word of caution. He co-hosts with Tim Timberlake, appears on TBN, and is promoted by Charisma magazine. On the other side, he has been welcomed on Josh Howerton’s program, the author Jackie Hill Perry and her husband Preston Perry attend 2819, and the church’s worship team has partnered with Reach Records.

No evidence was found that Mitchell belongs to the New Apostolic Reformation or claims the office of apostle. He is best described as a conservative charismatic evangelical, trained in Pentecostal circles, who has moved toward a sharper doctrine of sin and grace while keeping many charismatic habits of speech and practice.

The Church and Its Government

2819 Church is nondenominational. Its written statement confesses the Trinity, the full deity and full humanity of Christ, the inspiration and inerrancy of Scripture, and salvation by grace through faith. It practices believer’s baptism and a memorial Lord’s Supper with bread and grape juice. Those last two points differ from Presbyterian conviction, though many faithful Baptists share them.

Its government is much less clear. Mitchell refers to “the elders,” and the church names him and his wife Lena as its leaders, but it publishes no list of elders, no account of how leaders are chosen or held accountable, and no plan of church discipline. In a 2025 sermon he told visitors there is no membership requirement at 2819. A church of thousands, with tens of thousands more “Digital Disciples” watching online, can’t be shepherded the way Hebrews 13:17 (opens in a new tab) describes unless the shepherds know the sheep and the sheep belong to a flock. A livestream can’t do that work.

Worship and the Danger of Idolizing a Preacher

The Lord’s Day gathering at 2819 Church is built like a concert. Reporters have described a countdown clock, loud music and percussion, strobe lights, and a smoke machine. Volunteers pray over each seat, greeters dance, and the worship team moves from a fast song into a slow one while the room lifts its hands. People line up before dawn to get in, and some travel hundreds of miles each Sunday. ACCESS carried the same pattern into the arenas, with a produced program and tens of thousands gathered around one preacher.

What God requires in worship

Reformed churches hold what is called the regulative principle of worship. God alone decides how he is to be worshipped, and the church may add nothing he has not commanded.

But the acceptable way of worshipping the true God is instituted by himself, and so limited by his own revealed will, that he may not be worshipped according to the imaginations and devices of men, or the suggestions of Satan, under any visible representation, or any other way not prescribed in the holy Scripture.

Westminster Confession of Faith 21.1 (opens in a new tab)

This is an excerpt from the original Confession.

The Confession also allows circumstances common to human actions to be ordered by Christian prudence under Scripture’s general rules (WCF 1.6 (opens in a new tab)). A clock, microphone or ordinary lighting is not an additional element of worship merely because people made it. The objection concerns religious acts God has not appointed and staging used to manufacture a sense of his presence. This site holds that God appoints the singing of inspired Psalms without instruments, as explained in How God Wants to Be Worshiped. Emotional intensity cannot establish that worship is acceptable. Examine its content and conduct by Scripture.

Wherefore we receiving a kingdom which cannot be moved, let us have grace, whereby we may serve God acceptably with reverence and godly fear: for our God is a consuming fire.

Hebrews 12:28-29 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

Feelings aren’t the enemy. David danced before the Lord, and the Psalms are full of tears and shouts. But holy affection rises out of truth understood. When a room is engineered to stir the feelings first, people learn to measure God’s presence by what they feel, and they go looking for the next service or the next stadium that can make them feel it again.

Signs of a culture built around one man

Concern about idolizing Mitchell has been raised in public and more than once. Reporters note the enthusiastic reaction whenever he takes the stage, and one observer reported that some members have begun dressing like him. VIP tickets that included a meet-and-greet with him sold for $200, and every stop on the Street Preachers tour sold out. In 2025 a woman interrupted a service to call the church to repent of idolizing its pastor, and security removed her. In August 2026 a visiting minister said he was removed after raising the same concern. The matter has come up often enough that Mitchell addressed it on The Breakfast Club, saying “there is a difference between idolization and admiration.”

Mitchell says the right things about it. He has told his church, “He’s just a man. Come feel God’s presence. It’s much better,” and he has urged them to thank God for growth “and not idolize a preacher.” He should be taken at his word that he wants no worship for himself.

But his platform pulls against his warnings. He sells paid time with himself on tour. He builds stadium events around his own voice. He tells his church, “If you don’t like the vessel, pray for the vessel, especially if God sent the vessel.” His whole ministry rests on a story of a personal charge from God in Israel. Every one of those things lifts the man, and none of them is required by his calling. A preacher who means his warnings will take down the platforms that feed the problem. John the Baptist showed the right spirit.

He must increase, but I must decrease.

John 3:30 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

Who then is Paul, and who is Apollos, but ministers by whom ye believed, even as the Lord gave to every man? I have planted, Apollos watered; but God gave the increase. So then neither is he that planteth any thing, neither he that watereth; but God that giveth the increase.

1 Corinthians 3:5-7 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

Why this makes him a particular danger

Movements like Bethel Church in Redding, California, are easy for conservative believers to spot. Mitchell is much harder to spot. He sounds like a sound evangelical. He preaches sin and repentance and grace, and he draws young believers who would never go near Bethel. So his errors reach ears that are otherwise on guard.

His growth has been sudden and enormous. He relaunched with 183 people in January 2023. He now draws about 6,000 a week in person and tens of thousands online, and in the space of a year he filled State Farm Arena and then AT&T Stadium. If the errors documented here continue, they can mislead a large audience that also hears him teach genuine biblical truths. That is reason to examine and correct those teachings without predicting how his future influence will compare with another ministry.

What This Report Does Not Charge

Some charges against Mitchell pass from video to video without proof. Fairness means saying plainly which ones the record does not support, so that no one repeats them.

  1. He is not shown to belong to the New Apostolic Reformation. No evidence was found that he is part of it or claims the office of apostle.
  2. He does not preach a money-for-blessing gospel. He rejects the prosperity gospel by name, teaches that the tithe does not bind the New Testament believer, and promised no harvest when he asked his church to give.
  3. He does not teach that God always heals. He has comforted grieving families with the hope that a saint who died unhealed is “completely healed right now.”

Summary of Findings

The weight given to each finding follows the Westminster Standards. A grave finding touches the foundation of the faith. A serious finding departs from clear Reformed teaching in a way that harms hearers. A caution marks a weakness believers should watch for.

Gospel of grace. Sound
Denies works salvation and preaches Christ’s righteousness counted to the believer.
Person of Christ. Grave
Taught that the Son emptied himself “of some of his divine nature.” No correction found.
Revelation. Serious
Regular claims of direct words and charges from God, outside Scripture.
Prayer. Serious
Called ACCESS “a portal from earth to heaven” and spoke of putting “a demand” on God.
Fasting. Serious
Said fasting can “move God” and promised that bodies would be healed.
Spiritual gifts. Caution
Continuationist, with unverified healing reports made public.
Perseverance. Caution
Final salvation tied to staying in God’s will. Leans conditional.
Interpretation. Caution
Expository in form, loose in some applications.
Money. Caution
No prosperity gospel found. VIP tickets and harsh answers to critics.
Associations. Caution
Trained under Rod Parsley, commends T.D. Jakes. No tie to the New Apostolic Reformation found.
Church order. Caution
The cited sources do not explain the eldership or accountability structure, and the report identifies a sermon rejecting a membership requirement. Lack of a published account does not prove there is no oversight.
Worship. Serious
Instrumental and uninspired praise differ from this site’s Reformed Presbyterian rule of worship. Staging a religious experience needs scrutiny, while ordinary practical circumstances must not be treated as forbidden worship elements.
Personality culture. Serious
Visible signs of idolizing the preacher, fed by paid meet-and-greets and stadium events despite his warnings.
Character. Credit
Willing to apologize publicly. Warns against idolizing him.

Counsel for the Saints

Should Christians listen to Philip Anthony Mitchell? The answer of this report is no, not as a teacher to sit under. A believer may hear a clip and find something true in it, and truth is still truth when it comes from a man with errors. But a teacher is someone we trust to feed us week after week. Mitchell’s errors reach the person of Christ, the sufficiency of Scripture, and the nature of prayer itself, and those are not side issues.

My brethren, be not many masters, knowing that we shall receive the greater condemnation.

James 3:1 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

Where should saints be most careful with him? Test anything he says about who Jesus is against the Creed and the Confession, beginning with the “Stay Awake” sermon. When he says God told him something, ask where God said it in his Word. Language about portals, atmospheres, and demands should send you back to Hebrews 10 (opens in a new tab), where Christ alone is our way to the Father. Promises of healing or breakthrough tied to fasting or to a gathering need a promise from God behind them, and he doesn’t supply one. And when he warns about false assurance, take the warning to heart, but rest your soul on the promise that Christ keeps his own.

How should saints speak of him? With truth and with charity. Quote his own words when naming his errors. Don’t call him a wolf or a deceiver without proof of his motives, and don’t repeat charges the record doesn’t support. And pray for him. He has shown that he can humble himself and correct a public mistake. Christians should pray that he does the same about the person of Christ and the authority of Scripture.

For regular feeding, point believers first to the faithful preaching of their own church, where elders know their names and watch over their souls. For further help, point them to men whose doctrine has stood the test of time, such as R.C. Sproul, Sinclair Ferguson, John MacArthur, Voddie Baucham, and Paul Washer, and behind them to J.C. Ryle, Charles Spurgeon, and the Puritans.

Mitchell named his new book after the letter of Jude. The saints would do well to take that verse seriously.

Beloved, when I gave all diligence to write unto you of the common salvation, it was needful for me to write unto you, and exhort you that ye should earnestly contend for the faith which was once delivered unto the saints.

Jude 3 (KJV)

Contending for the faith begins with the faith about Christ himself. That’s where Mitchell most needs correction, and it’s where the church must stand most firmly.
