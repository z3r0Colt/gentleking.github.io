What the Bible Says About Money and Debt

Protect basic needs, know what you owe, and ask for help before deadlines. Scripture on earning, giving, borrowing, saving, and responsibility toward others.

About 16 minutes to read

If you’re short of money this month, start with the numbers. Write down what is coming in, what must be paid, and when each bill is due. Make sure your household has food, necessary medicine, and a safe place to live. Contact creditors or utility providers before deadlines when you can, and ask your church’s deacons for help if essential needs are at risk.

The rest of this guide explains what Scripture teaches about earning, borrowing, giving, and handling money faithfully. If debt has become unmanageable, go straight to When Debt Has You Under. If you’ve lost your job or home, read Loss of Work or Home. If financial trouble has brought thoughts of suicide, call 988 or text 988 in the United States, and tell someone safe near you. No debt is worth your life.

The Earth Is the Lord’s

First ask whose money it is.

The earth is the Lord’s, and the fulness thereof; the world, and they that dwell therein.

Psalm 24:1 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

God says it again through Haggai, The silver is mine, and the gold is mine (Haggai 2:8 (opens in a new tab)). That takes in the paycheck, the truck, and the change in the jar. So we aren’t owners. We're stewards, managing what belongs to another, and one day we’ll give an account.

Jesus said, He that is faithful in that which is least is faithful also in much (Luke 16:10 (opens in a new tab)). Then he called money the unrighteous mammon, and that which is another man’s (16:11-12). Mammon is money and worldly goods, and it’s lent to us. That’s good news for a family with little. God measures faithfulness by what you do with what’s in the account, not by its size. Forty dollars handled honestly before him isn’t a small thing.

Contentment and the Love of Money

But godliness with contentment is great gain. For we brought nothing into this world, and it is certain we can carry nothing out. And having food and raiment let us be therewith content. But they that will be rich fall into temptation and a snare, and into many foolish and hurtful lusts, which drown men in destruction and perdition. For the love of money is the root of all evil: which while some coveted after, they have erred from the faith, and pierced themselves through with many sorrows.

1 Timothy 6:6-10 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

Paul doesn’t say money is the root of all evil. He warns that loving money leads people into many kinds of evil. They that will be rich means those who want to be. A man can love money with almost none of it. It shows in envy and in the lottery ticket. The snare is a hunter’s trap, and the ruin it pictures is real.

Jesus said, Take heed, and beware of covetousness (Luke 12:15 (opens in a new tab)). Then he told of a rich man who planned bigger barns and said to his soul, take thine ease. But God said unto him, Thou fool, this night thy soul shall be required of thee (Luke 12:19-20 (opens in a new tab)). The parable warns against assuming that stored wealth makes your future secure. So is he that layeth up treasure for himself, and is not rich toward God (12:21).

Paul knew hunger, and he wrote, I have learned, in whatsoever state I am, therewith to be content (Philippians 4:11 (opens in a new tab)). It had to be learned. I can do all things through Christ which strengtheneth me (4:13) was written about that very lesson. Paul spoke of Christ strengthening him to be content in want as well as in plenty.

Let your conversation be without covetousness; and be content with such things as ye have: for he hath said, I will never leave thee, nor forsake thee.

Hebrews 13:5 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

Conversation means your whole way of life. Contentment rests on God’s promise. Paul also charged the rich to trust not in uncertain riches, but in the living God, who giveth us richly all things to enjoy, and to be ready to distribute (1 Timothy 6:17-19 (opens in a new tab)). If you have a warm house and food in the cupboard, don’t be quick to think that charge is only for someone else.

The Prosperity Gospel

Some preachers teach that God wants every believer rich and healthy, and that enough faith, or a large gift to their ministry, will unlock it. Paul warned of men supposing that gain is godliness (1 Timothy 6:5 (opens in a new tab)). That teaching crushes the poor saint by telling him his want proves weak faith. Yet Christ was poor. It isn’t a different emphasis. It’s another gospel. See Where Heresy Shows Up.

Honoring God With Your Substance

Honour the Lord with thy substance, and with the firstfruits of all thine increase:

Proverbs 3:9 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

The firstfruits were brought to God at the beginning of the harvest. Christians should plan to give willingly and in keeping with their means, rather than treating generosity as an afterthought. Giving doesn’t excuse neglecting your dependents or refusing to pay what you owe (1 Timothy 5:8 (opens in a new tab) and Romans 13:7-8 (opens in a new tab)).

The best picture of giving comes from the poor churches of Macedonia, whose deep poverty abounded unto the riches of their liberality (2 Corinthians 8:2 (opens in a new tab)). They first gave their own selves to the Lord (8:5), and the money followed. Then Paul gives the root of all giving.

For ye know the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ, that, though he was rich, yet for your sakes he became poor, that ye through his poverty might be rich.

2 Corinthians 8:9 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

The Son of God left heaven’s glory, was laid in a manger, and gave himself on the cross. We don’t give to earn his love. We give because we have it.

So give regularly and in proportion. Upon the first day of the week let every one of you lay by him in store, as God hath prospered him (1 Corinthians 16:2 (opens in a new tab)). Give gladly, for God loveth a cheerful giver (2 Corinthians 9:7 (opens in a new tab)). And give in trust. He which soweth bountifully shall reap also bountifully (9:6). Sowing is a picture of seed thrown into the dirt in hope. The harvest is real, though not always paid in money, for God is able to make all grace abound toward you (9:8). The widow and her two mites show that the size of a gift alone doesn’t measure its cost to the giver. Jesus said she gave more than all the rich, for she gave of her want (Mark 12:41-44 (opens in a new tab)). Her gift doesn’t authorize leaders to pressure people to give money needed for basic care.

Does the Tithe Still Bind?

Faithful Reformed teachers differ here. Some hold that the tithe, a tenth, binds Christians still. Abraham gave tithes long before Moses (Genesis 14:20 (opens in a new tab)), and Jesus told the Pharisees not to leave it undone (Matthew 23:23 (opens in a new tab)). Others answer that the tithe supported Israel’s Levites and temple, which Christ fulfilled. They add that the New Testament names no percentage.

Plan regular, willing support for the church according to your means (Galatians 6:6 (opens in a new tab)). Some Christians use a tenth as a starting point. Paul says a willing gift is accepted according to what a person has, and he doesn’t intend relief for others to burden the giver (2 Corinthians 8:12-14 (opens in a new tab)). If food, necessary care, or safe housing is at risk, ask your deacons for help and discuss your circumstances with your elders. Giving should not be funded by neglecting dependents or by new debt you can’t repay.

Borrowing and Debt

The rich ruleth over the poor, and the borrower is servant to the lender.

Proverbs 22:7 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

When you owe, part of next month’s paycheck is spent before you earn it. Debt promises freedom and delivers a kind of servitude. Scripture is just as plain about failing to pay. The wicked borroweth, and payeth not again (Psalm 37:21 (opens in a new tab)). Borrowing creates a real obligation. If you can’t meet it, be honest about that and seek a workable arrangement or qualified advice. Inability to pay after illness or job loss isn’t the same as borrowing deceitfully or refusing to pay when you can.

Owe No Man Any Thing

Owe no man any thing, but to love one another: for he that loveth another hath fulfilled the law.

Romans 13:8 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

Some read this as a ban on every loan. Many Reformed teachers read it with the verse before, Render therefore to all their dues (13:7). They take it as a command to pay what is due when it’s due, and not to stay in debt. The same Bible that warns about debt regulates lending. God forbade charging interest to a poor brother (Exodus 22:25 (opens in a new tab)), and Jesus said, from him that would borrow of thee turn not thou away (Matthew 5:42 (opens in a new tab)). He wouldn’t command his people to lend if every loan were sin. Other faithful Christians hold that a believer should avoid borrowing almost entirely, and they deserve respect. These readings both require honesty about what you owe. If you can’t pay, the debt guidance below explains how to seek help. Paul’s continuing command to love your neighbor remains.

Cards, Payday Loans, and Car Notes

  1. Credit cards. A balance can become costly when interest and fees accumulate. Review the terms, avoid adding spending you can’t cover, and ask the issuer about hardship options when needed. Putting a card away doesn’t cancel its balance or automatic charges.
  2. Payday and title loans. The Larger Catechism names usury and extortion as sins (WLC 142 (opens in a new tab)). Calvin and many after him allowed fair interest in trade, but lending that preys on the desperate is what Scripture condemns. Go to your deacons first.
  3. Buy now, pay later. Several small payments at once can quietly eat a paycheck. If you couldn’t buy it today, ask whether you should buy it at all.
  4. Car loans. Choose transport you can sustain. Where a car is necessary, compare the full cost of borrowing, insurance, fuel, and likely repairs. An affordable monthly payment alone doesn’t show what the vehicle will cost.

Cosigning

The old word for cosigning is surety. It means promising to pay another man’s debt if he doesn’t. Be not thou one of them that strike hands, or of them that are sureties for debts. If thou hast nothing to pay, why should he take away thy bed from under thee? (Proverbs 22:26-27 (opens in a new tab)). If you’ve already done it, Solomon says to go humbly and press the man to release you. Give not sleep to thine eyes, nor slumber to thine eyelids (Proverbs 6:3-4 (opens in a new tab)). He pictures a roe and a bird slipping out of a trap (6:5), and the trap is real. When a son or friend needs help, it’s often kinder to give what you can afford to lose than to sign for what you can’t (WLC 141 (opens in a new tab)).

Thou Shalt Not Steal

The eighth commandment reaches much further than shoplifting.

The eighth commandment requireth the lawful procuring and furthering the wealth and outward estate of ourselves and others.

Westminster Shorter Catechism Q&A 74 (opens in a new tab)

The Larger Catechism fills it out (WLC 141-142 (opens in a new tab)). It requires honest dealing, paying each his due, restoring what we wrongly kept, and giving and lending freely as we're able. It requires a lawful calling worked hard, careful provision, and frugality. It forbids theft, fraud, extortion, and usury, and also covetousness, anxious care, envy, idleness, waste, and wasteful gaming (see What the Bible Says About Gambling). It even forbids robbing yourself and your family of the right use and comfort of what God gave. No one keeps that list. It should send us all to Christ.

Working and Saving

God’s first plan for a family’s needs is work. If any provide not for his own, he hath denied the faith (1 Timothy 5:8 (opens in a new tab)). A modest wage faithfully earned is a holy thing. See Diligence, Honesty, and Providing for Your Own.

Proverbs commends diligence and planning rather than hasty spending (Proverbs 6:6-8 (opens in a new tab) and 21:5, 20). A budget helps you see which needs you can meet and where help is required. When income allows, set aside money for repairs or periods without work. Jesus’ parable of the rich fool warns against making wealth your security (Luke 12:16-21 (opens in a new tab)). Saving for foreseeable needs can serve your family without becoming your highest hope.

Paying Taxes

Taxes are part of what is due. For for this cause pay ye tribute also, Paul wrote, for they are God’s ministers (Romans 13:6 (opens in a new tab)). He wrote under Nero. Jesus said, Render therefore unto Caesar the things which are Caesar’s (Matthew 22:21 (opens in a new tab)). That holds when taxes are high and rulers waste them (WCF 23.4 (opens in a new tab)). It means an honest return, cash jobs included. Don’t cheat. See What the Bible Says About Government.

Remembering the Poor

He that hath pity upon the poor lendeth unto the Lord; and that which he hath given will he pay him again.

Proverbs 19:17 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

Here’s a loan Scripture urges on you, and it’s made to God. The apostles asked Paul for one thing, that we should remember the poor (Galatians 2:10 (opens in a new tab)). John asks, whoso hath this world’s good, and seeth his brother have need, and shutteth up his bowels of compassion from him, how dwelleth the love of God in him? (1 John 3:17 (opens in a new tab)). Bowels was the old word for the deep inward feelings.

Christ gave his church an office for this work. When widows were neglected, the church chose seven men of honest report, full of the Holy Ghost and wisdom, to see to it (Acts 6:1-3 (opens in a new tab)). Reformed churches see the deacon’s office there. Give to the deacons’ fund, and tell them when you see a need. If the need is yours, let them help. Saints are bound to relieve each other in outward things, according to their several abilities and necessities (WCF 26.2 (opens in a new tab)).

When Debt Has You Under

If you’ve stopped opening bills because you’re afraid of what they say, begin with the due dates and the notices that threaten housing, utilities, or other necessities. Ask someone trustworthy to help you sort the papers. Debt may have come through illness, a layoff, or your own decisions. You can face the facts without hiding them from your household or your church.

Scripture tells of a widow whose husband had feared the Lord and still left debts. The creditor was coming to take her two sons as bondmen. God filled her empty vessels with oil, and Elisha told her, Go, sell the oil, and pay thy debt, and live thou and thy children of the rest (2 Kings 4:1-7 (opens in a new tab)). That happened. God didn’t shame her. He provided so that she could pay. He may not fill your jars that way, but his ordinary way of helping is through his people.

  1. Tell the truth. If you’re married, tell your spouse all of it, if it’s safe to. Then tell your deacons.
  2. Avoid making it worse. Stop avoidable borrowing and spending. If you need emergency help for food, medicine, or shelter, ask the deacons or local assistance services rather than taking a high-cost loan.
  3. Write it all down. Every debt, its amount, rate, and due date.
  4. Talk to those you owe. Ask whether a hardship arrangement is available and keep a record of any terms. A creditor may decline. Beware anyone who promises to make debt vanish for a fee.
  5. Make a workable payment plan. Find out what is due and whether you can negotiate. Don't promise payments that would leave your family without food, housing, or necessary care. Seek reputable financial or legal advice when needed.

Before agreeing to a collection demand, check whose debt it is and what amount is claimed. In the United States, the CFPB debt-collection guide (opens in a new tab) explains validation notices, disputes, and finding help. Don’t ignore court papers. Get qualified local advice about deadlines and your options.

If you’re considering bankruptcy, get advice from a qualified legal or financial professional so you understand your options and duties. Your elders can counsel you about honesty and responsibility, but they cannot replace the legal advice. A lawful discharge has real legal consequences, so don’t assume every discharged debt remains legally payable.

If some of this is sin, the overspending, the hiding, the envy, confess it plainly. Jesus taught us to pray, forgive us our debts (Matthew 6:12 (opens in a new tab)). Jesus uses debt here as a figure for our guilt before God. Christ paid it, blotting out the handwriting that was against us, nailing it to his cross (Colossians 2:14 (opens in a new tab)). Christ’s pardon calls us to honesty and repentance. It doesn’t promise a financial windfall or a date by which every bill will be paid.

Christ Our Treasure

Lay not up for yourselves treasures upon earth, where moth and rust doth corrupt, and where thieves break through and steal: But lay up for yourselves treasures in heaven, where neither moth nor rust doth corrupt, and where thieves do not break through nor steal: For where your treasure is, there will your heart be also.

Matthew 6:19-21 (opens in a new tab) (KJV)

Moths in the closet and rust on the truck are real, and everything you can hold will wear out or be taken. Treasure in heaven is just as real, and nothing touches it.

The true treasure is Christ himself. He was sold for thirty pieces of silver, and he died under God’s wrath for sinners who loved money more than God. On the third day he rose. Now he offers himself freely. He that hath no money, come ye, buy, and eat (Isaiah 55:1 (opens in a new tab)). If you've loved money, or feared its lack more than you trusted God, turn from that and trust Christ alone. If you don’t know him yet, read the gospel. Whoever has him is rich, whatever the bank says.

Make a Plan With the Money You Have

Gather your pay information, bank balance, bills, and debt notices. Write down what your household needs for the coming month, starting with food, housing, utilities, medicine, and transport needed for work or care.

  1. List every bill. Record the amount, due date, interest rate where relevant, and whether an essential service or your housing is at risk.
  2. Contact the people you owe. Ask about payment arrangements, hardship programs, or a corrected bill. Keep a record of what they say.
  3. Decide what you can pay. Plan honestly for necessities and other lawful obligations. If you cannot cover them, tell your deacons and seek reliable community help early.
  4. Plan your giving. Give freely and responsibly according to your means, without neglecting those entrusted to your care (1 Timothy 5:8 (opens in a new tab) and 2 Corinthians 9:7 (opens in a new tab)).
  5. Build a reserve when possible. Even a small amount set aside for emergencies can prevent new borrowing.

Bring the decisions before the Lord in prayer and seek counsel if you cannot see a lawful way forward. You can begin making the calls today, even before your whole situation is resolved.
